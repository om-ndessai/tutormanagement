# Reward mastery, forgive breaks, audit approvals

The market already splits children's rewards the way the evidence says it should: small, frequent tokens for doing questions, and separate recognition (levels, certificates, badges) for mastering them. What backfires is pressure: prizes a child expects, streaks shown broken, ranks that tell a child they are near the bottom, and feedback that never arrives. Tutoring centres run a token economy by hand (Mathnasium's stamp cards, Sylvan's tokens), schools run one through behaviour-points stores, and practice apps run one through coins and proficiency badges. No tutoring-management product found offers points, badges or a rewards store, and no product found rewards anyone, child or tutor, for checking whether a question is right, so Question Quest has that niche to itself. Its weak point is the one the validation literature is clearest about. Every crowd system that became accurate decided truth by redundancy and known-answer checks, and a randomized trial found that one reviewer misses 13% of what a careful process catches, against 3% for two. Quest lets one tutor's click approve a question for every organization, usually on the strength of one student's answer. Federal law does not forbid points, badges or streaks for children. But Vermont's proposed design-code rules name points, badges and streaks outright, recent state codes converge on quiet hours for notifications, and families should be told, and allowed to refuse, when their children's answers help approve a bank that other organizations use. For Aimtrue, Phase 42 needs three additions: seeded audits and a sampled second review behind the one-tutor approval, a feedback moment when the tutor reviews, and an invisible gate on rapid guesses. Phase 44 should count practice weeks rather than consecutive days, forgive breaks automatically, flatten its streak bonuses and weight badges toward mastery. Phase 45 should favour math-related privileges given promptly and certificates for cleared levels, and drop the points leaderboard. This report agrees with the companion report that challenge standings should replace that leaderboard, but only as personal bests and bands, and only after a cooperative group goal, which is the safer default for children.

## Centres pay for effort, apps gate on mastery, and nobody rewards checking

### Franchises run prize cabinets or ceremonies, and publish no outcomes

The big tutoring franchises fall into two camps. **Mathnasium** runs an in-centre token economy. Instructors stamp a rewards card for each page of math completed "error-free", and full cards are traded at the centre's Rewards Cabinet ([Mathnasium Seal Beach](https://www.mathnasium.com/sealbeach/news/rewarding-students-is-part-of-the-program)). Passing a Mastery Check earns more stamps and a spin on a prize wheel ([Mathnasium El Segundo](https://www.mathnasium.com/math-centers/elsegundo/news/how-mathnasium-el-segundo-helps-motivate-students)). Each centre sets its own prices, and some scale them by level so younger students are not disadvantaged: at Littleton, a first-level card takes **24 stamps, against 10 from level 5 up** ([Mathnasium Littleton](https://www.mathnasium.com/littleton/news/how-mathnasium-littleton-helps-motivate-students)). Prizes run from candy to a **Nintendo Switch at 350 punch cards** ([Mathnasium Spokane South](https://www.mathnasium.com/math-centers/spokanesouth/news/how-mathnasium-spokane-south-helps-motivate-students)). Some centres add cash-like draws, such as a $100 gift-card drawing for a perfect mastery check or school test ([Mathnasium Ahwatukee](https://www.mathnasium.com/math-centers/ahwatukee/news/student-incentive-100-for-100)). **Sylvan** hands out tokens, "often 20 and 30 per hour", for "effort and attitude that is not dependent on correct answers" ([Brainerd Dispatch, sponsored](https://www.brainerddispatch.com/business/sylvan-learning-center-helps-fill-in-gaps)). Its instructional standards require instructors to award and track them ([Workopolis posting](https://www.workopolis.com/jobsearch/viewjob/-BdYrhmr9PUIY42dMK2DJtcGUdcH_pJ0Ob65IMnYjgci3Leg787F7UWx8Mi-3N_U)).

The other camp recognises milestones in public. **Kumon's** Advanced Student Honor Roll runs from Bronze (about at grade level) to Platinum (about three years ahead), with certificates, medals and trophies at centre ceremonies ([Kumon](https://www.kumon.com/resources/what-awards-can-students-achieve-in-the-kumon-program/)). The **Russian School of Mathematics** honours outside contest results; one 2026 ceremony recognised more than 1,500 award winners ([Lexington Observer](https://lexobserver.org/2026/07/16/community-announcement-russian-school-of-mathematics-honors-massachusetts-and-new-hampshire-students/)). The objection to tokens comes from inside the industry: Huntington's founder said in 1992 that "it is typically very difficult to wean a person off a token system of reward" ([Christian Science Monitor](https://www.csmonitor.com/layout/set/amphtml/1992/0824/24121.html)). These sources are franchisee marketing pages, mostly undated and read through search extracts, and no franchise publishes retention or learning results for its reward system.

### Tutoring software stops at reports, while schools built the stores

None of the mainstream tutoring and lesson-business products checked (TutorBird, Teachworks, TutorCruncher, Oases Online, My Music Staff) documents points, badges, streaks or a rewards store. For students, they offer a portal, a practice or study log and tutor-written lesson reports ([TutorBird](https://www.tutorbird.com/?p=5213); [TutorCruncher](https://help.tutorcruncher.com/en/articles/8225249-reports)). Studio software comes closest to recognition parents can see. Jackrabbit Class prints a branded Skills Progress Report "meant to be displayed at home" ([Jackrabbit](https://www.jackrabbitclass.com/?p=34)), and Pike13 shows families their children's skill programs ([Pike13](https://help.pike13.com/knowledge/skill-tracking-for-families)). Wise claims gamification and in-session leaderboards ([Wise](https://www.wise.live)). The fullest system sits outside the category, on the music-lesson platform TeacherZone, whose leaderboard can be set to show a student only their own points, their teacher's students or the whole school ([TeacherZone](https://help.teacherzone.com/knowledge/gamification-overview-points-and-badges)). Centres fill the gap by hand or with school tools. One Mathnasium centre runs a "digital prize cabinet" that students order from at home "with parent permission", and it points parents to LiveSchool to check card totals ([Mathnasium Costa Mesa](https://www.mathnasium.com/math-centers/costamesa/news/new-digital-prize-cabinet-98204726)).

School behaviour systems are the mature market for points and stores, and for the criticism of them. LiveSchool charges **$49-$99 per staff member a year** ([LiveSchool pricing](https://whyliveschool.com/pricing)). It lets schools offer tangible items or privileges such as early dismissal, plus "point goals" reached within a time window ([LiveSchool Help](https://help.whyliveschool.com/en/articles/6313366-running-your-rewards-system)). Vendors disagree on whether families see what a child spent. PBIS Rewards' family app shows the items and privileges bought ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/-/id1035388252)), while ClassDojo keeps redemptions off parent and student reports ([ClassDojo Help](https://help.classdojo.com/hc/en-us/articles/205647205-How-to-Redeem-Class-Points)). The sector is consolidating into curriculum and student-information suites: HMH bought Classcraft in 2023 ([PrivSource](https://www.privsource.com/acquisitions/deal/3AS28W)), and PowerSchool agreed to buy Kickboard in 2021 ([THE Journal](https://thejournal.com/articles/2021/11/12/powerschool-to-acquire-sel-provider-kickboard.aspx)). Researchers argue that public score displays can "humiliate or shame students" and that tracking promotes "compliance and conformity" ([The Conversation](https://theconversation.com/digitally-tracking-student-behaviour-in-the-classroom-encourages-compliance-not-learning-110181)). That critique targets public display and behaviour control. Private points for academic work avoid both.

### Tutor badges reward reliability and revenue, never judgement

Tutor marketplaces gamify their supply side with status badges that raise a tutor's visibility to buyers. Preply's Super Tutor badge is reassessed every month over the previous 90 days, and a single absence report can remove it ([Preply Help](https://help.preply.com/en/articles/16556084-how-absences-and-cancellations-affect-your-ranking-and-visibility)). Outschool publishes its Star Educator criteria, including a rating of at least 4.95 and replies to 65% of messages within 24 hours ([Outschool Support](https://support.outschool.com/en/articles/8475071-your-guide-to-educator-badges-on-outschool)). Tutors complain most about badges that are opaque and vanish without warning ([Koala Bites](https://koalabites-newsletter-624e17.beehiiv.com/p/preply-update-november-2024)). School software gamifies staff too. PBIS Rewards' Teacher Rewards gives "Staff Stars" for routine acts such as logging in or rewarding a student, and staff spend them in a store whose requests an admin approves ([PBIS Rewards](https://www.pbisrewards.com/features/teacher-rewards/)). No platform found rewards tutors or teachers for validating content.

### Practice apps keep three currencies apart and gate the one that matters

K-8 practice products nearly all pay a spendable currency for correct answers or finished lessons, which children spend on avatars and pets. Their learning signals (stars, mastery levels, lesson badges) are tied to proficiency instead, and a lifetime total sits beside both. Beast Academy shows "Available" and "Total" Beast Bucks separately ([Beast Academy Help](https://help.beastacademy.com/a/1798927-beast-builder-and-avatar-overview)), and IXL names a student a "Rockstar" at 10,000 questions ([IXL blog](https://blog.ixl.com/2021/11/12/using-ixl-awards-and-certificates-to-inspire-learning/)). The most deliberate designs refuse to pay for volume that shows no learning:

- Mathletics pays 10 points per correct answer but caps each activity at **300 points a week** "to encourage students to work across a variety of activities" ([Mathletics](https://knowledgebase.mathletics.com/how-do-students-earn-m-coins-in-mathletics)).
- Reflex opens its store only after the day's "Green Light" of accurate answers ([ExploreLearning](https://help.explorelearning.com/en/articles/9000644-reflex-key-terms)).
- Khan Academy brought streaks back in 2024 as a weekly count, renewed only when a skill reaches Proficient. The team considered and rejected renewing streaks by watching a video or attempting an exercise ([Khan Academy blog](https://blog.khanacademy.org/?p=17224)).

The cadence is weekly almost everywhere: Khan's streak, Zearn's goal of three lessons a week ([Zearn](https://webassets.zearn.org/Implementation/SupportingK8MathwithZearnMath.pdf)) and Mathletics' Bronze certificate for 1,000 points in a week ([Mathletics support](https://support.mathletics.com/support/solutions/articles/265818-certificates-and-points)). Leaderboards are class-sized. Matific's StarMaster ranks one class, resets weekly and can be switched off or anonymised by the teacher ([Matific Help](https://help.matific.com/en-us/collections/19744928-students-motivation)). Mathletics ranks classes by average points per student, so a large class gains no advantage ([Mathletics](https://support.mathletics.com/support/solutions/articles/265975-hall-of-fame-and-leaderboard-criteria)). The one mechanic that drew a regulatory complaint was paywalled cosmetics. In 2021, Fairplay and 21 other groups asked the FTC to act against Prodigy, which made desirable items members-only "regardless of how much currency a player has earned" ([Fairplay](https://fairplayforkids.org/feb-19-2021-advocates-to-ftc-prodigy-math-game-preys-on-kids-and-families/)).

Two more market facts place Question Quest precisely. First, every practice product found gives immediate right-or-wrong feedback and help after a miss; Beast Academy, for example, offers a hint after the first wrong try and the solution after the second ([Beast Academy Help](https://help.beastacademy.com/a/1890360-how-to-find-solutions-to-beast-academy-online-problems)). Withheld answers appear only in diagnostics, such as IXL's diagnostic arena, which tells students whether they were right but never shows the correct answer ([IXL](https://www.ixl.com/materials/us/i_guides/Teacher_Plan_Diagnostic)). Second, nobody rewards error reports. Duolingo receives about **200,000 reports a day**, 72% of them "my answer should have been accepted", and only about 15% of those prove valid. It ranks reports with a machine-learned quality score and does not notify or reward the reporter ([Duolingo blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/how-user-reports-improve-course-content)). Quest's "Saved" screen therefore behaves like a diagnostic, not like practice, and its paid tutor review has no precedent.

| Segment | What earns a reward | What it buys or signals | Who sees it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Franchise token economies (Mathnasium, Sylvan) | Pages done, mastery checks, effort | A prize cabinet, from candy to consoles and gift cards | The child and the centre; parents at some centres |
| Franchise recognition (Kumon, RSM) | Working ahead of grade level; outside contest results | Tiered certificates, medals, ceremonies | The public, via ceremonies and local press |
| Tutoring-management software | Nothing found | Lesson and progress reports; Jackrabbit's printable skills report | Parents |
| School behaviour systems (LiveSchool, PBIS Rewards, ClassDojo) | Expected behaviour | Store items and privileges; point goals | Families (purchases shown or hidden, depending on the vendor) |
| K-8 practice apps | Correct answers and lessons; proficiency | Avatar items; stars and badges; certificates; class leaderboards | The child, the teacher, the parent |
| Tutor marketplaces | Reliability, ratings, revenue | Visibility and bookings | Buyers |

## Rewards that inform help children; pressure, ranks and delay backfire

The research averages are positive, but they say little about any single mechanic. The better-controlled meta-analyses put gamification's effect on learning at about **g = .46-.50** ([Huang et al. 2020](https://pure.ecnu.edu.cn/en/publications/the-impact-of-gamification-in-educational-settings-on-student-lea/); [Bai et al. 2020](https://www.gbl.uzh.ch/quartz/references/Bai-et-al.-%282020%29)). The first math-only synthesis finds **g = .383 on motivation from 11 studies**, all in secondary or higher education. Its authors advise against "excessive competition" and in favour of "personal progress and cooperative tasks" ([Ratinho et al. 2026](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-025-10108-1)). A newer synthesis shows the two moderators that matter most for planning, though its spread between studies is extreme. Points, rewards and rankings on their own gave g = .533, against 1.285 when combined with game dynamics and aesthetics. Studies shorter than a week gave g = 1.874, against about .5 for studies lasting a month to a semester ([Li et al. 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1253549/full)). The companion report, [Math challenge platforms market](Math%20challenge%20platforms%20market.md), covers the standard Sailer and Homner figures and Hanus and Fox's semester-long decline. Very few studies in any of these syntheses are primary-school math, so Aimtrue's own measurements will be among the most relevant evidence for 8-to-14-year-olds.

### Points help as small, immediate rewards for effort, gated against guessing

The laboratory evidence carries a warning that applies to children in particular. Across 128 experiments, rewards that people expected for doing a task reduced their later free-choice interest in it, and the effect was larger for children than for college students:

- **Rewards for doing the task:** d = −.43 for children, against −.21 for college students.
- **Tangible rewards overall:** d = −.39 for children, against −.27 for college students.
- **The worst case of any category:** a performance reward where the person got less than the maximum, **d = −.88**.
- **Unexpected rewards:** no harm (d = .01).
- **Interesting versus dull tasks:** tangible rewards undermined interest in interesting tasks (−.68) but not in dull ones.
- **Praise:** it helped college students, but did not measurably raise children's interest (+.11, not significant).

The authors concluded that rewards "must be used extraordinarily carefully with children" ([Deci, Koestner & Ryan 1999](https://depts.washington.edu/techdocs/papers/deciExtrinsicRewardsAndIntrinsicMotivation99.pdf)). The counterpoint is Cameron and Pierce's smaller undermining effect of −.21, which the same paper disputes for pooling interesting and dull tasks. A 40-year meta-analysis, mostly of adults, adds that incentives predict how much people do, while intrinsic motivation predicts how well they do it ([Cerasoli et al. 2014](https://oa.mg/work/10.1037/a0035661)).

Field experiments in US schools refine the warning rather than overturn it. Fryer paid students in three cities. In New York and Chicago, students paid for test scores or grades showed "statistically zero" effects. In Dallas, second graders were paid **$2 per book, but only after passing a comprehension quiz on it**; they gained in reading, and the gains lasted after payments stopped. His reading is that children respond to rewards for inputs because they "do not know the educational production function" ([Fryer 2011](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/sites/default/files/publications/919_Financialincentives_and_student_Fryer_2011.pdf)). Rewards aimed at one subject carry a cost elsewhere: paying Houston students, parents and teachers for math objectives raised math and lowered reading ([Fryer & Holden](https://fryer.scholars.harvard.edu/resource/pdf-multitasking-dec-2013)). Timing is decisive. Across more than 5,700 students near Chicago, an immediate $20 or a trophy costing about $3 raised low-stakes test scores by about 0.1 SD. A trophy gave **+0.29 SD among grades 3-8 at one site**, and the same rewards delivered a month later had no effect: "All motivating power of incentives vanishes when rewards are handed out with a delay" ([Levitt et al. 2016](https://rady.ucsd.edu/_files/faculty-research/sadoff/Levitt_et_al_Behavioralist_Goes_to_School_Nov_2016_AEJ_Policy.pdf)). Rewards close to the activity also beat unrelated tokens: third graders given a book for reading were more motivated to read afterwards than those given a token ([Marinak, UMD](https://drum.lib.umd.edu/items/73a62f89-4b5e-4aa2-b1cc-791638bee992)).

Rewarding effort invites tapping through questions, and the fix is well studied. In middle-school math tutors, "gaming the system" goes with poorer learning, and it depends more on the lesson than on the student, so design can reduce it ([Baker, CMU](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~rsbaker/gaming.html)). Assessment research flags a rapid guess when an answer comes faster than 10% of the item's mean time, usually capped at 10 seconds. This rule, called NT10, is deliberately conservative ([Frontiers in Education 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2023.1127644/pdf)). In a randomized trial with 800 eighth graders, a pop-up asked students who answered implausibly fast whether they had really tried. It cut disengaged responses (odds ratio 1.44 against the control group). An up-front pledge to try made students *more* disengaged than the control group. Scores did not change, and 55% of the students who saw the pop-up said they simply had not known the answer ([Arslan & Finn 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10672233/)). Alerting the adult also works. Across more than 250,000 test events, NWEA's proctor alerts were followed by **+.25 SD more time on math items and +.29 SD higher math performance** in a non-randomized comparison ([NWEA](https://www.nwea.org/uploads/2020/03/researchbrief-the-impact-of-proctor-notification-when-students-disengage-2019.pdf)).

### Streaks lift children who already practise, if they count weeks and forgive breaks

The first field experiment on streaks in education is encouraging and specific. It covered 60,000 Peruvian students in grades 4-6. Messages highlighting **weekly** streaks raised the share of weeks in which users connected by 9.4 percentage points, more than personalised reminders (6.9) or generic ones (2.5). Missing a week did not lead to longer gaps afterwards. Reminders did better at getting non-users to start, and the streak effect grew once a habit had formed. The study is an unreviewed working paper, the final test reached only 2.3% of students, and students saw right-or-wrong feedback after every exercise ([Aulagnon et al. 2026](https://www.ipr.northwestern.edu/documents/working-papers/2026/wp-26-05.pdf)).

Seven experiments with adults show how streaks do harm. Showing someone a broken streak lowers their later engagement compared with showing an intact one. The effect is stronger when people blame themselves for the break, and weaker when the streak can be repaired ([Silverman & Barasch 2023](https://udspace.udel.edu/items/42ce576b-8e1f-429a-8541-e29e48dbcfbb)). Similarly, giving adults a limited "emergency reserve" of slack helped them persist after a miss ([Sharif & Shu](https://anderson-review.ucla.edu/emergency-reserves/)). Khan Academy retired its daily streaks in January 2021. Over 99% of learners did not use them, and streaks "can actually be demotivating, especially when circumstances beyond one's control" break them ([Khan Academy](https://support.khanacademy.org/hc/fr/community/posts/360075847492/comments/360013900252)). It later rebuilt them as weekly streaks renewed by proficiency. The companion report covers Brilliant's two banked "streak charges" and Duolingo's company-reported streak figures. No peer-reviewed study of daily streaks, streak anxiety or streak freezes in children was found.

### Badges raise quantity, and certificates work as recognition rather than bait

Badges reliably raise how much of the targeted activity people do. In a study of 1,031 university students writing and answering multiple-choice questions on PeerWise, badges had "a highly significant positive effect on the quantity of students' contributions, without a corresponding reduction in their quality" ([Solve Education summary of Denny 2013](https://solveeducation.org/wp-content/uploads/Gamification-Report.pdf)). In a randomized Coursera experiment, making badges more visible raised engagement, for example with a pop-up saying how many more actions the next badge needed. Several small badges for one action also beat a single "super-badge" ([Anderson et al. 2014](https://arxiv.org/abs/1403.3100)). The effect is concentrated in a minority: a reanalysis of Stack Overflow data estimates that only about 20% of users respond to badges at all ([Hoernle et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2002.06160)). The type of badge matters for learners. In a middle-school applied-math tutor, different badge types motivated students with different levels of prior knowledge ([Abramovich et al. 2013](https://ri.cmu.edu/?p=127319)). Engineering freshmen on a Khan Academy instance chased the repetitive, easier badges most deliberately ([Ruipérez-Valiente et al.](https://webs.um.es/jruiperez/papers/journals/2017_IJEE_Badges_preprint.pdf)). Volume badges carry a quality cost when the work is reviewing. Across 3.9 million revisions to Stack Overflow answers, users revised far more on days they earned badges, and unusually heavy revising made rollbacks more likely ([IEEE TSE](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/8485395)).

Certificates follow the same rule as prizes. Preschoolers who liked drawing and **expected** a "Good Player" certificate drew less afterwards; those who received one unexpectedly did not ([summary of Lepper, Greene & Nisbett 1973](https://www.psychologynoteshq.com/overjustification-effect/)). That matches Deci's null result for unexpected rewards. Older students say they value quieter recognition. Among 764 students in grades 5-12, teachers' "quiet praise" ranked first, even though rewards slightly edged out praise overall ([Fefer et al.](https://independent.academia.edu/SarahFefer/Papers)). A 2025 pilot found that students preferred private praise and "messages sent home to parents/guardians" ([Contemporary School Psychology](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40688-025-00546-7)). IXL offers the cleanest template on the market. It generates certificates automatically at milestones, collects them for teachers and parents, and sends them in a weekly email ([IXL blog](https://blog.ixl.com/2021/11/12/using-ixl-awards-and-certificates-to-inspire-learning/)).

### Feedback teaches through the explanation, and silence plants wrong answers

The content of feedback matters more than its timing. Across 40 studies of computer-based feedback on single items, an explanation gave an effect size of **.49**, the correct answer alone **.32**, and right-or-wrong alone **.05**. Effects were larger in math and smaller when feedback was delayed ([Van der Kleij et al. 2015](https://acuresearchbank.acu.edu.au/item/86y84/effects-of-feedback-in-a-computer-based-learning-environment-on-students-learning-outcomes-a-meta-analysis)). The evidence on timing is mixed:

- In a small crossover trial of math homework, seventh graders scored 67% with immediate right-or-wrong feedback, against 55% with next-day feedback (effect size .37) ([Kelly & Heffernan](https://education.umd.edu/sites/default/files/uploads/1%20Kim%20Kelly%20and%20Neil%20Heffernan.pdf)).
- A randomized test across 38 college classes found no general advantage for immediate feedback ([ManyClasses 1](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/ampps/25152459211027575/)).
- Delayed feedback helped engineering students transfer what they learned, even though they preferred immediate feedback ([Mullet et al. 2014](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/delaying-feedback-promotes-transfer-of-knowledge-despite-student-/)).

Prior knowledge cuts both ways. Right-or-wrong feedback helped second and third graders who knew less, and hurt those who knew more ([Fyfe & Rittle-Johnson 2016](https://ir.vanderbilt.edu/items/133cb4e3-42ab-4296-a618-23c30ed5040e)). The specific cost of giving no feedback is false knowledge. Multiple-choice testing plants its wrong options as later answers ([Roediger & Marsh 2005](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/the-positive-and-negative-consequences-of-multiple-choice-testing/)). Among second graders, hard multiple-choice questions answered without feedback did more harm than good, and immediate feedback showing the correct answer **eliminated** the harm ([Marsh, Fazio & Goswick 2012](https://scholars.duke.edu/publication/746625)). One message has randomized evidence at scale. Growth-mindset messages, shown above fraction problems to more than 250,000 Khan Academy visitors, raised the rate at which they solved problems ([Yeager et al.](https://aip.brightspotcdn.com/06/c1/781dd466457ca2098c324e659e78/yeager-et-al-r-and-d-agenda-6-10-13.pdf)).

### Ranks hurt the bottom, and every lift fades after about four weeks

The companion report covers the core finding: leaderboards lift those near the top and demotivate those near the bottom. Three findings here sharpen it. Deci's d = −.88 for getting less than the maximum is the laboratory analogue of a low rank. Students list anxiety and jealousy among gamification's downsides ([Bai et al. 2020](https://www.gbl.uzh.ch/quartz/references/Bai-et-al.-%282020%29)). And the math meta-analysis advises limiting competition ([Ratinho et al. 2026](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-025-10108-1)). The strongest case for leaderboards is that, in a randomized study of adults, leaderboards, badges and performance graphs raised people's sense of competence ([Sailer et al. 2017](https://opus.bibliothek.uni-augsburg.de/opus4/frontdoor/index/index/docId/109059)). In the same study, no game element raised people's sense of autonomy.

On durability, a 14-week study of 756 university students found that gamification's effect began falling after four weeks. It stayed low for two to six weeks, then partly recovered between weeks six and ten as students became familiar with the system ([Rodrigues et al. 2022](https://www.doi.org/10.1186/s41239-021-00314-6)). For the adults who teach, the largest causal test is sobering. Among 140,461 teachers with nearly 3 million students on Zearn, researchers tested 15 nudges. The best one was an invitation to log in for an updated weekly report on the teacher's students. It raised lessons completed by 5.06% (3.30% after correcting for picking the best of many), beating swag, raffles and celebrity endorsements. Even so, the effect was at least 30 times smaller than expected ([Wharton Knowledge](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/a-simple-way-to-boost-math-progress-in-elementary-school/)).

| Mechanic | Strongest evidence | What it implies |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A point per attempt | Input rewards worked in the field when gated by a check (Fryer); rewards for simply doing a task undermine children's interest in the lab (Deci) | Keep it small and symbolic, and give it only for answers that show effort |
| A point for being right, arriving days later | Delayed rewards have no motivating power (Levitt); getting less than the maximum is the most undermining case (Deci) | Treat it as feedback, and never show the points a child missed |
| Streaks | Weekly streaks deepened use without discouragement (Peru trial); seeing a broken streak lowered adults' engagement | Count weeks, forgive breaks automatically, never display a broken chain |
| Badges | More quantity without lower quality (PeerWise); ladders of small badges beat one big badge (Coursera trial) | Use ladders, put the first rung early, prefer mastery to volume |
| Certificates and prizes | Expected certificates undermined preschoolers; unexpected rewards were neutral; rewards close to the activity beat tokens | Publish the rule but don't dangle the prize; prefer math-related rewards |
| Feedback | Explanation .49, correct answer .32, right/wrong .05; multiple choice without feedback planted wrong answers in second graders | The tutor's review must give the child the right answer and the reason |
| Leaderboards | Low ranks demotivate; the math meta-analysis advises limiting competition | Put personal progress and cooperative goals first |

## One reviewer is not a validation system

### Accurate crowds came from redundancy and known answers

No successful labelling system let one contributor's word become ground truth. reCAPTCHA recorded a user's answer for an unknown word only if the same user had correctly typed a "control" word whose answer was known. It accepted a spelling only at 2.5 votes: two agreeing humans plus an OCR guess, or three humans. About 68% of words needed two people and the rest needed three or more. The result was **99.1% word accuracy against 83.5% for OCR**; the professionally double-keyed reference itself held 189 errors, against reCAPTCHA's 216 ([von Ahn et al. 2008](https://www.site.uottawa.ca/~stan/csi2911/reCaptcha_Science.pdf)). The ESP Game stored an image label only after independent pairs of players agreed on it, and only from players who had passed test images with known labels ([Wikipedia: ESP game](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ESP_game)). Snapshot Serengeti retired an image after 5 to 25 volunteer classifications. Its combined answers matched the experts on **97.9% of images, slightly better than an individual expert's 96.6%**. Its votes also signal their own reliability: 99.8% of images where volunteers largely agreed were classified correctly, against 71-85% of images where votes were spread out, which the authors send to experts ([Swanson et al. 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4999033/)).

### Peers catch wrong keys only when many of them look

When students write and answer each other's questions, peers catch many wrong keys, but not all of them. In one programming course, 7 of 61 student-written questions (11%) had wrong answers, and other students found every one ([Denny, UBC CWSEI](https://cwsei.ubc.ca/files/outcomes/events/Denny_PeerWise_Talk.pdf)). In two physics courses, students found only 3 of 5 ([Bates, UBC CWSEI](https://cwsei.ubc.ca/files/outcomes/events/Bates_Peerwise_Oct2012.pdf)). Raters also inflate their scores. Across 77,297 peer reviews of 12,803 resources by 2,141 undergraduates, mean and median ratings failed to flag poor resources. Models of each reviewer's reliability did better, and the authors found that "instructor oversight and training for students remain necessary" ([Darvishi et al. 2023](https://research.monash.edu/en/publications/assessing-the-quality-of-student-generated-content-at-scale-a-com/)). A wrong key costs learners more than a point. One instructor reported that students' "main complaint" was errors, with some working "for hours trying to reproduce one of the answers" ([PeerWise community](https://peerwise.cs.auckland.ac.nz/docs/community/using_peerwise_to_assign_homework)). All of this evidence comes from undergraduates; no study of children judging question quality was found.

### Student answers expose a wrong key only after dozens of responses

A multiple-choice item with the wrong key has a recognisable signature in student answers. The keyed option draws fewer students than one of the distractors, especially among strong students, and choosing it correlates negatively with overall score. But ambiguous items and items with two correct answers look the same, so "no one statistical index is completely reliable" ([Stahl & Applegate 2013](https://rasch.org/rmt/rmt271c.htm)). In their simulation, three flags used together caught **85% of wrong keys with no false positives, once an item had at least 20 responses**. Operational testing programmes calibrate items on far more. PISA needs 250 to 750 respondents per item per country ([arXiv 2407.10899](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.10899)). The Duolingo English Test needs about 500 test-takers and about six exposures per item, helped by features of the item's text ([McCarthy et al. 2021](https://aclanthology.org/2021.emnlp-main.67)). Student data also catches what people miss. In a 2025 pilot of AI-written SAT-style math, 800 items fell to 711 after screening. Two teachers then rated each item, and 146 disagreements went to an adjudicator. Even in the resulting 46-item bank, **student data flagged 16 items (35%)** as extremely hard or poorly fitting ([Renaissance Philanthropy](https://www.renaissancephilanthropy.org/insights/the-universal-math-exam-pilot-using-llms-to-generate-math-test-items)).

### Reviewers agree on whether a key is right, and on little else

For K-8 math word problems written by GPT-4 Turbo, teacher reviewers judged 95.8% of solutions accurate but only 84.4% of problems appropriate. They agreed with each other 92-96% of the time on accuracy, against 75-80% on appropriateness ([MATHWELL, Christ et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.15861v4)). Distractors are the weakest part. Math educators judged only 22 of 60 GPT-4 distractors valid, and two of those they passed were in fact invalid ([HEDGE, EDM 2024](https://educationaldatamining.org/EDM2024/proceedings/2024.EDM-posters.113/index.html)). On softer judgements, agreement collapses. Reviewers reached a Fleiss κ of only 0.02-0.31 on realism, authenticity and readability ([arXiv 2604.05160](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.05160v1)). Four judges classifying 920 items as standard or flawed reached κ = 0.3 before discussion ([EDULEARN 2013](https://library.iated.org/view/GUIMARAES2013ASS)). A detailed rubric with trained raters raises agreement to α = 0.84 ([Chem. Educ. Res. Pract. 2019](https://pubs.rsc.org/en/content/articlehtml/2019/rp/c8rp00262b)). Aimtrue's questions reach Quest already `checked`, with a blind solver's verdict shown to the tutor. That automation targets the kind of error reviewers agree on best, whether the key is right. It leaves the kinds they agree on least (wording, distractors, grade fit), and those are exactly where one reviewer's yes-or-no verdict is weakest.

### Rewarded review queues get rubber-stamped, and the fixes are audits and earned rights

Wherever review counts are rewarded, some reviewers go fast and get it wrong. Stack Exchange moderators describe reviewers who let spam through and "have failed multiple audits and have been banned from review on several occasions". One moderator proposed recording an audit failure, after the fact, for everyone who passed a post the community later destroyed as spam ([Meta Stack Exchange](https://backiee.wasmer.app/https_meta_stackexchange_com/a/403670)). The site treats a suspension as a temporary hold on the reviewing privilege, not a penalty ([Stack Overflow help](https://stackoverflowteams.com/help/review-suspensions)). Wikipedia's new-page reviewer right has to be earned (90 days and 500 article edits), and can be removed for a "pattern of failing to exercise sufficient care". In its backlog drives, a redirect counts 0.2 points against 1 for an article, and reviewers earn credit for re-reviewing other participants' work. Its October 2026 snapshot shows 891 active reviewers facing a backlog of 29,535 articles ([Wikipedia NPP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:New_pages_patrol/Reviewers); [Backlog drive, September 2026](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:New_pages_patrol/Backlog_drives/September_2026)). OpenStreetMap's humanitarian mapping adds a second validation stage by experienced mappers, which in most months falls behind the mapping ([OSM wiki](https://wiki.openstreetmap.org/wiki/Tasking_Manager/Validating_data)). Google's Local Guides programme is widely described as gamed by "point chasers", though no one has measured how much ([Search Engine Land](https://searchengineland.com/good-guides-gone-bad-how-googles-local-guides-program-fails-businesses-and-consumers-326724)). The trade-off runs both ways: Stack Overflow cut the votes needed to close a question from five to three because reviewers were scarce ([The Loop, Q2 2021](https://stackoverflow.blog/2021/04/23/the-loop-our-community-public-platform-roadmap-for-q2-2021/)). Published measurements of how much any of these countermeasures reduces careless review are scarce.

### A second reviewer cuts misses from 13% to 3%

The cleanest randomized evidence comparing one reviewer with two comes from a different task, screening research abstracts. One reviewer missed **13%** of relevant studies and two missed **3%**, at the cost of more false alarms: two reviewers correctly passed fewer irrelevant studies (68.7% against 79.2%) ([Gartlehner et al. 2020](https://cris.maastrichtuniversity.nl/en/publications/single-reviewer-abstract-screening-missed-13-percent-of-relevant-/)). Some illustrative arithmetic shows what this could mean for Quest. Suppose 3% of the roughly 18,500 waiting questions carry a flaw that a careful reviewer would catch:

- If one tutor misses 13% of those flaws, Quest would approve about 72 flawed questions platform-wide.
- At a 40% miss rate (Bates' 3 of 5), it would approve about 222.
- With two independent reviewers, it would approve about 17.
- MATHWELL's broader appropriateness failures would scale each figure up about fivefold.

These figures are assumptions, not measurements. The structure behind them is not: a false approval in one organization reaches every organization, while the speed a single reviewer buys benefits only one.

## The law leaves points alone and closes in on streaks, nudges and children's answers

### Federal law punishes deception, not gamification

The amended COPPA Rule has been fully enforceable since 22 April 2026, and it does not ban streaks, push notifications or engagement prompts. The FTC dropped its 2024 proposal that would have stopped operators from using the "support for internal operations" exception to send engagement prompts without parental consent. It kept only a duty to say what the exception is used for ([Jones Day](https://jonesday.com/en/insights/2025/05/ftc-finalizes-amendments-to-coppa--rule); [Mondaq](https://www.mondaq.com/privacy-protection/1579750/ftc-finalizes-amendments-to-coppa-rule)). It also declined to write an edtech exception into the rule ([Federal Register](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2025-04-22/html/2025-05904.htm)). The older rule text still forbids making a child's participation in "a game, the offering of a prize, or another activity" depend on more personal information than necessary ([FTC, Edmodo filing](https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/ftc_gov/pdf/2023129edmodojointmotionorder.pdf)).

Enforcement has targeted deception and purchases:

- Epic's order followed children buying in-game currency "by simply pressing buttons" ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2023/03/ftc-finalizes-order-requiring-fortnite-maker-epic-games-pay-245-million-tricking-users-making)).
- The $20 million Genshin Impact order covered loot-box odds and multi-tier virtual currency. Commissioner Ferguson, now FTC Chair, dissented on the currency counts ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/01/genshin-impact-game-developer-will-be-banned-selling-lootboxes-teens-under-16-without-parental); [Ferguson concurrence](https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/ftc_gov/pdf/ferguson-cognosphere-concurrence.pdf)).
- The pending Sendit complaint alleges the company wrote fake "friend" messages to push a paid membership ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/09/ftc-alleges-sendit-app-its-ceo-unlawfully-collected-personal-data-children-deceived-users-about)).

No FTC case found targets points, badges or streaks as such. The design "duty of care" many expected has not arrived. KOSA cleared Senate Commerce on 5 August 2026 but was blocked on the Senate floor, and the House passed a different bill, the KIDS Act, without that duty ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/08/05/kosa-privacy-social-media-senate.html); [K-12 Dive](https://www.k12dive.com/news/house-passes-kids-internet-and-digital-safety-act/824128/)). The companion report covers COPPA's consent and contest rules in detail.

### Vermont proposes to ban streaks by name, and other states converge on quiet hours

Vermont's Age-Appropriate Design Code takes effect on 1 January 2027, and the Attorney General's proposed rules would bar three things. The first is "variable or intermittent rewards (e.g., likes, badges, points)". The second is "social pressure features", including "streaks" and "popularity indicators". The third is notifications designed to "encourage, increase, prolong, or reinitiate engagement". The summary describes no revenue threshold, and coverage applies when minors make up 2% or more of a service's audience. Final rules are due by 1 January 2027 ([Mayer Brown](https://www.mayerbrown.com/en/insights/publications/2026/09/vermonts-proposed-aadc-rules-would-force-a-redesign-of-how-online-services-present-content-to-minors-reaching-far-beyond-social-media-companies)). Nebraska's code lists "rewards or incentives for frequency of visits or time spent" and notifications as covered design features. It bans notifications to minors from 10pm to 6am, and from 8am to 4pm on school-year weekdays. But it reaches only businesses that pass all three of its tests: $25 million in revenue, data on 50,000 consumers, and at least half of revenue from selling data ([Nebraska LB 504](https://www.nebraskalegislature.gov/FloorDocs/109/PDF/Slip/LB504.pdf)). New York's SAFE for Kids Act and California's SB 976 reach only platforms built around an "addictive feed" ([Covington](https://www.insideprivacy.com/childrens-privacy/new-york-publishes-final-safe-for-kids-act-rules/)).

The app-store laws reach any mobile app, whatever the business's size:

- Texas's law has been enforceable since an appeals court stayed the injunction against it on 4 June 2026 ([CCIA](https://ccianet.org/litigation/ccia-v-paxton/)).
- California's AB 1043 age-signal duties start on 1 January 2027 ([Kelley Drye](https://www.kelleydrye.com/viewpoints/blogs/ad-law-access/faq-digital-age-assurance-act-california-youth-safety-on-the-internet)).
- Utah and Louisiana follow in 2027 ([Alston & Bird](https://www.alstonprivacy.com/louisiana-delays-app-store-accountability-effective-date-to-july-2027/)).

No North Carolina design code or app-store act was found, though the state's 2025-26 legislative sessions were not searched directly. A fair reading of Vermont's draft is narrow. Fixed, announced points for a stated action look more like a grade than a slot machine, but streaks are named outright, which makes them Phase 44's riskiest mechanic if Aimtrue ever serves Vermont children.

| Pattern | Named by | Status, October 2026 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Streaks, especially loss-framed or on by default | Vermont proposed rules; EU DSA guidelines (off by default); UK consultation | Proposed rules; non-binding guidance; consultation |
| Rewards for visit frequency or time spent | Nebraska LB 504 | In force, for large data businesses only |
| Variable or random rewards | Vermont proposed rules; FTC Genshin Impact order; UK ICO | Proposed rules; consent order; guidance |
| Notifications at night or in school hours | Nebraska (10pm-6am, plus 8am-4pm on school-year weekdays); Vermont statute (midnight-6am); New York SAFE for Kids | In force; from 2027; from 2027 |
| Public rankings of children | Vermont ("popularity indicators"); Nebraska (others viewing a minor's data); FERPA practice | Proposed rules; in force; school customers only |
| Fake social pressure in messages | FTC Sendit complaint | In litigation |

### Using children's answers to approve a shared bank is a disclosure question

The student-privacy laws most likely do not reach a platform sold to private tutoring organizations. North Carolina's SOPIPA-style law binds only an operator whose service is "designed and marketed for K-12 school purposes" and used mainly for purposes "directed by" a school ([N.C.G.S. § 115C-401.2](https://www.ncleg.gov/EnactedLegislation/Statutes/HTML/BySection/Chapter_115C/GS_115C-401.2.html)). FERPA binds only institutions that receive Department of Education funds ([34 CFR 99.1](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/34/99.1)). Their pattern is still the one to follow, and it becomes binding the day Aimtrue sells to a school. The North Carolina law expressly allows using information "not associated with an identified student" to "improve educational products". University FERPA practice treats any display of grades to other students in a class as a disclosure ([Washington State Univ.](https://ferpa.em.wsu.edu/faqs/staff-faq)). COPPA applies to under-13s regardless. The FTC's commentary on the amended rule says that disclosing children's data "to train or otherwise develop" AI is not integral to a service, so it needs separate, verifiable parental consent ([Data Protection Report](https://www.dataprotectionreport.com/2025/06/ftcs-coppa-rule-changes-include-ai-training-consent-requirement/)). The same rule bans keeping children's data indefinitely and requires a written retention policy. That applies to an append-only points ledger as much as to any other table.

Children's Quest answers feed decisions that change a bank other organizations use, which is closer to labelling data than to ordinary practice. No case or regulator has addressed that use directly, so this analysis is inference, not legal advice.

### App stores and foreign codes point the same way

Apple requires explicit in-app opt-in, and an in-app way to opt out, for promotional push notifications. It allows apps to "incentivize users to take specific actions within apps (such as by completing a level)" ([Apple App Store Review Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)). Its Kids Category bars links out of the app and "other distractions" across the whole app, which makes it a poor fit for one app that also serves admins, tutors and parents. Google Play's Families policy bars "emotionally manipulative tactics to encourage... in-app purchases" ([Google Play](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/9893335)). For a future UK or EU launch, the UK ICO's Children's Code names "reward loops… notifications and auto-play" as features designed to extend engagement, and asks for pause buttons that keep a child's progress ([ICO Standard 5](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/childrens-information/childrens-code-guidance-and-resources/age-appropriate-design-a-code-of-practice-for-online-services/5-detrimental-use-of-data/)). The EU's guidelines under the Digital Services Act (DSA) want streaks and notifications during sleep hours off by default, although micro and small enterprises are exempt ([Hogan Lovells](https://hlc.com/en/publications/the-longawaited-eu-guidelines-on-article-281-dsa-what-online-platforms-must-know)). A UK consultation in January 2026 also named "streaks" among addictive features to restrict ([Hunton](https://hunton.com/privacy-and-information-security-law/uk-government-launches-consultation-on-under-16s-social-media-ban)). School buyers have lost their best-known checklist: the Student Privacy Pledge was retired on 25 April 2025 ([FPF](https://fpf.org/student-privacy-pledge/)). No procurement rubric that scores gamification was found. A short published statement of how Quest motivates children without pressure would answer the questions careful organizations and parents are starting to ask, at almost no cost.

## What it means for Question Quest

### Phase 42 has the right instincts and three gaps

Much of Phase 42 already matches the evidence:

- **Points are fixed, announced and never decrease.** That avoids the mechanic behind IXL's angriest reviews, a score that drops after a miss, which reviewers say left children crying as their SmartScore fell ([Trustpilot: IXL](https://uk.trustpilot.com/review/www.ixl.co.uk?page=3)). It also keeps the points clear of Vermont's ban on "variable or intermittent" rewards.
- **Points are private.** Only the student, their guardians, their current tutors and the admins see them, which avoids the public displays at the centre of the ClassDojo critique.
- **Tutors are paid for reviewing, not for a verdict.** A tutor earns the same point for approving or flagging, which follows PBIS's "Staff Stars" model rather than Preply's revenue badges.
- **Reviewers can abstain.** The "Not sure" button plays the role of the confidence rating RiPPLE asks its reviewers for.
- **Answers are kept as their own records, not as audit-log lines.** The organization's flag is off by default.
- **The focused screen suits practice.** The full-screen, one-question view is the kind of focused practice critics prefer to Prodigy's game world, where Common Sense Media found "long stretches" between questions ([Common Sense Media](https://www.commonsensemedia.org/app-reviews/prodigy-kids-math-game)).
- **An adult sets the starting level**, as Beast Academy parents and Zearn teachers do.

**The first gap is validation.** The owner decided that one tutor's "Question is right" approves a question for the whole platform. Each approval is recorded as a quest approval, so a platform admin can find and retire a bad one. The case for this is strong: about 18,500 questions are waiting; they are already `checked` and solved blind; tutors are competent adults; and Stack Overflow itself gave up some redundancy to keep its queues moving. But each approval usually rests on one student's answer, because a question waiting for review is not served to a second student in the same organization. That is far below the 20 responses at which answer statistics start to work. Nothing in the plan checks the reviewer, either. Approving takes one tap while flagging requires a reason, so the cheapest way to earn a point is to approve. That is an inference about effort; no study has measured it.

Three additions keep Decision 1 and recover most of what redundancy provides:

- **Seed the review queue with test items.** Mix in questions whose verdict is already known, both approved questions and deliberately broken ones, as reCAPTCHA did with its control words. Suspend a tutor's reviewing privilege, but not points already earned, after several failures, as Stack Exchange does.
- **Send a sample to a second reviewer.** Route a random share of quest approvals to the console or to a tutor in another organization, showing only the question, its key and its solution, never the student's name or answer. Also route any approved question whose key later draws fewer students than a distractor, once organizations' tests have produced enough answers. This is Snapshot Serengeti's rule of sending uncertain items to experts.
- **Let a review's point stand only while its verdict stands.** The same applies to the Phase 44 Helper badge. This is the after-the-fact audit that Stack Exchange's moderators proposed.

A list of reasons the tutor can tap, instead of a typed reason, would also make flagging as quick as approving.

**The second gap is feedback.** "Saved" exists for a good reason: a key nobody has reviewed could be wrong, and showing a wrong key as correct teaches a wrong answer. Withholding right-or-wrong at answer time costs little learning directly (effect size .05), may even suit stronger students, and has a precedent in diagnostic tests. Its real cost is that hard multiple-choice practice without feedback plants wrong answers in children's memory, and showing the correct answer afterwards removes that harm. The fix is to make the tutor's review the feedback moment. Once a question is approved, show the student the question again with the right answer and the worked solution. Those are the forms of feedback with effect sizes of .32-.49; a bare "+1" is not. After a miss, show the right answer and bring back a similar question later. This also gives Phase 44 its best notification.

Two things support this:

- **A review-time target of a day or two**, with a line such as "4 answers waiting for your tutor", so the student knows feedback is coming.
- **Truthful wording on the Saved screen**, such as "You're helping check new questions; your tutor will show you how you did", plus the growth-mindset message that the Khan Academy experiment supports.

Showing the key after one tutor's approval raises the cost of a false approval, which strengthens the case for the audits above.

**The third gap is guessing.** A point per answer is a reward for input with no quality check. The planned `quest_attempts` columns include the time an answer was submitted but not the time the question was shown, so the platform cannot yet tell a 2-second tap from a 2-minute solution. Four changes close this gap:

- Record when each question is shown.
- Quietly withhold the attempt point from answers faster than an NT10-style floor: 10% of the question's median time, capped at 10 seconds, with a fixed 3-5 second floor until a question has enough answers.
- Before saving a very fast answer, ask "That was quick: want another look?". Never ask for an up-front pledge, which made students more disengaged.
- Mark runs of rapid guesses in the tutor's queue, so the reviewer knows a wrong answer there is no evidence against the key.

Two smaller changes complete Phase 42. First, Quest moves a student up a level when every question in it has been answered, whereas every competitor moves students up on proficiency. A "level complete" moment should wait until a set share of the level's answers is confirmed right, which also gives Phase 45 its natural certificate. The flow should also move on only when the student taps "Next question", never by itself, and offer an obvious "Stop for today" that keeps progress, as the ICO code and Vermont's ban on autoplay both expect. Second, the data rules should be written down before Phase 44 adds more data:

- Keep `quest_attempts` inside the student's organization.
- Let only the tutor's verdict travel with an approved question.
- Tell guardians in plain words that their child's answers help tutors check questions that join a bank every organization uses.
- Give guardians a way to take their child out of Quest.
- Put `quest_attempts` and `points_ledger` on the written retention schedule, with deletion on request. An append-only ledger must still allow a child's rows to be deleted.

The companion report's case for building parental consent before more gamification applies here with full force.

### Phase 44 should count weeks, forgive breaks and reward mastery

The planned streak counts consecutive days with an answer or review, with bonuses of +2 points at 3 days, +5 at 7 and +10 at 30. It is the part of Phase 44 that both the evidence and the law push against. A tutoring week means one or two lessons. A sick day, or a weekend without the iPad, breaks a daily streak for reasons outside the child's control, which is why Khan Academy dropped daily streaks. The only randomized trial with children counted weeks and found that breaks did no harm, and Vermont's draft names streaks as a social-pressure feature.

The streak should work like this instead:

- Count practice weeks, where a week counts after, say, two days of practice.
- Give one or two banked weeks of grace, applied free and automatically.
- Never show a broken streak. After a break, show the best streak and total practice, and quietly start a new one.
- Word every streak message as a gain, never as a loss.
- Let students and guardians hide streaks. For children under 13, an organization could reasonably start with streaks off.

The bonuses are expected rewards for simply showing up, which is the category that undermines children's motivation most in laboratory studies. The escalating +10 at 30 also makes a break on day 29 the most painful moment in the system. The bonuses should be flattened to one small fixed amount, or replaced by a badge at each milestone, and the 7-day and 30-day streak badges should become week-based.

The planned badge set is mostly right:

- The 10/50/100/500 ladder of confirmed answers matches the evidence that several small badges beat one big one, and that badges should count confirmed answers rather than raw ones.
- "Level cleared" and "First approval" are the mastery badges that the middle-school evidence favours.
- The first rung should be reachable in a child's first session or two, and "My progress" should show the distance to the next one ("7 of 10 confirmed").

On the tutor side, "Sharp eye", for a flag the platform admin upholds, rewards the scarce behaviour of catching a flaw, and is the best counterweight to rubber-stamping the plan has. "Helper", for 100 reviews, rewards volume in exactly the setting where badge days produced rollbacks on Stack Overflow. It should count only reviews that pass audits and whose verdicts stand. Every badge should have published, fixed criteria. That answers tutors' main complaint about marketplace badges, and keeps badges out of Vermont's "variable" rewards.

"My progress" should lead with competence: levels cleared, standards where answers were confirmed right, and a trend line. Points alone are the weakest form of gamification, and feedback on competence is what survives the fading of novelty.

The home-screen nudge ("2-day streak: one question keeps it going") should appear only to a child with an active streak. Streak messages deepened use among children who were already engaged, while plain reminders worked better for those who had lapsed. The nudge must be truthful, with no invented classmates (the theory behind the FTC's Sendit case). It should appear in the app during the student's own session, and never be used to pull back a child who has gone quiet.

When push notifications arrive, student reminders need:

- the guardian's opt-in for children under 13, plus Apple's opt-in wording and an in-app switch;
- quiet hours on the organization's clock, from 10pm to 6am and from 8am to 4pm on school-year weekdays, the widest window found;
- a limit of one a day.

The companion report's advice to send streak nudges to parents still holds. For tutors, the Zearn megastudy suggests that a weekly note such as "Your students answered 42 questions; 9 await your review" works better than any tutor badge.

Two smaller points round out Phase 44. A "quiet mode", showing progress without confetti, protects children who already like math, whose interest rewards undermine most. And Phase 44 should be judged on 10-12 weeks of data against a baseline, not on its launch week, with later badge rungs held back for the dip that typically comes between weeks four and eight.

### Phase 45 should ship privileges and certificates, not a points leaderboard

**Rewards shelf.** Good precedents exist, and the evidence adds one design rule: rewards should be close to the activity and given promptly. Mathnasium shows a per-centre catalogue priced in the centre's own currency, with prices scaled by level, though it publishes no results. School stores show that privileges can sit beside goods. The plan's own examples, "choose the next lesson's game" and "5 minutes of puzzle time", are rewards close to the activity, the kind that beat tokens for third-grade readers. The shelf should favour those, plus harder challenge sets, a chosen next topic and certificates, over stickers and toys. It should:

- keep the plan's ban on money, gift cards and anything cash-like, which keeps Quest clear of Mathnasium-style drawings, PBIS-style gift cards and the rules on virtual currency;
- have rewards marked as given at the next lesson, because a reward delayed by a month motivated nothing in the trials;
- show guardians what was redeemed, as PBIS does rather than ClassDojo, which suits Nebraska's parental tools and the transparency rules;
- keep the spendable balance (points earned minus points redeemed) separate from the lifetime total shown on certificates, as Beast Academy does, so spending never shrinks a child's record;
- send any physical reward to the guardian's address, never the child's;
- report Quest activity alongside the tutor's lesson plan, not instead of it, since rewards aimed at math alone pulled effort away from reading in Houston.

**Certificates.** Certificates should mark mastery. They should follow IXL's pattern, generated automatically, printable at home and announced to guardians in a short note, and name a tier and a date, as Kumon does, rather than print a raw point total. The evidence on expected rewards says to publish the rule but not to dangle the prize: no countdowns such as "answer 50 more to win a certificate". The certificate should arrive as recognition of a level cleared and confirmed. The plan's offline recognition of tutors is consistent with PBIS's admin-approved staff store, as long as its criteria are published and stable.

**Leaderboard.** The companion report proposed replacing Phase 45's opt-in points leaderboard with challenge standings. These are the results of a Club Challenge in which everyone answers the same set of questions, shown as personal bests, fixed-threshold awards and percentile bands, with a first-name table only if an organization turns one on ([Math challenge platforms market](Math%20challenge%20platforms%20market.md)). This report's evidence supports dropping the points leaderboard, and adds three reasons specific to Quest:

- **Quest points mostly measure volume**, since a point comes with every answer.
- **Children answer different questions.** Quest deliberately gives each question to about one student per organization, and each student works at their own level.
- **Confirmed points lag tutor review by days**, so a weekly board would partly rank children by how quickly their tutor works.

A board of Quest points would be the weakest leaderboard possible. But the evidence also limits what replaces it. Challenge standings still rank children. Deci's d = −.88 still describes the child near the bottom, the math meta-analysis still advises limiting competition, and Vermont's draft names "popularity indicators". The companion report's safeguards (bands, personal bests and fixed thresholds, never "27th of 30") are therefore the essential part of its proposal, and should be tightened further:

- a named table needs both the organization's opt-in and each guardian's approval;
- it shows a child only the few names near their own position;
- it resets weekly;
- it never shows the bottom of the table.

There is also a sequencing reason not to tie Phase 45 to challenge standings. A Club Challenge needs enough approved questions at each level that no invited student has seen, and Quest is what produces them. Phase 45 should therefore ship the rewards shelf and certificates with a cooperative goal in place of a leaderboard, for example "Our group checked 200 questions this month". The goal should count reviews and confirmed answers, never approvals, so it cannot reward rubber-stamping. Any standings belong in the challenge phase.

## Conclusion

Question Quest is two games on one screen, and the evidence pulls them in opposite directions. The child's game is learning, and every finding says to slow it down and close the loop: check the effort point, prompt the too-fast tap, and make the tutor's review give back the right answer and the reason. The tutor's game is validation, and every finding says it cannot run on trust in one click. The systems that made crowds accurate tested their reviewers with known answers and sought a second opinion where the signal was weak. The most valuable reward Quest can give sits where the two games meet, and no competitor offers it: "A question you answered has been approved; here is why it's right." That one message is feedback, a reason to come back, the kind of competence signal that outlasts novelty, and a reward that informs rather than pressures.

The direction of regulation reinforces the evidence rather than working against it. The mechanics Vermont's draft names (variable rewards, streaks, popularity indicators, notifications that pull users back) are the same ones the research shows backfiring with children. Fixed, announced, private recognition of mastery is both the safest choice under the strictest text found and the best supported by the studies. That makes compliance a design brief rather than a cost, and it suggests an order of work. Put the audits and the feedback moment into Phase 42 before Phase 44's badges and Phase 45's shelf multiply the number of reviews, because rewards scale whatever the review queue already does, careful or careless.
