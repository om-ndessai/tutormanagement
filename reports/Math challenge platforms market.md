# Turn Question Quest into fair math contests

Math "challenges and clashes" have an empty middle. Classroom quiz games (Kahoot!, Wayground, Blooket, Gimkit) give everyone the same set but score speed or luck, know children only by nickname and never leave the classroom; adaptive practice contests (Sumdog, World Maths Day, 99math) cross schools but give each child different questions; formal contests (MATHCOUNTS, the AMC, Math Kangaroo) run a fixed, scored paper but only once a year, on paper or proctored, at $18-$53 a student; and the rated arenas built for enthusiasts (AoPS's For The Win!, MathDash) are small. **No product runs a year-round, same-paper, accuracy-scored competition between identified students of different tutoring centers**, and Aimtrue already holds its three hard parts: a standards-tagged bank whose keys are checked by code and marked exactly by `checkAnswer`, enrolled students with guardians on file, and organizations that map onto the coach-and-team model student tournaments already use. The evidence says to score correct answers first and use time only to break ties (Kahoot! itself added an Accuracy mode in 2025), to keep every unsupervised event unrated (frontier AI now solves AIME-level problems, and leaked AMC papers sold for $200-$400 in 2025), and to prefer near-peer groups, teams and personal bests to public ranks, which demotivate the children at the bottom. The recommended path runs in five phases: an in-organization Club Challenge, a tutor-hosted Live Clash on Durable Objects, Open Challenges across organizations once students without email can sign in (F1) and parental consent exists (F7), ratings and leagues, and finally supervised tournaments with live Countdown finals. Three constraints bind before any of it scales: only about a dozen questions are approved by a person, most children cannot sign in, and D1's metered reads punish real-time polling. Commercially, play inside an organization is a retention feature that belongs in the subscription (a retained student is worth $3,000-$4,800 a year to a center charging $250-$400 a month), while cross-center events can carry a per-entry fee well below Math Kangaroo's $18-$20.

## Five product families surround an empty middle

**Classroom quiz shows** set the expectations teachers bring. Kahoot!'s Classic mode gives more points the faster a correct answer arrives; players per game run from 40 on the free plan to 800 on Kahoot! One, and teachers pay **$36-$228 a year** ([Kahoot! plans](https://kahoot.com/schools/plans/)). After years of teacher requests, Kahoot! shipped an **Accuracy mode in May 2025** — the same time for everyone, one point per correct answer, an optional unlimited timer for accessibility ([Kahoot! blog](https://kahoot.com/blog/2025/05/06/teacher-takeover-accuracy-mode/)) — describing it as "a way to slow down the pace and focus on what matters most—accuracy" ([Kahoot! blog, Sept 2025](https://kahoot.com/blog/2025/09/04/back-to-school-season-with-kahoot/)). Math is a weak spot across the category: Kahoot! offers a symbol palette rather than LaTeX ([Kahoot! blog, 2020](https://kahoot.com/blog/2020/10/13/math-symbol-editor-kahoot/)), and one teacher reports that typed answers accept only letters and numerals ([Kahoot! community](https://support.kahoot.com/hc/en-us/community/posts/44325420738323-SPECIAL-CHARACTERS-IN-ANSWER-TYPES)). Nearpod's Time to Climb scores "speed and accuracy" on two to four multiple-choice options ([Nearpod FAQ](https://news.nearpod.com/pdf/Time-to-Climb-FAQs-ISTE-Launch.pdf)), and Quizizz, renamed Wayground in June 2025, is moving toward AI-generated "supplemental learning" ([THE Journal](https://thejournal.com/Articles/2025/06/24/Quizizz-Rebrands-as-Wayground-Announces-New-AI-Features.aspx?p=1)).

**Game economies** make luck part of the design. In Blooket's Gold Quest "every chest is random" — gold, a steal, a swap or a dragon ([Blooket help](https://help.blooket.com/hc/en-us/articles/42100784373655-Game-Overview-Gold-Quest)) — because its founder was "tired of the same kids always winning" ([UBC ETEC522](https://blogs.ubc.ca/etec522/?p=5820)). Gimkit turns correct answers into cash for upgrades, caps live games at 500 players ([Gimkit help](https://help.gimkit.com/en/article/player-maximums-18mbcz0/)), charges $59.88 a year for Pro and $1,000 a year per school ([Gimkit Pro FAQ](https://help.gimkit.com/en/article/gimkit-pro-faq-14h6d62/)), and later rebuilt its streak bonus to reward accuracy over speed ([Gimkit blog](https://blog.gimkit.com/blog/an-upgrade-gets-an-upgrade)); teachers still complain that power-ups give students "too much power" ([Common Sense Education](https://www.commonsense.org/education/reviews/5029045/teacher-reviews)). The underlying need is real — weaker students want a chance — and luck is simply the cheapest way to give them one.

**Adaptive practice contests** cross schools but give up the shared paper. Sumdog runs free week-long contests ranked by accuracy rather than speed, with questions adapted to each child so that "any class... can win" and a cap of 1,000 questions per pupil ([Sumdog contests](https://learn.sumdog.com/en-gb/schools/maths/contests/how-mat-contests-work)). World Maths Day is free, with twenty 60-second live challenges ([3P Learning FAQ](https://www.3plearning.com/world-maths-day/faq/)); 99math lets children join with a code and a nickname and no account ([Educators Technology](https://www.educatorstechnology.com/2023/06/a-teachers-review-of-99math.html)) and runs global "Math Game Days" ([99math blog](https://blog.99math.com/post/en-teen-entrepreneur-revolutionizes-learning-math-through-competition-and-games)); Live Mathletics matches students worldwide "only with those on the same ranking" and offers an asynchronous Shadow Mode in which a student races a classmate's recorded score ([Live Mathletics guide](https://marketing-cdn.3plearning.com/uploads/docs/user-guides/Live-Mathletics_User-Guide_A4.pdf)). These are fluency and effort contests; none can say "everyone answered the same paper", which is exactly what a math club's selection round needs.

**Formal contests** have the paper and the rules but not the frequency. MATHCOUNTS (grades 6-8) runs a Sprint round of 30 problems in 40 minutes, a Target round of four pairs at 6 minutes a pair, a Team round of 10 problems in 20 minutes and an oral head-to-head Countdown at up to 45 seconds a problem ([MATHCOUNTS](https://www.mathcounts.org/comp)); it charges **$42-$53 per student for 2026-27** ([MATHCOUNTS registration guide](https://www.mathcounts.org/sites/default/files/2026-08/FINAL%20MATHCOUNTS%20Registration%20Guide%202027.pdf)) and drew more than 65,000 competitors in 2025-26 ([MATHCOUNTS annual report](https://mathcounts.org/annual26)). The AMC reaches **about 300,000 students a year** ([MAA](https://maa.org/math-competitions/about-amc)), and Math Kangaroo USA grew to **60,504 participants in 2026, up about 13%** ([Cuemath](https://www.cuemath.com/blog/math-kangaroo-2027-registration/)). All of them happen once a year, or a few times, and are run by schools.

**Rated arenas and effort leagues** serve enthusiasts directly and stay small. AoPS's For The Win! gives everyone the same problem, scores "both the correctness and speed", keeps a ranking that "may also come down" — and showed **14 players online** in a snapshot on 2026-10-21 ([AoPS FTW](https://artofproblemsolving.com/ftw)). MathDash, founded by two contest alumni, runs Kahoot-style synchronous contests with an Elo rating and divisions, and in mid-2024 reported **500 daily and 1,500 weekly active users, 40% one-month retention and 20% week-over-week organic growth, with everything free** ([MIT Solve](https://solve.mit.edu/solutions/90601)). ContestDojo is the registration back office for student tournaments such as Berkeley Math Tournament, where any adult registers as a coach, creates an "organization", receives a join code and assigns seats to teams ([BMT FAQs](https://berkeley.mt/faqs/)). Brilliant's leagues are weekly experience-point competitions among 30 learners that reward effort, not skill ([Brilliant help](https://help.brilliant.org/en/articles/6399120-leagues-faq)).

The empty cell is the combination of a **fixed, verified paper, identified participants, scoring fair to children and regular competition between organizations**. FTW's fourteen people online, under the best-known name in contest math, says the niche is under-served rather than saturated — and also warns that thin live audiences are a structural problem for any real-time math arena, not just an opportunity.

## Contest conventions, not game-show scoring, should set the rules

### The round structure competition families already know

MATHCOUNTS has already decomposed competition into the formats Aimtrue wants: a timed same-set round (Sprint), fewer and harder problems with more time that count double (Target, with individual score = Sprint + 2 × Target), a collaborative round (Team), and a live head-to-head (Countdown) in which, at nationals, the top 12 written finishers enter a bracket where the first correct answer within 45 seconds scores, early matches go to the best of three questions and the final four play first to three ([AoPS Wiki](https://artofproblemsolving.com/wiki/index.php/MathCounts)). The lesson is where speed belongs: the written rounds that decide most placings reward correctness, and a short, explicitly speed-based live format decides only among the top finishers.

Online contests add three devices worth taking. Purple Comet's window ran from 14 to 23 April 2026, with each team's supervisor choosing when its clock started ([Purple Comet](https://www.purplecomet.org/)) — the way one paper crosses time zones and schedules. Codeforces' **virtual participation** replays a past contest against its frozen standings, unrated, and asks people not to enter if they have seen the problems ([Codeforces](https://codeforces.com/blog/entry/21225)). Mathletics' Shadow Mode, racing a classmate's recorded run ([Live Mathletics guide](https://marketing-cdn.3plearning.com/uploads/docs/user-guides/Live-Mathletics_User-Guide_A4.pdf)), solves the waiting problem of live play in a small organization, and Sumdog's per-pupil question cap keeps time spent from dominating ([Sumdog](https://learn.sumdog.com/en-gb/schools/maths/contests/how-mat-contests-work)).

Pacing conventions give concrete defaults for children: MATHCOUNTS Sprint allows about **80 seconds a problem**, Math Kangaroo **2.5-3 minutes** (24-30 questions in 75 minutes) ([Think Academy](https://www.thethinkacademy.com/blog/?p=5573)) and Noetic **2.25 minutes** (20 problems in 45 minutes) ([Math Explorers Club](https://www.mathexplorersclub.com/noetic-learning-math-contest-prep-guide)). A contest division on the Beast Academy and AoPS levels can run at 80-90 seconds a question and a grade-level division at about two minutes. Aimtrue's answer types fit natively: every bank question is multiple choice or a number with a KaTeX stem, marked exactly by `checkAnswer` (`apps/api/db/schema.sql`), just as MATHCOUNTS answers are numbers, Purple Comet's are integers and Kangaroo's are five-option multiple choice ([Purple Comet](https://www.purplecomet.org/); [Think Academy](https://www.thethinkacademy.com/blog/?p=5573)).

### Speed belongs in the tie-break, never in a child's per-question score

Two independent lines of evidence argue against per-question speed points for children. Teachers asked for years for a mode that did not reward the fastest before Kahoot! shipped one ([Kahoot! blog](https://kahoot.com/blog/2025/05/06/teacher-takeover-accuracy-mode/)), and one study of a Kahoot-style tool found that because faster answers score higher, students "may feel anxiety and stress and may risk too much at the expense of spending enough time to find the right answer" ([i-JEP](https://online-journals.org/index.php/i-jep/article/download/21359/9529/75849)). The best-studied speed-scored math system, Math Garden, with **853 million responses from more than 452,000 children**, uses the rule S = (2x − 1)(d − t), paying the remaining time for a correct answer and subtracting it for a wrong one — and its own designers report that the rule "can have a negative effect on less confident, yet high ability users" while "a substantial amount of guessing remains" ([Brinkhuis et al. 2018](https://learning-analytics.info/index.php/JLA/article/view/5796/6478)).

Contest scoring solved this long ago by ranking on problems solved and using time only to separate ties: ICPC adds 20 minutes per wrong attempt and Codeforces Educational rounds 10, counted only on problems eventually solved ([Codeforces](https://codeforces.com/blog/entry/105575)), while LeetCode and AtCoder add 5 ([LeetCode](https://support.leetcode.com/hc/en-us/articles/360011984773-What-does-the-Finish-Penalty-Time-column-mean-in-the-ranking); [AtCoder](https://atcoder.jp/contests/abc465)). Guessing is handled in two accepted ways: the AMC pays **6 per correct answer, 1.5 per blank and 0 per wrong answer** ([Math Prep Pro](https://www.mathpreppro.com/blog/amc-10-2025-guide)), and Math Kangaroo deducts nothing but weights questions **3, 4 or 5 points** by difficulty ([Think Academy](https://www.thethinkacademy.com/blog/?p=5573)). Kangaroo's weighting suits Aimtrue particularly well: bank questions carry a writer-assigned difficulty of 1, 2 or 3 that has not yet been calibrated against real answers, and a gentle 3:4:5 ratio limits the damage one mislabelled question can do, where 1:2:3 would triple it.

### Three numbers, kept apart: practice skill, challenge best, clash rating

Chess sites learned to separate their numbers, and the structure maps onto math. Chess.com and Lichess run a slow **rated** puzzle mode in which the player's and the puzzle's ratings both move ([Chess.com help](https://support.chess.com/en/articles/8608686-how-do-puzzles-work-on-chess-com)) beside fast **unrated** score-attack modes whose only number is a personal best; Chess.com's live Puzzle Battle has its **own** rating, built from results against opponents, which "has nothing to do with the puzzles' difficulty" ([Chess.com Puzzle Battle](https://support.chess.com/en/articles/8708930-what-is-puzzle-battle-on-chess-com)); and Lichess gives Puzzle Storm **no leaderboard at all** because "where there is a leaderboard, there is cheating" ([Lichess](https://lichess.org/page/storm)).

For Aimtrue that means three numbers. Questions get a difficulty rating estimated from practice answers, the way Math Garden updates person and item together after every answer ([Brinkhuis et al. 2018](https://learning-analytics.info/index.php/JLA/article/view/5796/6478)) — the cheapest way to calibrate an AI-written bank — and never moved by tournament answers given under unusual pressure or collusion. Challenges stay unrated, reported as a personal best, a fixed-threshold award and a percentile band. Clashes get a separate Glicko-2 rating, starting at **1500 with a deviation of 350 and a volatility of 0.06**, reported honestly as rating ± 2 deviations and updated after each event or session, since the system works best at **10-15 games per rating period** ([Glickman](http://www.glicko.net/glicko/glicko2.pdf)). Codeforces gives a parent-friendly reading of rating gaps — **200 points means the higher-rated player finishes ahead about 75% of the time, 400 points about 90%** ([Codeforces](https://codeforces.com/topic/20847/en1)) — and AoPS's Alcumus shows a per-topic rating as a probability, so a 75 means three in four expected right ([AoPS blog](https://artofproblemsolving.com/blog/articles/alcumus-a-peek-under-the-hood-of-our-adaptive-learning-tool)), a framing that suits a mastery score shown to parents. AtCoder's declared rated ranges, with Beginner Contests rated only for 0-1999, keep strong players from farming beginner events ([AtCoder](https://atcoder.jp/contests/abc465)).

### Recommended rules

| Element | Recommendation | Reason from the evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Challenge score | 3, 4 or 5 points per correct answer by the bank's difficulty 1, 2 or 3; nothing deducted for a wrong answer | Kangaroo's transparent no-penalty weighting; a gentle ratio caps the damage of an uncalibrated difficulty |
| Guessing | No blank credit in grades 3-5; in contest divisions, optional AMC-style credit of a quarter of a question's value for a blank multiple-choice answer | The AMC's 1.5 of 6 is the standard deterrent; small children are better served by simplicity |
| Tie-breaks | Fewer wrong answers, then less time from start to final submission as measured by the server; otherwise the place is shared | Puzzle Battle breaks ties on fewer errors; ICPC and AtCoder use time only to break ties |
| Speed | Never per-question points in a challenge | Kahoot!'s retreat; i-JEP; Math Garden's own warning |
| Awards | Fixed thresholds (for example 90%, 75% and 50% of the maximum), never "top N" | The MAA's fixed AIME cutoffs stop a cheater displacing an honest child |
| What a child sees | Their own score, award, personal best and a band ("top quarter"), not "27th of 30" | Lichess; rank-position research (next section) |
| Team score | Average of an organization's top four in each division | MATHCOUNTS team convention; stops big centers winning on volume |
| Duel (clash) | Same question sequence for both, 3-minute clock, three strikes, the opponent plays on; most correct wins, ties to fewer wrong, then the earlier last correct answer | Puzzle Battle: accuracy counts and no child waits |
| Countdown (clash) | One question to both, 45 seconds, the first correct answer scores and a wrong answer ends that player's turn on it; best of three, first to three in a final | MATHCOUNTS conventions families already know |
| Participation | Season points to every player for each correct answer, a bonus for a win, a monthly reset | Puzzle Battle pays season points win or lose |
| Clash rating | Glicko-2 (1500, 350, 0.06), shown to children as a band and to adults as a number; provisional until the deviation drops to about 100 | Glickman; hides early swings |
| Late entrants | Virtual participation: unranked, against the frozen standings | Codeforces |

## Leagues and streaks lift engagement; public ranks hurt the bottom

The engagement evidence is strong but mostly company-reported. Duolingo's leagues, which match learners of similar engagement into weekly groups, raised **overall learning time 17%** and **tripled "highly engaged learners"**, and streak work took the share of daily users on a 7-plus-day streak **nearly 3x**, to over half ([Lenny's Newsletter](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/how-duolingo-reignited-user-growth)); one controlled test that separated the daily goal from the streak gained **3.3% in Day-14 retention** ([Duolingo blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/improving-the-streak)). Brilliant runs leagues of 30 over 10 levels in which, in the first five, 15 are promoted and 5 drop ([Brilliant help](https://help.brilliant.org/en/articles/6399120-leagues-faq)), and lets learners bank **up to two "streak charges"** to save a missed day ([Brilliant streak charge FAQ](https://help.brilliant.org/en/articles/8507079-streak-charge-faq)); Chess.com's daily puzzle streak survives as long as each puzzle is solved within **48 hours** ([Chess.com help](https://support.chess.com/en/articles/8608686-how-do-puzzles-work-on-chess-com)). These numbers measure time and retention, not learning, and the downsides are documented too: "streak anxiety" driven by loss aversion ([ScreenWise](https://screenwiseapp.com/guides/duolingo-and-the-streak-obsession)) and top-league players for whom staying put became "a compulsion" ([po-ru.com](https://po-ru.com/2022/05/30/week-73-competitive-learning)).

On learning, the research is modest and two-sided on competition. A meta-analysis of gamification found **g = .49 on cognitive outcomes** — the only effect that held in the rigorous subset — against .36 for motivation and .25 for behaviour ([Sailer & Homner](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-019-09498-w)). Hanus & Fox's 16-week course with a leaderboard, badges and coins ended with **lower motivation and lower grades** in the gamified section, which the authors attributed to competition and long exposure ([Dichev & Dicheva review](https://educationaltechnologyjournal.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41239-017-0042-5/tables/13)). Leaderboards lift those near the top and harm those near the bottom, whose members in one classroom felt it "was not worth trying" ([GamiFIN 2017](https://ceur-ws.org/Vol-1857/gamifin17_p7.pdf)). Among **274 first and second graders** learning proportion, competition and collaboration both raised perseverance, but only competition produced gender differences, with older boys underperforming ([Child Development](https://academic.oup.com/chidev/article/94/5/1239/8255182)), and girls carry math anxiety beyond what their achievement predicts, d = .21-.25 ([PMC6876627](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6876627)). The reassuring finding is about stakes: across 694 students, ungraded Kahoot! play was rated **less** anxiety-provoking than group work, and lower performers engaged more ([CBE-LSE](https://lifescied.org/doi/10.1187/cbe.20-08-0187)).

Four defaults follow. Give weaker students a chance through **fair grouping rather than luck** — divisions by level, near-peer groups of 20-30, team scores that average a club's best four, and awards against thresholds every child can reach. Make **speed and public rank opt-in**, and pair every timed challenge with an untimed practice copy. Run **short seasons**, since Hanus & Fox's decline built over a semester. Send streak nudges **to parents, not children**, and forgive missed days, honouring Duolingo's own rule to "protect the channel" ([Lenny's Newsletter](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/how-duolingo-reignited-user-growth)). This bears directly on the planned Phase 45: an opt-in leaderboard of Quest points would rank children by volume, exactly what Duolingo's top league turned into a compulsion, and is better replaced by challenge standings, which rank a fixed paper fairly.

## Every unsupervised answer-only contest is now AI-solvable

The AMC's 2025 cycle shows where open contests have landed. An investigation published in February 2026 found Discord servers taking pre-orders for AMC solutions at **$200-$400 per test**, an AMC 12A file posted "a full day before administration", and hired former olympiad qualifiers solving the papers "often with generative AI"; perfect AMC 12A scores rose from **fewer than 30 in 2022 to "well over 300"** ([Lakeside Tatler](https://tatler.lakesideschool.org/8567/showcase/solutions-for-sale-inside-the-discord-marketplaces-undermining-americas-top-math-competitions/)), and the AMC 12A's Distinguished Honor Roll required a perfect 150 ([Harker Aquila](https://harkeraquila.com/99024/science-and-technology/maa-overhauls-aime-rules-over-cheating-concerns/)). AI capability makes the point general: two labs' models reached **IMO gold-level scores in July 2025** ([TechRepublic](https://www.techrepublic.com/article/news-openai-model-math-olympiad/)), reported AIME 2025 scores run from 86.7% to 100% ([BenchLM](https://benchlm.ai/benchmarks/aime2025)), and grade 3-8 multiple choice is far easier than either. Browser extensions already read a question and answer within seconds, and very few studies can detect AI help from answer logs alone ([arXiv 2510.18881](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.18881)).

The organizers' response has been structural rather than surveillance. The MAA moved AIME qualification to **fixed cutoffs** (100 on the AMC 10, 85 on the AMC 12), put the AIME itself in **Pearson test centers** on 5-6 February 2027 for students 13 and over ([MAA](https://maa.org/news/maa-announces-official-testing-centers-for-the-2027-american-invitational-mathematics-examination/); [Harker Aquila](https://harkeraquila.com/99024/science-and-technology/maa-overhauls-aime-rules-over-cheating-concerns/)), and kept banning remote proctoring with a discussion embargo until the morning after the window ([AMC 8 manual](https://maa.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/06/2025-26-AMC-8-Teachers-Manual.pdf)). Other contests sort integrity into tiers: Math Kangaroo's proctored and remote-proctored modes give an official rank while its unproctored "Virtual" mode gives only a ranking range ([Think Academy](https://www.thethinkacademy.com/blog/?p=5573)); Noetic's $29 At-Home option gives a percentile and no medals ([Math Explorers Club](https://www.mathexplorersclub.com/noetic-math-contest-at-home)); mathleague.org requires a Zoom camera, a silent adult chaperone, a password release and "release number" embargoes ([mathleague.org](https://mathleague.org/onlinecontests.php)); and Purple Comet now bans "ChatGPT, Gemini, Copilot, or Claude" by name ([Purple Comet](https://purplecomet.org/changes)). Tab-switch and fullscreen checks are weak signals that cannot see a second device, and even a glance at the taskbar clock trips them ([Redmenta](https://help.redmenta.com/en/article/13hjcy4/)).

For Aimtrue the lessons become rules. Unsupervised challenges stay **unrated, with fixed-threshold awards**, so a cheater gains a badge but displaces nobody; only live or supervised play is ranked, and live clashes at 30-45 seconds a question make a chatbot round-trip uncomfortable, which is why Countdown is the most AI-resistant online format. The server owns the clock, stamping when each question is served and each answer arrives and never trusting a client's duration; sets are released only when the clock starts; question order and choices are shuffled per student; keys and solutions wait until the window closes; and anomalies — a correct answer far faster than the question's difficulty predicts, near-constant answer times, identical wrong answers across children — go to the tutor as prompts to look, never as accusations. Two Aimtrue facts strengthen this. The platform knows exactly which questions each student has seen (`quest_attempts` is unique per student and question), so it can build a challenge **no participant has met before**, a guarantee no paper contest can make; and the Phase 36 pipeline can write a **fresh, embargoed set for each event**, linted, solved blind and approved by a person, so a leak costs one event, not the bank.

Integrity has a second half: **the key must be right.** A wrong key in a ranked event is a fairness failure no anomaly check can catch, and today only about a dozen questions are approved by a person; the rest are `checked`, and single-tutor Quest approvals are only beginning to add to the pool. Ranked challenges should therefore draw only `approved` questions and carry a contest-style regrade: after the window closes, a tutor may void a question, which goes to the console as flagged, and every entry re-scores automatically because scores are derived, not stored.

## Privacy and store rules make live play a design problem

**COPPA as amended has been fully enforceable since 22 April 2026**, with penalties up to **$53,088 per violation**, a separate parental consent before a child's data goes to a third party for a purpose not integral to the service, a written security programme and a published retention schedule ([Mondaq](https://webiis10.mondaq.com/unitedstates/privacy-protection/1790866/coppas-amended-rule-is-now-in-full-effect-what-operators-need-to-know); [Davis Polk](https://www.davispolk.com/insights/client-update/ftc-prioritizes-coppa-enforcement-new-compliance-obligations-take-effect)). The FTC's FAQ shapes this feature in four places ([FTC COPPA FAQ](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/complying-coppa-frequently-asked-questions)): game scores are "support for internal operations" and low-risk; a screen name becomes personal information when it "functions as online contact information", so display names are the risk; a contest may collect only a child's online contact information, contact the child once and route a winner's address through the parent; and only a *school* may consent in a parent's place "in the educational context". A private tutoring center is a commercial customer, so **verifiable parental consent is the safe default** for under-13s. Question Quest already collects answers online from signed-in children, which makes the consent centre (F7, `docs/roadmap/feature-plans.md`) overdue for gamification generally, not only a dependency of tournaments.

Live play carries the enforcement history. The FTC's **$275 million** Epic Games order followed default-on voice and text chat that matched children with strangers, and requires both to be off by default and enabled only with a parent's affirmative consent ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2022/12/fortnite-video-game-maker-epic-games-pay-more-half-billion-dollars-over-ftc-allegations)). Apple says "random or anonymous chat" does not belong on the App Store, requires filtering, reporting and blocking wherever users post content, and requires contests to be developer-sponsored with in-app official rules disclaiming Apple ([Apple guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)); Google Play requires "adult action" before children can exchange personal information and adult controls over any social feature ([Google Play Families](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/9893335)). Texas's App Store Accountability Act is enforceable pending appeal ([Pearl Cohen](https://www.pearlcohen.com/fifth-circuit-stays-injunctions-against-texas-app-store-accountability-act/)) and Utah's key duties start on 6 May 2027 ([Loeb & Loeb](https://www.loeb.com/en/insights/passle/2026/05/update-on-utah-app-store-law--another-waiting-game)), so the app must honour the age and consent signals the stores pass. The UK expects children's profiles private by default ([RPC on the ICO code](https://www.rpclegal.com/snapshots/data-protection/spring-2023/ico-publishes-guidance-on-compliance-of-game-design-with-the-childrens-code/)), and the EU's draft guidance on minors even asked for streaks off by default ([Covington](https://www.covingtonblogs.com/2025/05/22/european-commission-publishes-draft-guidelines-on-the-protection-of-minors-under-the-dsa/)).

The design that follows has no chat or free text at all, at most a few preset reactions; **no random matchmaking for children**, only opponents from the child's own organization or from an event an organization deliberately entered; system-assigned handles wherever results cross organizations, never names or chosen nicknames; visibility that defaults to the organization; and no cash-like prizes, paid entry or element of chance, because paid entry plus chance plus a prize makes a lottery ([Venable](https://www.venable.com/-/media/files/events/2022/03/sweepstakes-contests-and-other-types-of-giveaways.pdf)). Prodigy is the cautionary tale for monetization: in 2021, 22 groups asked the FTC to act against in-game upsells pushed at children who met the product free at school ([Axios](https://www.axios.com/prodigy-math-game-ftc-complaint-d693af67-019a-4ff5-9bff-8c73d1277342.html)). Never sell to the child.

Accessibility shapes the timer. WCAG 2.1 AA remains the procurement standard, with the Department of Justice's deadlines for public entities moved to **26 April 2027 and 26 April 2028** ([Plante Moran](https://plantemoran.com/explore-our-thinking/insight/2026/04/doj-extends-ada-title-ii-web-accessibility-compliance-deadlines)). Some contests forbid extra time in competition rounds ([MOCTM](https://moctm.org/images/pdf/FAQ_-_Middle_Contest.pdf)), but tutoring families with an IEP or 504 plan will expect it. The answer is a per-student time multiplier (1.25x, 1.5x, 2x or untimed), set privately by an adult, recorded and visible only to staff; across organizations, extended-time entries get their own listing. A turn-based alternative to the live clash, keyboard-operable answer entry and no colour-only signalling complete the picture.

## Tutoring centers buy retention; families buy contests

Market-size figures are unusable: estimates of the global game-based learning market range from **$9.22 billion for 2024** ([Market Growth Reports](https://market-growth-reports.odoo.com/blog/our-blog-1/game-based-learning-market-size-shares-regional-insights-and-forecasts-through-2033-11)) to **$24.5 billion for 2025** ([IMARC](https://www.imarcgroup.com/game-based-learning-market)), a 2.7x spread that comes from defining the category differently. Company evidence holds up better. Kahoot! was taken private at about **$1.7 billion** in January 2024 ([Kahoot!](https://kahoot.com/press/2024/01/23/kahoot-delisted-from-oslo-bors-following-successful-acquisition-led-by-goldman-sachs-asset-management/); [K-12 Dive](https://www.k12dive.com/news/kahoot-acquired-17-billion-deal/688424/)), after last reporting 1.3 million paid subscriptions in 2022 ([Kahoot! Q4 2022](https://kahoot.com/files/2023/02/2023-02-16_Q4-2022-Earnings-Presentation.pdf)), and Duolingo reached **12.5 million paid subscribers in Q1 2026, up from 10.3 million** ([Duolingo](https://investors.duolingo.com/static-files/aab30d54-eb91-422e-b365-c03859fea85c)) — families plainly pay for gamified daily practice at scale.

The sizing that matters to Aimtrue is bottom-up. Mathnasium's 2026 disclosure lists **1,047 outlets** with **average gross receipts of $384,874** (median $326,428) per center open a year, paying a 10% royalty ([Franchise Chatter](https://www.franchisechatter.com/2026/07/21/mathnasium-franchise-review-2026-costs-fees-news-average-revenues-and-or-profits/)); its tuition runs about **$250-$400 a month**, per a lower-quality source ([ThePricer](https://www.thepricer.org/mathnasium-cost/)). At that tuition, keeping one student a year longer is worth $3,000-$4,800 to a center — far more than any engagement add-on would cost, and the core of the pitch for in-organization challenges.

| Who pays | What for | Price anchor |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tutoring centers | Management software | About **$35-$145 a month** for 80 students ([Teach 'n Go](https://www.teachngo.com/blog/teachworks-vs-tutorbird)) |
| Teachers | Quiz games | **$36-$228 a year** each ([Kahoot!](https://kahoot.com/schools/plans/)) |
| Parents | Consumer subscriptions | Beast Academy Online **$99.99 a year** ([Beast Academy](https://help.beastacademy.com/a/1866695-subscriptions-overview)); Brilliant **$240** ([Brilliant](https://brilliant.org/resources/choosing-brilliant/is-brilliant-worth-it/)) |
| Families or schools | Contest entry, per student per event | Math Kangaroo **$18-$20**, MATHCOUNTS **$42-$53**, AMC about $2.50-$3 per student plus a school fee ([Kangaroo memo](https://mathkangaroo.org/mks/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/Memo-2026-v.2.pdf); [MAA](https://maa.org/amcreg)) |
| Parents | An *unofficial* at-home contest | Noetic At-Home **$29** for a percentile alone ([Math Explorers Club](https://www.mathexplorersclub.com/noetic-math-contest-at-home)) |

Noetic's price shows that a credible comparison with peers is itself worth money to parents, even without official standing. Demand signals favour broad formats over elite ones: MATHCOUNTS competition schools grew **1%** to 3,762 while its non-competitive National Math Club grew **6%** to 45,587 students ([MATHCOUNTS](https://mathcounts.org/annual26)), Math Kangaroo USA grew **13%** ([Cuemath](https://www.cuemath.com/blog/math-kangaroo-2027-registration/)), and Mathnasium opened **63 US locations in 2025** ([1851 Franchise](https://1851franchise.com/mathnasium/mathnasium-2025-record-growth-romania-2026-expansion-2731372)). MATHCOUNTS itself is digitizing around its physical contest with a livestreamed Countdown and new parent dashboards ([MATHCOUNTS](https://mathcounts.org/annual26)), and MathDash's 40% one-month retention ([MIT Solve](https://solve.mit.edu/solutions/90601)) suggests rated play keeps the enthusiasts it reaches, even if FTW's thin room shows how few it reaches.

The reachable buyers, in order, are independent centers and small chains (franchise technology is chosen and billed by the franchisor, which makes Mathnasium-style systems hard to sell into one center at a time ([Franchise Chatter](https://www.franchisechatter.com/2026/07/21/mathnasium-franchise-review-2026-costs-fees-news-average-revenues-and-or-profits/))); math clubs and circles run by any adult, the coach model ContestDojo serves ([BMT FAQs](https://berkeley.mt/faqs/)); families paying per event; and, last, schools, which bring FERPA, WCAG procurement and district agreements. The business model should split accordingly: play inside an organization comes with the organization's plan as a retention tool; open events between organizations start with a free pilot season and then carry about **$5-$10 per student per event**, below Kangaroo, honest about being unofficial and in line with Noetic's at-home price; supervised official events may charge more. Sponsorship, which funds MATHCOUNTS through defence, engineering and quant-trading firms ([MATHCOUNTS](https://mathcounts.org/annual26)), is a later option at a scale Aimtrue has not reached.

## Aimtrue's edge is the verified paper between identified students

Aimtrue's differentiation is a cluster no competitor holds whole. Its keys are checked by code — the `compute` expression evaluated exactly in big-integer fractions — and each question is solved blind by a second model with the key hidden, which answers the critique of shallow, teacher-typed quiz content; KaTeX stems and exact marking replace symbol palettes and letters-only answer fields. Its participants are enrolled members of an organization with guardians on file, precisely what nickname games lack, so trustworthy standings become possible and consent has an obvious home. Its organizations with tutors already mirror ContestDojo's coach, organization and seat model. Its bank spans NC 2026 grades 3-8 and Math 1 plus Beast Academy and AoPS levels, so a "Standards" division can serve the broad club while a "Contest" division serves MATHCOUNTS-bound enthusiasts. And no quiz game feeds a tutoring record: Aimtrue can show a tutor on the Phase 43 Home that Sofia missed three fraction questions in Saturday's challenge and set the next lesson's topic from it.

Its own gaps are as concrete:

| Gap | Today | What closes it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Approved questions | About a dozen approved by a person; single-tutor Quest approvals just started | Quest throughput; platform-admin approval for anything used between organizations; fresh sets per event |
| Children's access | Google sign-in only; Quest limited to signed-in students (owner decision 3, `docs/gamification-plan.md`) | F1: a guardian's "Continue as", or a code shown in the lesson |
| Consent | No consent records | F7: a `consents` table with purposes such as `cross_org_competition` |
| Real time | No WebSockets or Durable Objects | One Durable Object per match (Phase B) |
| Answer timing | `quest_attempts` records only `submitted_at` | Served and answered timestamps, for calibration and anomaly checks |
| Read budget | D1 free tier: 5 million rows read a day per account, shared with production | Results written once; standings from indexed rows; no polling |
| Content breadth | Standards needing a figure are skipped; no parameterized variants | Later; shuffled order and choices cover most sharing within a window |
| Cross-organization rule | R13: "nothing crosses organizations" | An explicit new exposure rule built on handles, with its crawl |

## A five-phase roadmap from club challenge to cross-center finals

These are proposals: under the project's rules each becomes a numbered phase only when the owner writes it into `docs/plan.md`. They naturally follow Phase 44 (streaks, metrics and badges); Phase A could take the place of Phase 45's opt-in points leaderboard while Phase 45's rewards shelf and certificates stay as planned. Sizes use the project's S/M/L/XL scale, with Phase 42 (three tables, an organization flag, full-screen flows on web and app) as the unit.

| Phase | Formats | Scoring and rating | Precondition | Size and risk |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A. Club Challenge | Sprint (same set, timed, within a window), its untimed practice copy, virtual replay | Weighted correct answers; ties to fewer wrong, then time; fixed-threshold awards; unrated | Enough approved questions per level | M (about 1-1.5x Phase 42); low-medium |
| B. Live Clash | Duel, Countdown, Shadow duel, optional group round | Duel and Countdown rules; season points; unrated | Durable Objects on all three Workers | L (about 2-3x); medium-high |
| C. Open Challenges | Sprint across organizations, by division and window | As A, plus percentile bands and a top-four team average; unrated, unofficial tier | F1, F7, a new exposure rule | M plus F1 and F7 (each M); medium-high, mostly policy |
| D. Ratings and leagues | Rated clashes, weekly near-peer leagues inside an organization, question calibration | Glicko-2 clash rating shown as bands; question ratings from practice; rated ranges | Clash data from B | M; medium |
| E. Supervised tournaments | Supervised Sprint plus a canvas Target round, live Countdown finals between organizations, a team Swiss league | Official standings for supervised entries; qualifying thresholds; Sonneborn-Berger tie-break | A-D; a lawyer's review of fees and prizes | L-XL; high |

### Phase A: a Club Challenge on one paper inside one organization

**Scope.** An admin or tutor creates a challenge with a title, a division (one bank level: G3-G8, Math 1, BA1-BA5 or AoPS), 5-25 questions, a pace preset (about two minutes a question for grade levels, 80-90 seconds for contest levels), an open-close window (a week by default) and invitees (their own students, or the organization's signed-in students at that level). The Worker draws approved questions of the level, preferring ones no invitee has met in Quest or an earlier challenge, and freezes the set; the creator may also pick by hand. A student opens it full screen and the server starts the clock at first open; the paper allows moving between questions as real contest papers do, saves each answer as it is given, and closes on submit or when the clock runs out. Results, keys and solutions appear only after the window closes, after which the challenge remains as an untimed practice copy and a virtual replay against the frozen standings, both unranked.

**Scoring and what people see.** Points are 3, 4 or 5 by difficulty with nothing deducted for a wrong answer; ties go to fewer wrong answers, then to server-measured time. Gold, silver and bronze awards at fixed thresholds go into Phase 44's badges, points for taking part and for awards go into the existing points ledger, and a challenge counts as an active day for streaks. A student sees their own score, award, personal best and band; guardians see their own child's result; tutors see their students; admins see the full table; a first-name class table appears only if the organization turns it on.

**Integrity and privacy.** The server owns the clock with a small latency grace; order and choices are shuffled per student (choices are compared as text, so shuffling cannot change the marking); questions are served only after the start and keys are withheld until the close; per-question timestamps feed "worth a look" flags for fast correct answers on difficulty-3 questions, near-constant timing and identical wrong answers, with tab-away counts as a soft web signal; and a tutor or admin can void a question after the close, flagging it to the console while scores re-derive. Phase A stays inside one organization under a new exposure rule (R20) modelled on Quest's R18 and crawled the same way; the audit log records that a challenge was created, closed or had a question voided, never an answer. Its privacy posture equals Quest's (answers from signed-in children) — another reason to schedule F7 — and per-student time multipliers stay private.

**Reuse, data and risk.** It reuses the bank, `checkAnswer` and KaTeX rendering; Quest's full-screen answering screens on web and app, iPad layout included; the organization-flag pattern (`gamify_challenges`); `points_ledger`, `activity_days` and Phase 44's `user_badges`; the audit and exposure-crawl machinery; and the Assessment PDF writer for certificates. Data is four additive, organization-scoped tables — `challenges`, `challenge_questions`, `challenge_entries` (start, submission, time multiplier) and `challenge_answers` (first seen, answered, answer, correct) — and a 30-student, 20-question challenge costs a few thousand row reads. The real constraint is supply, not code: a 10-question challenge on one level needs ten approved questions there that no invitee has seen. Until Quest approvals catch up, practice-only challenges may draw `checked` questions labelled "not yet reviewed", mirroring `canvas_assessments_unreviewed`; ranked ones may not.

**Success metrics** (proposed targets, measured from a baseline in organizations with the flag on): at least one challenge per active tutor a month; at least 60% of invited students start and 90% of starters finish; at least 40% of participants enter a second challenge within 30 days; voided questions below 2%. Alongside, compare Quest active days of participants against non-participants, watch a one-tap "how did that feel?" for anxiety broken down by gender, and over a longer horizon track assessment scores on the challenged standards.

### Phase B: a Live Clash inside one organization, hosted by a tutor

**Scope.** Real-time play on one Durable Object per match, with WebSocket clients on web and app and the binding restated on all three Workers under the bindings rule. A tutor pairs students, or a student invites a classmate in the same organization; there is no random matchmaking. Four formats share the engine:

| Format | How it plays |
| --- | --- |
| **Duel** | Puzzle Battle rules: the same question sequence for both, a 3-minute clock, three strikes, the opponent plays on, scores shown live. Each player moves at their own pace, so mobile latency rarely decides a result. |
| **Countdown** | The MATHCOUNTS ritual for a club session: 45 seconds (60 for grades 3-5), the first correct answer scores, best of three, a bracket seeded from a Phase A challenge's top finishers. Best on one room's network. |
| **Shadow duel** | Race a classmate's recorded run, Mathletics-style. Needs no Durable Object and solves the empty-opponent problem in a small organization. |
| **Group round** (optional) | Host-paced for up to about 30 players, scored like Kahoot!'s Accuracy mode: a point per correct answer, total answer time breaking ties. |

**Scoring, integrity and privacy.** Phase B is unrated. Season points go into the ledger — one per correct answer for every player plus a bonus for a win, with a monthly view that resets — and results are stored to seed Phase D's ratings, with Lichess Arena's anti-farming rules adapted: no points for very short matches or for more than a few daily repeats against the same opponent ([Lichess](https://lichess.org/tournament/help?system=arena)). The Durable Object is the clock: it stamps each question, accepts an answer only inside the window plus a small latency allowance, marks it with the shared `checkAnswer`, and reveals the next question only after recording the last; only approved questions are used, a disconnected player has about 15 seconds to reconnect before losing the current question, and a tutor is often in the room. There is no chat or free text, at most three preset reactions; opponents see each other's first names because they share an organization; results reach both players, their guardians, their tutors and the admins; the audit log records a clash, never its answers.

**Infrastructure, size and metrics.** On the free plan Durable Objects allow **100,000 requests and 13,000 GB-seconds a day, with WebSocket messages counted 20 to one** ([Cloudflare](https://developers.cloudflare.com/durable-objects/platform/pricing/)), which the project's own roadmap reads as about 28 hours a day of an object kept awake (`docs/roadmap/future-ai.md`) — roughly 560 three-minute duels if each stays awake throughout, more with hibernation between messages. Each result is written to D1 once, in one batch, at the end; nothing polls D1, protecting the read quota production depends on, and the test Worker's separate account keeps separate limits. The phase is about 2-3x Phase 42, as the first real-time feature on two clients with reconnection and two-device tests in Playwright and Maestro, and its risk is medium-high. Success means at least 97% of matches completing without a dropped connection, answers reaching the server within 400 ms at the 95th percentile, at least two clashes per participating student a week, regular Countdown sessions in each organization each month, a healthy rematch rate, the post-match feeling pulse, and no widening gender gap in participation.

### Phase C: Open Challenges across organizations, as an unofficial tier

**Preconditions.** F7 must exist so a guardian can record a `cross_org_competition` consent for each child; F1 should exist (a guardian's "Continue as" or an in-lesson code) or entries stay limited to the minority of students who sign in; and R13 in `docs/data-exposure.md` must be amended explicitly, with a crawl of what crosses.

**Scope and scoring.** A platform admin creates an Open Challenge in the console (whether host organizations may too is the owner's decision), with divisions by level, a 7-10 day window in which each student starts their own clock on the Purple Comet model, and one frozen paper per division drawn from a **reserved, freshly written** set — through the Phase 36 pipeline, lint and blind solve, approved by a platform admin, never served in Quest, and embargoed until the morning after the window. Organizations opt in and their admins enter consenting students, after which entries work exactly as in Phase A. Individuals receive **percentile bands** (top 1%, 5%, 10% and 25%) and fixed-threshold honour awards rather than a ranked list of other centers' children; teams are ranked by each organization's **top-four average** per division, shown with the organization's name unless it chooses anonymity. The tier is unrated and says so plainly, as Kangaroo's "Virtual" mode does, and virtual replay opens after the close.

**Integrity, privacy and business.** Fresh questions per event, per-student shuffling, embargoed keys, event rules banning AI tools and internet lookup in Purple Comet's wording (acknowledged by guardians), a platform admin's cross-organization review of timing anomalies and shared wrong-answer patterns, and fixed thresholds together mean a cheater displaces nobody. Children appear across organizations only as system-assigned handles that cannot serve as contact information and need no moderation; the console sees handles, divisions and scores but never names, a new console rule since platform admins today never read an organization's people; nothing passes messages between organizations; families receive results through their own organization; and extended-time entries get their own listing. Pricing is a free pilot season, then about $5-$10 per student per event or a season pass per organization. The phase reuses Phase A in full, the Phase 36 pipeline and console approval, the multi-organization model and branding, and email notices to admins when results post. It is M on top of F1 and F7, with medium-high risk that is mostly policy and legal. Success means 5-10 organizations in the pilot event, at least 70% of invited families completing consent, at least 60% of organizations entering a second event, the anomaly review's findings rate, admin satisfaction, and each event's D1 reads under 5% of the daily budget (a 2,000-student, 25-question event is roughly 150,000 row reads across its window).

### Phase D: ratings and leagues

**Scope.** Question calibration gives each question a rating estimated from practice and Quest answers, never tournaments, which needs the answer-timing columns noted among the gaps and in return balances papers across divisions, tunes the 3/4/5 weights and sharpens anomaly checks. A Glicko-2 clash rating per student per level family — a fact the organization records, so it carries `organization_id` — shows children a band such as "Silver III" while guardians, tutors and admins see the number and its interval; it stays provisional until the deviation falls to about 100 and updates once per session or event, as Glickman advises. Rated ranges and divisions stop strong students farming beginner events, and weekly near-peer leagues of 20-30 inside an organization rank by points from correct answers rather than raw volume, with generous promotion in the bottom tiers and no paid repair.

**Integrity, size and metrics.** Only live or supervised play is rated, the Lichess principle, and because every account is an identified person, smurfing is far harder than on open arenas; ratings stay inside the organization with the progress audience. The phase is M with medium risk, chiefly tuning and explaining ratings to parents, where the Codeforces "200 points = 75%" reading helps. Success means a well-calibrated rating (predicted against actual win rates), at least half of duels starting between 35% and 65% predicted, steady weekly league participation, no excess drop-off in demotion zones (the Hanus & Fox warning), and question ratings converging after a few hundred answers.

### Phase E: supervised tournaments and live finals between centers

**Scope.** A supervised tier lets a tutor attest that a student sat the challenge under supervision during a scheduled lesson at a recorded location (Phase 33), with the attester never the student's guardian, echoing the MAA's "unrelated" proctor; only supervised entries earn official standings and qualifying scores against fixed thresholds. A Target round on the canvas sets two to four harder problems that count double, marks the final answer by code and puts the working in the tutor's PDF (Phases 38 and 40) — the most AI-resistant written format Aimtrue can offer. Live Countdown finals between organizations bring together the top 8-12 qualifiers per division, seeded with byes on the MATHCOUNTS pattern and run on Phase B's Durable Objects, with a spectator view for organization members that shows handles only, carries no chat and no video or voice of children. A Swiss league of team duels between organizations follows Lichess Swiss, with Dutch pairing and the Sonneborn-Berger tie-break ([Lichess](https://lichess.org/zu/swiss)), and certificates close the loop.

**Prizes, privacy, size and metrics.** Prizes are non-monetary by default; any physical prize is sponsored by the developer under in-app official rules disclaiming Apple, with the address requested from the parent, and entry fees for a contest of skill need a promotions lawyer's state-by-state review that the research could not settle. Live play between organizations needs its own consent purpose, the analogue of the Epic order's affirmative consent, and opponents see a handle and an organization name. The phase is L to XL with high risk across fees and prizes, live event operations and exposure between organizations. Success means a rising share of supervised entries, finals completed without technical failure, organizations returning each season, event revenue above cost, zero privacy incidents and sponsor interest.

## Risks that could sink the program

| Risk | Why it matters | Mitigation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| AI and leak cheating | AMC papers sold and AI-solved in 2025; perfect scores rose more than tenfold ([Lakeside Tatler](https://tatler.lakesideschool.org/8567/showcase/solutions-for-sale-inside-the-discord-marketplaces-undermining-americas-top-math-competitions/)) | Unsupervised play stays unrated; fixed thresholds; fresh embargoed sets; a supervised tier for official standings |

## Conclusion

The opportunity in "challenges and clashes" is not a quiz game — that market is crowded, nearly free and criticised for exactly the speed and luck mechanics a newcomer would be tempted to copy. The opening is the trust layer underneath competition: the verified key, the identified participant, the unseen question and the fair tie-break, which AMC organizers, Kahoot! teachers and Sumdog's adaptive contests each lack in some combination. Aimtrue's own rules — exact marking by code, nothing crossing organizations, no money and no AI on children's data — turn out to be competitive advantages here rather than constraints, and they let a contest platform promise what no paper contest can in 2026: **no participant has seen these questions, every key has been checked twice, and nobody profits from cheating, because nothing unsupervised is ranked.**

Two facts should set the order of work more than any feature list.
