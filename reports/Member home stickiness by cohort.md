# Lead every home with its next action

Aimtrue should replace its five stitched signed-in layouts with **one stable member home whose top card ("Next up") is chosen by a fixed, time-bound priority**, whose sections are ordered by each person's hats and arrival intent, and whose habit layer is a forgiving weekly streak rather than daily pressure. The internal evidence is lopsided: production shows a real, repeatable loop for exactly one cohort, the tutor, who **recorded 22 lessons on 19 days and assessed 17 of them in 60 days**, while students and parents have no organic sign-ins at all and Quest, the Daily and Question Quest have **zero real answers** ([production usage data](../research_notes/Member%20home%20stickiness%20by%20cohort/production_usage_data.md)). Yet the web tutor needs about six taps to record today's lesson, the parent's web home leads with money and never shows the next lesson, and the newcomer's home swaps the guest page's "Grade 4 · Quick 5" for "BA1Q5 · Beta" ([current homes inventory](../research_notes/Member%20home%20stickiness%20by%20cohort/current_homes_inventory.md)). Every leading learner, parent and tutor product puts one dominant "do the next thing" control above the fold, and the best HCI evidence says to *copy* the predicted action to the top rather than reorder or hide anything. For children, the mechanics that measurably raise return visits without harm are low-bar, slack-tolerant, weekly goals with a kind "welcome back", not leaderboards, prizes or pushes. Tiles should be coloured by grade from a colour-blind-safe set and never rely on colour alone. Stickiness should be measured weekly (weekly actives over monthly actives, days played per week, week-over-week streak retention, cohort retention by hat, space kind and arrival), because a 3-days-a-week target makes daily metrics look bad by design. The cohort homes for students, parents, Quest and Assessment are therefore hypotheses grounded in market evidence, and the first job of the redesign is to instrument them so the next analysis has data.

## One tutor's loop is the only measured habit; the rest is design

Production data from 2026-11-03 is thin but unambiguous about where behaviour exists. Three spaces hold 23 members; two run Tutoring alone and one runs Assessment and Quest. The single real tutor's loop is open the app, **record the lesson just taught, assess it**, sometimes fix it or move a date, several days a week. The owner-admin entered a space 68 times on 25 days to add people, set up pairings and record payments. Everything else is the owner testing: 10 Quest games by 2 hosts, 1 canvas assessment, 0 Question Quest answers, 0 Daily results and 0 `activity_days` rows, the very input the streak is built from ([production usage data](../research_notes/Member%20home%20stickiness%20by%20cohort/production_usage_data.md)). Weekly sign-ins peaked at 42 in W41 and fell to 14 in W44, a decline too small to read as churn but a reminder that even the staff habit is not yet secure.

The current homes make the one proven loop harder than it needs to be and give the unproven cohorts nothing to latch onto. On the web, a tutor has **no primary action on the home**; recording today's lesson takes about six taps through the menu, and the form defaults to a hard-coded 16:00-17:00 rather than the schedule, while the app's Phase 43 "Record it now" card does the same job in one tap. Seven weeks of scheduled-but-unrecorded lessons in the seed are flagged nowhere. A parent's web home spends its first screen on Charged and Outstanding, with the next lesson two taps away; an admin's phone screen loses about 350 px to headers and selectors before any content; a parent-who-tutors with no pupils lands on an empty tutor dashboard while their child has a lesson today; and member level tiles are identical grey cards headlined by internal codes ([current homes inventory](../research_notes/Member%20home%20stickiness%20by%20cohort/current_homes_inventory.md)). The design question is therefore twofold: make the tutor's and admin's measured jobs nearly frictionless, and give every other cohort an obvious first action built from what comparable products do, then measure whether it takes.

## Eight cohorts ask eight different questions

Market research shows that each kind of user arrives with one question, and that the best products answer it in the first card. Tutoring and class businesses lead the parent's home with what is next: Outschool's signed-in homepage shows "upcoming enrolled classes", described as "the fastest way to confirm what's coming up next" ([Outschool](https://support.outschool.com/en/articles/2086860-how-to-use-outschool-as-a-parent)). Practice products lead with a short recent window that separates effort from mastery, as Beast Academy's two-week snapshot does, with a red flag on a struggling child ([Beast Academy Help](https://help.beastacademy.com/a/1793720-parent-reporting-overview)). Learner products put assigned work first: Khan Academy's Daily Mission, built from work due today, tomorrow or overdue, "sits at the top of the Learning Queue" ([Khan Academy Help](https://support.khanacademy.org/hc/articles/46056261189773)), and Zearn's "Next Up" is the teacher-assigned lesson ([Zearn Help](https://help.zearn.org/hc/en-us/articles/360058908394)). Self-directed learners get one big next control instead, from Khan Academy Kids' single green play button ([Common Sense Education](https://www.commonsense.org/education/app/khan-academy-kids)) to Duolingo's path, adopted because learners "weren't sure they were using Duolingo the 'correct' or 'best' way" ([Duolingo blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/new-duolingo-home-screen-design)). Tutoring software splits the same way between the tutor and the owner: TutorCruncher's tutor dashboard carries "Actions Required" for "outstanding lessons or reports" ([TutorCruncher](https://tutorcruncher.com/blog/introducing-the-new-dashboard-for-tutors)), Teachworks offers an "Uncompleted Lessons" widget beside owner widgets such as Overdue Invoices and a profit snapshot ([Teachworks](https://intercom.help/teachworks-e2d272c6e669/en/articles/14124151-the-teachworks-dashboard)), and studio tools make today's agenda the home, where the one recorded complaint is being unable to act from it ([Capterra](https://www.capterra.co.uk/software/181623/tutorbird)).

| Cohort | Evidence base | Their question | What Next up should offer |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Owner/admin of a tutoring space | Measured (people, payments, settings) | "What needs me today?" | Unrecorded lessons across tutors, join requests, invitations, today's agenda |
| Tutor | Measured (record + assess, 19 of 60 days) | "Who's next; did I record the last one?" | Lesson just ended → Record it now (prefilled); lesson soon → Start; reviews waiting |
| Tutoring parent | Market only | "When is the next lesson; has anything changed?" | Per child: next lesson or its cancellation, latest homework; money on Finance |
| Practice parent (from the guest page) | Market only | "Is my child practising, and getting it?" | A reviewed assessment, give one, **Play with <child>** |
| Tutored student | Market only | "What do I have to do?" | Assessment to continue; practise before the next lesson; the Daily |
| Quest player | Market only (guest funnel) | "Let me play." | Game invite → Join; Continue; Today's 5 |
| Assessment cohort | Market only | Student: "finish it"; giver: "give one, see results" | Continue or read reviewed; Start an assessment in two taps |
| Several roles | Inventory (wrong landing) | "What's urgent across my hats?" | The most time-bound item of any hat; one section per hat |

The parent rows deserve one more piece of evidence, because they shape tone as well as order. Nearly nine in ten parents believe their child is at grade level in maths when about half are not, and 97% of parents who knew their child was behind worried about maths against 22% of those who did not ([The 74, on Gallup and Learning Heroes](https://www.the74million.org/article/when-getting-good-grades-and-working-at-grade-level-are-not-the-same-thing/)). A parent card that reports only effort can reinforce that false comfort; one that pairs days practised with the level being worked and a concrete next step answers the question parents actually need answered. Parents also drown in volume: they juggle 10-15 school apps ([GovTech](https://www.govtech.com/education/k-12/using-too-many-school-apps-may-burden-parents-teachers)) and complain of Brightwheel's undifferentiated feed ([Capterra](https://www.capterra.com/p/144060/brightwheel/reviews/?page=72)), which argues for one calm card per child, not a feed. Co-play is the parent's most promising verb: joint media engagement research finds children learn more when parents talk and play alongside them ([Cooney Center](https://joanganzcooneycenter.org/publication/the-new-coviewing-designing-for-learning-through-joint-media-engagement/)), though young children often resist parent involvement ([Cooney Center](https://joanganzcooneycenter.org/?p=22597)), so "Play with <child>" should be offered, never pushed.

## Copy the likely action to the top; never reorder or hide

The strongest controlled evidence on adaptive homes comes from HCI studies of menus, and it is specific. Adaptive designs beat static ones only at high prediction accuracy (70%, not 30%), and the winning "split" design **copies predicted items to the top while leaving the originals in place** ([Gajos et al. 2006](https://kgajos.seas.harvard.edu/papers/kgajos-avi06.pdf)). Users preferred and were faster with user-controlled (adaptable) menus than with system-adaptive ones ([Findlater & McGrenere 2004](https://www.cs.ubc.ca/labs/edapt/papers/findlater2004.pdf)), and personalisation that hides unused features measurably lowers people's awareness of them and their performance on new tasks that need them ([Findlater thesis](https://open.library.ubc.ca/collections/24/items/1.0051322)). Netflix is the best industry evidence that a personalised home earns its keep, about 4-12% more engagement than simpler recommenders ([arXiv 2511.07280](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.07280)), but that gain comes from a vast, accurate system; a small product gains more from getting the first card right per arrival path than from ranking.

That evidence maps directly onto Aimtrue. Time-bound obligations are near-certain predictions of the next action: a lesson that just ended unrecorded, a lesson starting now, a cancellation to a child's lesson, a game invite, reviews waiting, an assessment half-done. They should outrank frequency guesses in a fixed priority, with "Later" dismissing an item for the day while everything else on the page keeps its place. Below the obligations, a **focus** sets the default card and the order of sections, drawn from, in turn, the person's own pin ("Make this my home"), their dominant activity over the last two weeks, how they arrived (a guest who played Quest, the guest Assessment, an invitation as tutor or parent, or making a space), and finally their roles. Arrival context should outrank a sign-up question: the near-universal "What brings you here?" question at Duolingo, Notion, Canva and Pinterest has **no published controlled result isolating its effect on retention**, and the circulating 30-50% lifts trace only to vendor marketing ([×marble](https://timesmarble.com/blog/personalization-for-saas-onboarding); [Chameleon](https://www.chameleon.io/blog/user-onboarding-best-practices)). The intent must stay a soft default: Kahoot fixes the account type at sign-up and changing it means contacting support ([Kahoot! Help](https://support.kahoot.com/hc/articles/14826253303315)), the wrong model for a product where one person is often a tutor and a parent at once, and Khan Academy teachers resent the Khanmigo Tools page being forced on them as their homepage ([Khan Academy community](https://support.khanacademy.org/hc/da/community/posts/31217984889613/comments/31576181690637)).

The awareness finding also settles the Quest-versus-Assessment tension. A Quest-first player should still see an Assessment entry point lower down, and an assessment giver should still see the Quest board: demote, never hide, or the cross-feature discovery that would deepen stickiness is lost. Classroom quiz tools show why the content, not the home, is the shared anchor: Blooket and Gimkit put **Host** and **Assign** on the same set ([TriviaMaker](https://triviamaker.com/blooket-vs-gimkit-vs-kahoot/)), just as an Aimtrue level tile can be played now or given as an assessment. For several roles, the evidence is thin; Airbnb's explicit "Switch to hosting" ([Airbnb Help](https://www.airbnb.com/help/article/3546)) suits roles whose data must never mix, which on Aimtrue is the money on Finance, while lighter hats are better served by one merged home that shows a section per hat present, hats with something today first, and hats with no content not at all.

For the office and the tutor, the home should become **operational first, analytical one tap away**. NN/g separates operational dashboards, which "impart critical information quickly to users as they are engaged in time-sensitive tasks", from analytical ones ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/dashboards-preattentive/)); today's web tutor and admin homes are analytical stat cards and spotlights, while their measured jobs are operational. The app's Phase 43 move of Analytics to More → Dashboard is the right direction, and the web should follow with the Now card and a prefilled Record it now.

## Weekly, forgiving habits fit children; leaderboards and pushes do not

Streaks are the best-evidenced return driver, and what makes them work is a low bar and slack. Separating Duolingo's streak from its daily XP goal, so one lesson extends it, raised Day-14 retention by **3.3%** and the share of new learners on a streak by 19%; learners with higher goals had been less likely to keep a streak, so the goal itself was the barrier ([Duolingo blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/improving-the-streak)). Duolingo's Weekend Amulet made learners **4% more likely to return a week later and 5% less likely to lose their streak** ([Duolingo blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/how-streaks-keep-duolingo-learners-committed-to-their-language-goals)). Peer-reviewed work explains why: people repeat a behaviour more when it extends an intact streak than after a broken one, an effect weaker when the streak can be repaired ([Silverman & Barasch, CU Boulder](https://www.colorado.edu/business/faculty-research/2023/04/19/or-track-how-broken-streaks-affect-consumer-decisions)); framing a goal as seven days with two emergency skips produced up to **40% more days meeting the goal** than a plain five-day goal ([UCLA Anderson Review](https://anderson-review.ucla.edu/emergency-reserves/)); the best of 54 gym programmes, at +27% weekly visits, rewarded **returning after a missed workout** ([Penn Today](https://penntoday.upenn.edu/news/wharton-study-best-ways-boost-workout-habits)); and paying for visits in a fixed daily window produced fewer visits than paying for them at any time ([Beshears et al.](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ormnsc/v67y2021i7p4139-4171.html)). Goal pursuit also rises at temporal landmarks such as the start of a week ([Dai, Milkman & Riis](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2014/06/Dai_Fresh_Start_2014_Mgmt_Sci.pdf)).

Aimtrue's existing weekly streak, three days played in seven, already has every one of those properties: a low bar per day, four built-in reserve days, flexible timing and a Monday fresh start. It sits in good company with Khan Academy's weekly streak and missions ([Khan Academy blog](https://blog.khanacademy.org/keeping-your-streak-alive-insights-tips-from-the-last-6-months/)), Mathletics' weekly points ([Mathletics](https://knowledgebase.mathletics.com/rewards-and-recognition/how-can-students-earn-points-in-mathletics)) and DreamBox's weekly lesson count ([DreamBox guide](https://info.discoveryeducation.com/rs/063-SDC-839/images/TeacherGuide-K-5-Math.pdf)). The redesign's job is to make it legible: seven day-dots with three targets, an explicit "one more day keeps your week" state (the at-risk state is the one thing Duolingo's widget research found worth showing ([Duolingo blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/widget-feature))), a "welcome back" after a missed week instead of a broken count, and partial progress shown as filling target rings on today's five questions, since effort accelerates as people near a visible finish line ([Kivetz, Urminsky & Zheng](https://business.columbia.edu/sites/default/files-efs/pubfiles/1200/goalgradient.pdf)). Prompting people to monitor progress raises goal attainment with an effect of d = 0.40 across 138 trials ([Harkin et al.](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/91437/)), which is the case for one self-referenced metric on every hat's band rather than a wall of statistics.

The mechanics to leave out are as well evidenced. Duolingo's leagues raised learning time 17% ([Lenny's Newsletter](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/how-duolingo-reignited-user-growth)), but Duolingo later had to change XP because climbing the board was not tracking learning ([Duolingo blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/time-spent-learning-well)), and in classroom studies low-ranked students lose motivation while top performers gain ([NSF-hosted study](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10510520)); Duolingo's child accounts reportedly have no leaderboards or friends ([Internet Matters](https://www.internetmatters.org/advice/apps-and-platforms/skills-building/duolingo)). Expected tangible rewards undermine intrinsic motivation across 128 experiments ([Deci, Koestner & Ryan](https://leeds-faculty.colorado.edu/dahe7472/deci%201999.pdf)), and a gamified course with badges, leaderboard and coins lowered motivation and exam scores ([Hanus & Fox](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compedu.2014.08.019)). About 80% of apps used by 3-5-year-olds carried at least one manipulative design feature ([Radesky et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9206186/)); the UK Children's Code forbids using children's data "in ways that have been shown to be detrimental to their wellbeing", with guidance naming reward loops, continuous play and notifications ([WSGR](https://www.wsgrdataadvisor.com/2020/01/update-uks-age-appropriate-design-code/)); and the FTC, while declining to require consent for engagement pushes, restated its concern about notifications that "keep kids online" ([Clark Hill](https://www.clarkhill.com/news-events/news/ftc-finalizes-long-awaited-coppa-rule-updates-to-strengthen-childrens-online-privacy-protections/)). The safe set for grades 1-8 is therefore: badges as unexpected, informational milestones; "beat your own best" and co-operative multiplayer instead of rankings; no chests, prizes or paid streak saves; no mascot guilt; and no direct pushes to children. Off-app prompts should go to adults: weekly one-sentence teacher-to-parent messages cut credit failure from 15.8% to 9.3%, with improvement-framed messages beating praise ([Brown Annenberg](https://annenberg.brown.edu/publications/underutilized-potential-teacher-parent-communication-evidence-field-experiment)), automated alerts cut course failures 27% though effects were weak in middle school ([J-PAL](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/node/2353)), and volume and complexity drive parents to opt out ([Fricke et al.](https://tipsbytext.stanford.edu/research/its-too-annoying-who-drops-out-educational-text-messaging-programs-and-why)). Because self-serve spaces send no email, an in-app "this week" card per child will carry most of this weight at first.

## Colour tiles by grade, and never by colour alone

The member tiles should borrow the guest home's achievement, coloured, iconed, human-titled cards, but map colour to a stable category rather than decoration. Colour coding speeds visual search over no coding ([Swan et al.](https://web.cse.msstate.edu/~swan/publications/b2hd-HFES93-win.html)), search time depends on colour *categories* that name differently rather than raw difference ([Optical Review](https://annex.jsap.or.jp/photonics/opticalreview/TOC-Lists/vol19/19d0276tx.htm)), and NN/g advises colours that "communicate the same kind of information everywhere in the interface" ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/videos/using-color-enhance-design/)). Grade is the right category: it is the word a family already uses, it bounds the set at eight, and it lets Beast Academy, AoPS and Kangaroo levels wear their grade's hue with an "Adv" mark, so a Grade 4 child finds every Grade 4 tile at a glance. Mode (quick five, timed dash, show your work) is then carried by icon and title, not a second hue, while progress belongs to a single-hue fill or stars, as in Beast Academy's "darker the blue, the stronger" reports ([Beast Academy Help](https://help.beastacademy.com/a/1793720-parent-reporting-overview)).

Accessibility constrains the palette hard. Up to 8% of men of Northern European descent have red-green colour-vision deficiency ([PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12385717/)), and dichromats took twice as long in a colour-cued search ([U. Minho](https://repositorium.sdum.uminho.pt/handle/1822/39759)). WCAG 1.4.1 requires that colour never be the only means of conveying information ([W3C](https://w3.org/WAI/WCAG21/Understanding/use-of-color)), so each tile also carries its level name, its code in small type and a mode icon. Eight hues is the ceiling of categorical palettes such as Okabe-Ito before shape or pattern must take over ([The Node](https://thenode.biologists.com/data-visualization-with-flying-colors/research/)), which fits grades 1-8 exactly; the hues should live as tokens with light and dark variants, be checked for 3:1 non-text and 4.5:1 text contrast, and be checked again so neighbouring grades stay apart under colour-blind simulation. No study tests colour-coded tiles with children specifically, so this is a reasoned transfer from adult search research, not a measured gain.

## Measure weeks, not days, and suppress small groups

Because the habit target is three days a week, daily ratios will look poor by design and should not lead. The useful frame is Duolingo's growth model, in which current-user retention (CURR) moved daily actives about **five times more** than the next-best lever ([Lenny's Newsletter](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/how-duolingo-reignited-user-growth)) and stood at 84% in Q2 2026, which Duolingo calls "one of our most important leading indicators" ([Duolingo Q2 2026 letter](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001562088/000162828026053299/q2fy26duolingo6-30x26share.htm)), translated to a weekly cadence, plus Andrew Chen's power-user curve, a histogram of days active that reveals what a single ratio hides ([a16z](https://a16z.com/the-power-user-curve-the-best-way-to-understand-your-most-engaged-users/)). Returning users matter too: more than half of Duolingo's top-of-funnel growth in one year came from users inactive for over a month ([Duolingo Q3 2024 letter](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1562088/000156208824000248/q3fy24duolingo09-30x24shar.htm)).

| Metric | What it answers | Reference point |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Weekly actives / monthly actives | Is the product part of most members' weeks? | Education apps' DAU/MAU median 12.6%, top decile 25.4% (vendor) ([mwm.ai](https://mwm.ai/glossary/dau-mau)); Duolingo ~42% DAU/MAU is the ceiling ([SEC](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001562088/000162828026053299/q2fy26duolingo6-30x26share.htm)) |
| Days played per week (L7 histogram) | Is the 3+ bucket growing? | Shape over level ([a16z](https://a16z.com/the-power-user-curve-the-best-way-to-understand-your-most-engaged-users/)) |
| Weekly CURR: met 3 days last week, again this week | Is the habit holding? | Duolingo's daily CURR 84% |
| Cohort retention at W1, W4, W8 by hat × space kind × arrival | Which homes work for whom? | Amplitude's 7% Day-7 bar (vendor) ([Amplitude](https://www.amplitude.com/blog/7-percent-retention-rule)) |
| Return within 7 days of a missed week | Does "welcome back" work? | Duolingo's resurrected-user share |
| Daily (Today's 5) completion | Is the smallest unit landing? | No public benchmark |
| Dosage: share reaching ~30 min a week | Is practice enough to matter? | Khan: 30+ min/week linked to ~20% greater-than-expected gains; only 9% reach it ([Khan Academy](https://blog.khanacademy.org/?p=18194)) |
| Tutor: lessons recorded within a day; reviews within N days | Is the staff loop healthy? | Internal baseline (22 recorded on 19 days) |

Two cautions apply. First, Aimtrue's population is tiny, so groups under five people should be suppressed in reporting, both for privacy and because percentages of three people mean nothing; for at least the next quarter the honest unit of analysis is individual journeys by cohort, not rates. Second, the visit record should be minimal (one row per person, space and day, holding the first client), rolled up on a schedule and purged with the space or account, and the privacy notice should be checked before it is switched on, since the Children's Code's data-minimisation and wellbeing standards apply to the measurement as much as to the mechanics ([Children's Code standards](https://edlaw.eduverse.com/docs/jurisdiction/nat/gb/childrens-code/STANDARDS.md)).

## Conclusion

The research reframes stickiness for Aimtrue as a routing problem before it is a motivation problem. The one cohort with a real habit, the tutor, already comes back several days a week because lessons create obligations; the home's job there is to remove taps, not add rewards, and the web's six-tap path to record a lesson is the single largest measurable drag in the product. For the cohorts with no usage yet, the market agrees on the shape (one obvious next action, a weekly forgiving goal, adults rather than children as the target of nudges) but offers almost no causal evidence that an intent-led home raises retention, especially in education. That makes the redesign an experiment as much as a fix: the fixed Next-up priority, the focus derived from arrival and activity, and the grade-coloured tiles are the treatment, and the weekly metrics with cohort splits are how Aimtrue will learn which of its own hypotheses are true.

One implication is easy to miss: the newcomer's continuity matters more than any streak mechanic. Duolingo's PM calls the first seven days the most fragile ([Lenny's Podcast](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/behind-the-product-duolingo-streaks)), and a guest who chose "Grade 4" and then lands among Beast Academy codes has been told, in effect, that the product they tried is not the one they joined. Keeping the guest's grade, tiles and visual language across sign-up is the cheapest retention lever available, and it is the one the current production data, with sign-up opened only today, has not yet had any chance to measure.
