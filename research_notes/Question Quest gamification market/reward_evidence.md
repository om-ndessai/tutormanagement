## 1. Meta-analyses of gamification's effect on learning and motivation: elements and moderators

### Takeaway
Every meta-analysis since 2019 finds a positive average effect of gamification on learning, roughly g = .46 to .50 in
the better-controlled syntheses and .8 to 1.1 in looser recent ones whose heterogeneity is extreme. None isolates
points, badges or streaks cleanly. The moderators that keep recurring are duration (short studies show the biggest
effects), design depth (points alone do less than points plus dynamics plus aesthetics), and level of schooling. Evidence
for children aged 8-14 in math specifically is thin: a few studies inside each meta-analysis, mostly secondary school.

### Cited Findings
**Core syntheses (2019-2020)**
- Sailer & Homner (Educational Psychology Review 32, 2020; online 2019): cognitive g = .49, motivational .36, behavioral
  .25; only the cognitive effect survived in high-rigor studies (figures and details in the earlier notes, section 5). —
  [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-019-09498-w). A later study cites Sailer & Homner as
  finding that intervention duration was NOT a significant moderator of behavioral outcomes. —
  [Rodrigues et al. 2022, Durham repository](https://durham-repository.worktribe.com/OutputFile/1203232)
- Bai, Hew & Huang (Educational Research Review 30, 100322, 2020): 30 interventions from 24 quantitative studies, gamified vs
  non-gamified; overall g = 0.504 (medium). The TYPE of game element, the NUMBER of elements and the level of
  research-design control did not change the effect significantly; sample size and intervention duration did. A companion
  qualitative synthesis of 32 studies found students valued enthusiasm, feedback, recognition and goal-setting, and
  disliked lack of utility and anxiety/jealousy. (Reference-page summary; ages not stated there.) —
  [UZH GBL reference page](https://www.gbl.uzh.ch/quartz/references/Bai-et-al.-(2020))
- Bai, Hew & Huang's earlier ICCE 2019 version (13 studies, information science, computing and engineering education)
  found a small effect, g = 0.36 [0.006, 0.714], p = .046. —
  [ICCE 2019 paper](https://v0.apsce.net/icce/icce2019/proceedings/paper_136.pdf)
- Huang, Ritzhaupt, Sommer, Zhu, Stephen, Valle, Hampton & Li (Educational Technology Research and Development 68,
  1875-1901, 2020): overall g = .464 [.244, .684]; 14 different design elements (e.g. leaderboards) were examined and
  "each produces different effects"; moderators tested included publication type, student classification (e.g.
  undergraduate) and subject area (e.g. mathematics). Element-by-element values were not retrieved. —
  [ECNU research listing](https://pure.ecnu.edu.cn/en/publications/the-impact-of-gamification-in-educational-settings-on-student-lea/)

**2022-2026 syntheses**
- Li, Ma & Shi (Frontiers in Psychology 14, 2023): 41 studies, 49 samples, >5,071 participants; g = 0.822
  [0.567, 1.078]. Moderators: elementary (k = 12) g = 1.293; the secondary and higher-education values (0.869 and
  0.014) are swapped between the paper's text and its Table 2, so which level shows near-zero is unclear; math (k = 3)
  g = 2.005 with a CI of 0.181 to 3.828; duration <1 week (k = 5) g = 1.874, 1 week-1 month 0.621, 1-3 months 0.519,
  3 months-1 semester 0.480, >1 semester (k = 5) 3.304; mechanics only (points, rewards, rankings) 0.533,
  mechanics + dynamics 0.997, mechanics + dynamics + aesthetics 1.285; fully online settings 0.340 (CI crosses zero).
  Single-study cells give implausible values (offline g = 35.2), a quality warning about the whole synthesis. —
  [Frontiers in Psychology 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1253549/full)
- Zeng, Sun, Looi & Fan (British Journal of Educational Technology, 2024, DOI 10.1111/bjet.13471): 22 experimental
  studies 2008-2023, gamified vs non-gamified classes; framed around the claim that students respond positively but the
  effect on achievement "is still a subject of debate" (pooled value not retrieved). —
  [CiteDrive record](https://www.citedrive.com/en/discovery/exploring-the-impact-of-gamification-on-students-academic-performance-a-comprehensive-metaanalysis-of-studies-from-the-year-2008-to-2023/)
- Ratinho, Figueiredo, Estêvão, Faísca & Martins (Educational Psychology Review 38:16, 2026): mathematics only,
  secondary and higher education; PRISMA 2020, preregistered, searches to November 2025; 45 studies in the qualitative
  synthesis, 11 in the meta-analysis; motivation g = .383 [.11, .66], p = .0218; engagement could not be pooled for lack
  of validated measures; only 4 of 45 studies reported negative effects; most interventions used Kahoot!, Classcraft and
  points, leaderboards and instant feedback. The authors advise limiting "excessive competition" and prioritizing
  "personal progress and cooperative tasks". — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-025-10108-1);
  [UAlg repository](https://sapientia.ualg.pt/entities/publication/e1c77e65-df9f-4a6f-9534-1f4fa9d686c6/full)
- Vega, Hidayanto & Muksar (Jurnal Pendidikan Matematika dan Sains, 2026): secondary-school math achievement, 16
  quasi-experimental studies 2015-2025, of which only 5 were gamification (the rest flipped classroom). Thin. —
  [JPMS](https://journal.uny.ac.id/index.php/jpms/article/view/96112)
- Bolat & Taş (Education and Information Technologies, 2023), gamified ASSESSMENT tools across subjects: larger effects
  on achievement in higher education than in K-12; the authors call for more K-12 experiments. —
  [Atatürk University record](https://avesis.atauni.edu.tr/yayin/dfe28532-a20a-486d-b5ec-d6948741f4ba/a-meta-analysis-on-the-effect-of-gamified-assessment-tools-on-academic-achievement-in-formal-educational-settings)
- A 2026 SAGE Open meta-analysis reports d = 1.12 with I² = 98.84% (covered in the earlier notes). —
  [SAGE Open 2026](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/21582440261421375)

### Inferences
- Treat "gamification works" as a small-to-medium average with huge spread. The realistic expectation for a
  points-and-badges layer on Question Quest is a modest gain that depends on design, not a guaranteed one.
- Points alone are the weakest form (Li et al.'s "mechanics only" g = .53 vs 1.29 with dynamics and aesthetics).
  Points that feed a visible goal (a level to clear, a progress card) are more likely to help than a bare counter.
- The biggest effects come from studies under a week long. Plan Phase 44 on the assumption that the novelty lift fades
  within about a month (section 7) and judge it on 8-12-week data, not the launch week.
- Very few studies in any meta-analysis are primary-school math. Aimtrue's own A/B data would be among the more
  relevant evidence for 8-14-year-olds.

### Gaps
- Sailer & Homner's moderator tables (game fiction, competition vs collaboration, duration) were again not readable
  (Springer redirected to login), so their element-level results remain unverified here.
- Huang et al.'s per-element effect sizes (points, badges, leaderboards) and Zeng et al.'s pooled value were not retrieved.
- K-12 math digital game-based learning meta-analyses (for example Byun & Joung 2018; Tokac, Novak & Thompson 2019)
  were not retrieved in this pass; no 2022-2026 meta-analysis restricted to primary or middle-school math gamification
  was found.

## 2. Rewards and intrinsic motivation: contingency, expectation, tangibility, and school incentive experiments

### Takeaway
In laboratory studies, expected tangible rewards reduce children's later free-choice interest, and they do so more than
for college students. The worst case is a performance reward where the person gets less than the maximum (d = −.88).
Unexpected rewards and rewards not tied to the task do no harm. Praise helps college students but did not measurably
raise children's free-choice interest. Field experiments in US schools add three points. Rewarding inputs works better
than rewarding outputs for children, who do not know how to turn effort into scores. A reward has to be immediate:
delayed by a month, it does nothing. A trophy can motivate grade 3-8 students as well as cash. For Question Quest, the
1-point effort reward is a classic input reward (good) that is gameable unless gated. The delayed correctness point
exerts almost no motivational pull at answering time, and can hurt as a visible "missed point".

### Cited Findings
**The meta-analytic core (laboratory, mostly single-session)**
- Deci, Koestner & Ryan (Psychological Bulletin 125(6), 627-668, 1999), 128 experiments. Free-choice intrinsic
  motivation (time spent on the task afterwards, unrewarded):
  - all tangible rewards d = −0.34 (92 studies); expected tangible d = −0.36; unexpected tangible d = 0.01 (9 studies,
    no effect; self-report 0.05); task-noncontingent rewards d = −0.14, not significant.
  - engagement-contingent (reward for doing the task) d = −0.40 (55 studies), children d = −0.43 after outliers (39
    studies) vs college d = −0.21 (12 studies), a significant age difference.
  - completion-contingent d = −0.36 (20 studies); performance-contingent d = −0.28 overall.
  - performance-contingent rewards where the person received LESS than the maximum, against a no-reward/no-feedback
    control: d = −0.88, "larger undermining ... than ... any other reward or reward-contingency group"; one study in which
    poor performers got nothing showed d = −0.95 (free choice) and −0.76 (interest).
  - all tangible rewards, children d = −0.39 (57 studies) vs college −0.27 (38).
  - verbal rewards (positive feedback): d = +0.33 overall on free choice, but children d = +0.11, not significant
    (7 studies), vs college +0.43; +0.31 on self-reported interest. Positive feedback given in a controlling way, or
    anticipated evaluation followed by praise (d = −0.40), undermines.
  - interesting vs dull tasks: tangible rewards undermined interest in interesting tasks (d = −0.68) but not in dull
    ones (d = +0.18, not significant).
  - The authors conclude rewards "must be used extraordinarily carefully with children in schools, homes, and elsewhere".
  — [Deci, Koestner & Ryan 1999 (full text)](https://depts.washington.edu/techdocs/papers/deciExtrinsicRewardsAndIntrinsicMotivation99.pdf)
- The counterpoint: Cameron & Pierce (Review of Educational Research, 1994) found all tangible rewards undermined free
  choice by only d = −0.21, with no effect on attitudes and none for unexpected or task-noncontingent rewards, and argued
  undermining was minimal and largely irrelevant to policy. Deci et al. reply that it combined interesting and dull
  tasks and miscoded contingencies. — [Deci et al. 1999, "Relation to previous meta-analyses"](https://depts.washington.edu/techdocs/papers/deciExtrinsicRewardsAndIntrinsicMotivation99.pdf).
  Cameron, Banko & Pierce (2001) restated the limited-harm view; Deci, Koestner & Ryan replied in Review of Educational
  Research (2001). — [Deci et al. search summary](https://pure.ewha.ac.kr/en/publications/a-meta-analytic-review-of-experiments-examining-the-effects-of-ex/);
  [Deci, Koestner & Ryan 2001](https://www.selfdeterminationtheory.org/SDT/documents/2001_DeciKoestnerRyan.pdf)
- Cerasoli, Nicklin & Ford (Psychological Bulletin 140(4), 980-1008, 2014), a 40-year meta-analysis of school, work and
  physical tasks (k = 183, N = 212,468; mostly adults): intrinsic motivation predicts performance (ρ = .21 to .45) even
  with incentives present; it is LESS predictive when incentives are directly tied to performance ("crowding out");
  intrinsic motivation predicts the QUALITY of performance and incentives its QUANTITY. —
  [oa.mg record](https://oa.mg/work/10.1037/a0035661); [Science for Work summary](https://scienceforwork.com/blog/incentives-motivation-performance/)
- Lepper, Greene & Nisbett (Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 28(1), 1973): about 51 preschoolers aged 3-4 who
  already liked drawing; those who EXPECTED a "Good Player" certificate with gold seal and ribbon later drew less in free
  time; unexpected-award and no-award children did not. (SEC; sample figures differ between summaries.) —
  [Psychology Notes HQ summary](https://www.psychologynoteshq.com/overjustification-effect/)
- Marinak (dissertation, 2004; published with Gambrell, 2008): 75 third-grade average readers randomized to book or token
  rewards, with or without choice, plus a no-reward control; reward TYPE mattered, choice did not. Students given a book
  (a reward close to the activity) were more motivated to read afterwards than those given a token. Small, single study. —
  [UMD DRUM record](https://drum.lib.umd.edu/items/73a62f89-4b5e-4aa2-b1cc-791638bee992);
  [Reading Rockets summary](https://www.readingrockets.org/article/reading-motivation-what-research-says)

**School incentive field experiments (2010-2016)**
- Fryer (Quarterly Journal of Economics 126(4), 1755-1798, 2011; NBER WP 15898, 2010). Randomized trials in 2007-08 and
  2008-09:
  - Dallas: 4,008 second graders in 42 schools, paid $2 per book (up to 20 a semester) IF they passed a comprehension
    quiz on it; average earnings $13.81 of a possible $80.
  - New York City: 16,449 fourth and seventh graders in 121 schools, paid for interim-assessment scores.
  - Chicago: 10,628 ninth graders in 40 schools, paid for grades.
  - Results: effects on state tests "statistically zero" in each city. The only significant gains were in Dallas
    (reading comprehension, vocabulary and language among English speakers), and they persisted after the program ended.
  - Fryer's reading: incentives for inputs work and incentives for outputs do not, because students "do not know the
    educational production function".
  — [J-PAL copy of QJE paper](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/sites/default/files/publications/919_Financialincentives_and_student_Fryer_2011.pdf);
  [NBER WP 15898](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w15898/w15898.pdf). A Brookings brief adds that students
  paid for outputs changed test-taking strategies instead of studying, and their enthusiasm faded when grades did not
  rise. — [Brookings brief](https://brookings.edu/wp-content/uploads/2016/06/092011_incentives_fryer_allen_brief2.pdf)
- Fryer & Holden (Houston, 50 schools): rewarding students, parents and teachers for math objectives mastered raised
  math and LOWERED reading achievement. Among low-baseline students there was no math gain and a negative reading
  effect, a multitasking cost of narrow rewards. — [Fryer & Holden "multitasking" paper](https://fryer.scholars.harvard.edu/resource/pdf-multitasking-dec-2013)
- Levitt, List, Neckermann & Sadoff (American Economic Journal: Economic Policy 8(4), 183-219, 2016): field experiments
  with over 5,700 elementary, middle and high school students in three districts around Chicago, rewarding improvement
  on a low-stakes test, with the reward announced just before it:
  - Large immediate rewards ($20 cash or a trophy costing about $3) raise scores about 0.1 SD. Pooled: financial
    high 0.093, financial loss-framed 0.153, trophy 0.044 (not significant), trophy loss-framed 0.115.
  - In Chicago Heights grades 3-8 (n = 363), the trophy gave +0.289 SD (SE 0.142) and the loss-framed trophy +0.267.
  - $10 had no effect; small financial rewards showed suggestive negative spillovers to other tests.
  - Rewards delivered one month later had no effect: "All motivating power of incentives vanishes when rewards are
    handed out with a delay."
  - Younger children respond more to nonfinancial rewards and older students mainly to loss-framed cash; effects were
    larger for boys and for math than reading; little effect on later tests. One CPS wave was null, unexplained.
  — [AEJ: Policy full text](https://rady.ucsd.edu/_files/faculty-research/sadoff/Levitt_et_al_Behavioralist_Goes_to_School_Nov_2016_AEJ_Policy.pdf);
  [NBER Digest](https://www.nber.org/digest/oct12/behavioralist-goes-school-leveraging-behavioral-economics-improve-educational)

### Inferences
- **Effort point (1 per answer).** This is an engagement/completion-contingent reward. In the lab that is the category
  most harmful to children's later interest (d ≈ −.4). But it is a symbolic point, not a tangible prize, and in Fryer's
  field data input rewards were the ones that worked. Keep it, but only as an INPUT WITH A QUALITY GATE, Dallas-style
  (books counted only when the quiz was passed): no point for a rapid guess (section 3). Keep it small and informational
  ("you practised 12 questions"), not a currency to grind.
- **Correctness point (1 more when right and approved).** This is a performance-contingent reward, delivered days later.
  Levitt et al. show that a one-month delay removes all motivating power, so the point will not raise effort at
  answering time. It works only as feedback when it arrives. And because a child gets 1 of 2 possible points on every
  miss, it reproduces Deci's "less than the maximum" case (d = −.88). Never show "points you missed"; show confirmed
  correct answers as good news, with the explanation (section 6).
- **Certificates and prizes (Phase 45).** Two findings point the same way: expected certificates undermined
  preschoolers' interest (Lepper 1973), and unexpected rewards are neutral (Deci 1999). So do not pre-announce
  "answer 50 to win a certificate". Award certificates for mastery milestones (level cleared), framed as recognition of
  competence, and let some arrive as surprises. A trophy-like non-cash reward can motivate grade 3-8 students (Levitt),
  but only when it is immediate and tied to an attainable improvement.
- **Reward proximity.** Rewards close to the activity (a book for reading) beat unrelated tokens (Marinak & Gambrell).
  For math, the shelf should favour math-related rewards: harder "challenge" sets, a puzzle, a level unlock, choosing the
  next topic, a certificate of a level cleared. Toy-style tokens are the weaker choice.
- **Multitasking.** Rewarding only Quest answers may pull effort from other practice (Fryer & Holden's reading drop).
  Report Quest activity beside, not instead of, the tutor's plan.
- **The task is probably "interesting" for many children, which is when undermining is strongest** (d = −.68 for interesting
  tasks). Children who already like math are the ones a heavy reward layer could hurt; offer them a "quiet mode"
  (progress, no confetti).

### Gaps
- No study was found that compares a point per answer with a point per correct answer for children in a learning app.
- No newer (2015-2026) meta-analysis of the undermining effect restricted to children was found. Cerasoli 2014 is mostly
  adults; the Lepper 1973 and Marinak details come from secondary summaries.
- No study was found of DELAYED, uncertain correctness rewards (a reward pending human confirmation) in any age group.

## 3. Gaming the system and rapid guessing: evidence, thresholds and countermeasures

### Takeaway
"Gaming", meaning progressing by exploiting the system rather than thinking, goes with poorer learning in Cognitive Tutor
math. It depends more on the lesson than on the student, so design can reduce it. Rapid guessing is detectable from
response time. The field convention is a per-item threshold of 10% of the item's normative (mean) time, often capped at
10 seconds (NT10), loosened to 20-30% in some work. Real-time responses work: a student-facing nudge cut disengaged
answers in a grade 8 RCT, and proctor alerts were followed by more time on items and higher scores. Effort-rewarded
systems invite click-through, but no study isolating per-attempt points as a cause was found.

### Cited Findings
**Gaming the system (Cognitive Tutor, ASSISTments; middle and high school math)**
- Gaming defined as attempting to succeed "by exploiting properties of the system rather than by learning the material".
  Gaming students showed significantly poorer learning (Baker, Corbett, Koedinger & Wagner, CHI 2004; CORR). —
  [Baker gaming page, CMU](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~rsbaker/gaming.html); [LearnLab wiki](https://learnlab.org/research/wiki/index.php/Baker_Choices_in_LE_Space)
- Harmful gaming happens on steps the student does NOT know. Gaming on known steps is not tied to poor learning, and
  students who game fall further behind over a tutor lesson. — [Baker gaming page](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~rsbaker/gaming.html);
  [Rodrigo et al., IEEE TAC](https://alls.ateneo.edu/wp-content/uploads/2012/04/ieee-tac-v28.pdf)
- Differences between LESSONS predict gaming better than differences between students. Gaming frequency was not
  correlated with a measure of performance goals (AIED 2005). — [Baker et al. AIED 2005](https://learninganalytics.upenn.edu/ryanbaker/BRCKAIED2005Final.pdf);
  [Baker gaming page](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~rsbaker/gaming.html)
- Countermeasure, "Scooter the Tutor" (Baker et al. 2006): an agent that shows displeasure when it detects gaming, and
  gives supplementary exercises on exactly the steps the student gamed. It reduced gaming, and the exercises
  significantly improved gaming students' learning. Students liked it, but it had little effect on their affect.
  Developer-run studies; effect sizes not retrieved. — [Baker gaming page](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~rsbaker/gaming.html);
  [Rodrigo et al.](https://alls.ateneo.edu/wp-content/uploads/2012/04/ieee-tac-v28.pdf);
  [LearnLab: Closing the Loop](https://learnlab.org/research/wiki/index.php/Baker_-_Closing_the_Loop)
- Walonoski & Heffernan (ITS 2006) built gaming detection and a prevention study in ASSISTments (about 70 students, 2004
  data); results not retrieved. — [Detection paper, Crossref](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1007%2F11774303_38);
  [Prevention paper, Crossref](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1007%2F11774303_80)

**Rapid-guessing thresholds (assessment research)**
- NT10 (Wise & Ma 2012): flag a response as a rapid guess if it is faster than 10% of the item's mean response time,
  commonly with a 10-second maximum. Response Time Effort (RTE) is the share of a student's responses that are
  "solution behavior". NT20 and NT30 are used where NT10 is too conservative. On a state assessment with more than
  80,000 students per grade, RTE was 0.991 in grade 3 and 0.980 in grade 8 under NT10. That study treated RTE < .90 as
  meaningful disengagement, a study-specific cutoff. —
  [Frontiers in Education 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2023.1127644/pdf);
  [aberrance R package, detect_rg](https://rdrr.io/cran/aberrance/man/detect_rg.html)
- Wise has called the 10% rule deliberately conservative, so that fast genuine solvers are not flagged. —
  [Hechinger Report](https://hechingerreport.org/researchers-can-detect-when-students-arent-exerting-effort-on-computerized-tests/)
- A theory-based alternative (Arslan & Finn 2023) set per-item minimum times from ACT-R cognitive models: 3.985 to
  54.241 s, mean 13.9 s, on NAEP-like grade 8 math items. A response counted as "not fully effortful" only if it was
  both INCORRECT and faster than the threshold. — [Journal of Intelligence 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10672233/)

**Real-time countermeasures**
- Arslan & Finn (Journal of Intelligence 11(11):204, 2023): RCT, 800 eighth graders in 8 schools across 7 US states, a
  low-stakes math test. A pop-up nudge ("your interaction with the item indicates that you may not have given your best
  effort") let students say whether they had tried and choose whether to go back.
  - Fewer not-fully-effortful responses than control (OR 1.44) and than an up-front effort pledge (OR 1.60).
  - The pledge did NOT help: those students were more disengaged than control.
  - No significant score difference.
  - 54% of nudge-arm students saw at least one nudge (mean 3.69); 55% agreed they had been nudged when they simply did
    not know the answer.
  — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10672233/)
- Wise et al. (2006), university students (as described by Arslan & Finn): warnings after three consecutive rapid
  guesses, at most two per test, raised RTE without significantly changing total scores. — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10672233/)
- Wise, Kuhfeld & Soland (2019), NWEA MAP Growth "proctor notification", more than 250,000 test events in 3 districts.
  QUASI: students who triggered alerts were compared with the prior year's students who would have.
  - After notification: engagement +.34 SD in reading, unchanged in math; time on items +.54 SD reading, +.25 SD math;
    performance +.38 SD reading, +.29 SD math.
  - Lower achievers are more likely to rapid-guess.
  - The authors read rapid guessers as opting out of items they CAN answer.
  — [NWEA research brief](https://www.nwea.org/uploads/2020/03/researchbrief-the-impact-of-proctor-notification-when-students-disengage-2019.pdf);
  [NWEA publication page](https://www.nwea.org/research/publication/the-effects-of-effort-monitoring-with-proctor-notification-on-test-taking-engagement-test-performance-and-validity/)

**Click-through under effort rewards**
- A Khan Academy community post describes children approaching gamified practice "like computer games", rushing to the
  end. One student finished a Year 4 course challenge in 15 minutes at 50% accuracy. Anecdotal. —
  [Khan Academy community](https://support.khanacademy.org/hc/es/community/posts/16042819700749-Guessing-on-Khan-Academy-and-improving-the-course-challenges)
- Fryer's Dallas design counted a book only once the child passed a comprehension quiz, a quality gate on an input
  reward. — [Fryer 2011](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/sites/default/files/publications/919_Financialincentives_and_student_Fryer_2011.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Gate the effort point on time.** Award it only when the response time is at or above a per-question floor. A
  defensible start is NT10, 10% of that question's median time across students, capped at 10 s, with a fixed 3-5 s
  floor until a question has enough responses. Move to NT20 for the gate if click-through persists. Keep the floor
  invisible: a child should not learn "wait 5 seconds, then tap".
- **Nudge, don't punish.** Because Quest has no going back, the right moment is BEFORE saving. If an answer comes in under
  the floor, show a one-tap check ("That was quick: want another look?"), as Arslan & Finn did, rather than silently
  docking points. Do not use an up-front "I promise to try" pledge; it made things worse.
- **Tell the tutor.** The NWEA result supports surfacing rapid-guess runs to the tutor (the "proctor") in the review
  queue, flagged, so the reviewer can talk to the student. Pair this with Scooter-style follow-up: a rapid-guessed
  question comes back later.
- **Keep two counters apart.** The tutor-facing record should separate effortful answers from rapid ones, because rapid
  guessing distorts the question bank's approval signal (a rapid wrong answer is not evidence the key is wrong).
- **Expect a lesson effect.** Gaming clusters by lesson (Baker). Monitor rapid-guess rates per level and question, and
  treat a spike as a sign the questions are too hard or too long for that level, not that the child is lazy.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study was found that isolates per-attempt points as a cause of rapid guessing in children's practice
  apps; the link rests on design logic and anecdote.
- Published rapid-guess thresholds come from TESTS. No threshold validated for self-paced PRACTICE by 8-14-year-olds was
  found, and the Arslan & Finn finding that 55% of students felt nudged when they did not know the answer shows the
  false-positive risk.
- Effect sizes for Scooter and the Walonoski & Heffernan prevention results were not retrieved. A 2024 LAK paper on
  on-demand hints for students who game (Vanacore, Gurung, Sales & Heffernan) was found as a lead only.

## 4. Streaks: habit formation, loss aversion, streak anxiety, freezes and repairs, children

### Takeaway
The first field RCT of streaks in education (Peru, grades 4-6, N = 60,000, 2022, working paper 2026) found that
highlighting WEEKLY streaks increased use among children who had already started, more than personalized reminders did.
It found no discouragement after breaks, and a suggestive learning gain. Personalized reminders were better at getting
non-users to start. Lab studies with adults show that SHOWING a broken streak reduces later engagement, and that letting
people repair a streak blunts the drop. Khan Academy retired streaks in 2021, citing demotivation when breaks are outside
the learner's control. For children, a weekly unit, forgiveness and never displaying a broken streak are the
evidence-based choices.

### Cited Findings
- Aulagnon, Cristia, Cueto & Malamud, "Streaks to Success?" (Northwestern IPR WP-26-05, draft of 19 February 2026, not
  peer-reviewed). Setting:
  - 60,000 grade 4-6 students in Peru who had used the Conecta Ideas math app the previous year, randomized to four
    arms: streak messages, personalized reminders with positive reinforcement, generic reminders, control.
  - 6-week treatment over the summer break (17 January to 27 February 2022). Each week brought a set of 30 exercises,
    and students saw right/wrong feedback after each exercise.
  - The streak unit was consecutive WEEKS of completed assignments. The streak message went out on Thursdays at 2 pm,
    on top of Monday's generic notice.
  - Baseline: only 5.3% of the control group used the platform even once.

  Results:
  - Connecting at least once: streak +2.8 pp, personalized reminder +3.8 pp, generic +1.4 pp.
  - Share of weeks connected among users: streak +9.4 pp, personalized 6.9 pp, generic 2.5 pp (control users connected
    in 31.7% of weeks). The streak advantage grew after week 1, "consistent with ... streaks becom[ing] relevant once
    students develop a habit".
  - 4-week streaks: 1.5% of the streak arm vs 0.5% of control.
  - No sign of discouragement: no longer gaps after a missed week.
  - Effects were concentrated on the days messages were sent.
  - Endline test: taken by only 2.3% (about 1,503 students), and more often in the streak arm. Streak arm +0.13 to
    0.17 SD vs control, not significantly different from the other arms; pooled +0.10 to 0.12.
  - Persistence beyond the following week was not measured.
  - The authors conclude streaks work best "to generate greater attachment among individuals who are already engaged";
    reminders suit jump-starting.
  — [IPR working paper](https://www.ipr.northwestern.edu/documents/working-papers/2026/wp-26-05.pdf)
- The same paper cites proprietary Duolingo tests: highlighting streaks gave +1% DAU and +3% Day-14 retention
  (Econsultancy 2023), and surfacing a "Streak Wager" store item gave +14% Day-7 retention (Duolingo Blog 2023) (CO). —
  [IPR working paper, fn. 6](https://www.ipr.northwestern.edu/documents/working-papers/2026/wp-26-05.pdf)
- Silverman & Barasch, "On or Off Track: How (Broken) Streaks Affect Consumer Decisions" (Journal of Consumer Research
  49(6), 1095-1117, April 2023), seven studies, adults, online language-learning and word/number games.
  - Highlighting INTACT streaks raises subsequent engagement relative to highlighting BROKEN ones. The effect depends
    on how the log represents behavior, not on the behavior itself.
  - It is amplified when people blame themselves for the break, and attenuated when a broken streak can be "repaired".
  - What counts as a streak is malleable: being able to keep one alive through either of two games motivated more than
    being limited to one.
  — [UDSpace](https://udspace.udel.edu/items/42ce576b-8e1f-429a-8541-e29e48dbcfbb);
  [CU Boulder Leeds summary](https://www.colorado.edu/business/faculty-research/2023/04/19/or-track-how-broken-streaks-affect-consumer-decisions);
  [UDaily 2024](https://udel.edu/udaily/2024/march/power-of-streaks-motivation-jackie-silverman)
- Khan Academy retired streaks on 4 January 2021. Reasons given:
  - over 99% of learners did not use them regularly, and the old code was hard to maintain;
  - learners and teachers reported that streaks "can actually be demotivating, especially when circumstances beyond
    one's control" (illness, no device or Wi-Fi) break them.
  Long streaks were converted into commemorative 50/100/200/500/1,000-day badges. A petition to restore streaks drew 63
  supporters when captured. — [Khan Academy announcement](https://support.khanacademy.org/hc/fr/community/posts/360075847492/comments/360013900252);
  [change.org petition](https://www.change.org/bringbackkastreaks)
- Emergency reserves as a model for streak freezes: Sharif & Shu, "Nudging persistence after failure through emergency
  reserves" (Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes, circa 2019 per the file name), one field and four lab
  studies with adults. Framing a goal with a limited "emergency reserve" of slack made people persist after missing a
  sub-goal and reach long-term goals more often than with equivalent goals without slack, via greater perceived
  progress. An earlier 5-week pedometer field experiment had about 240 participants. —
  [UCLA Anderson Review](https://anderson-review.ucla.edu/emergency-reserves/);
  [OBHDP manuscript](https://anderson-review.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/2021/03/Sharif-Shu_EmergencyReserveFailure_OBHDP2019.pdf);
  [Wharton field-experiment draft](https://marketing.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2016/10/Designing-More-Effective-Goals-by-Using-Emergency-Reserves-A-Field-Experiment.pdf)
- Duolingo's streak numbers (correlational 7- and 10-day effects, the goal/streak split test) and Brilliant's two banked
  "streak charges" are in the earlier notes, section 3 (CO). —
  [Mazal, Lenny's Newsletter 2023](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/how-duolingo-reignited-user-growth)

### Inferences
- **Count weeks, or days per week, not consecutive days.** The only child RCT used a weekly unit and found no harm from
  breaks. A daily chain for 8-14-year-olds runs straight into Khan Academy's problem: a sick day or a weekend with no
  device is outside the child's control. A "practised 3 days this week" streak fits tutoring weeks and keeps loss
  aversion low.
- **Build forgiveness in from day one.** Repair and reserves both soften the drop after a break (Silverman & Barasch;
  Sharif & Shu). Bank one or two freezes earned by practice (Brilliant's model, earlier notes), apply them automatically,
  and never charge for them.
- **Never display a broken streak.** After a break, show the longest streak and total practice instead ("best: 5 weeks"),
  and start a new one quietly.
- **Watch the bonus schedule (+2 at 3, +5 at 7, +10 at 30).** These are expected, engagement-contingent rewards, the
  category most undermining for children in the lab. And a large +10 at 30 makes a break at day 29 hurt. Smaller, flatter
  bonuses, or recognition (a badge) in place of points at the 30 mark, keep the stakes low.
- **The home nudge.** Streak messages deepen use among active children, while personalized reminders work better for
  lapsed ones (Aulagnon et al.). Show "one question keeps it going" only to children with a live streak, and use a
  plain, encouraging reminder for the rest. Never show a nudge at night, and send any reminder for children to the parent
  (earlier notes).
- **Expect day-of effects.** Effects bunched on message days, so a weekly rhythm matched to the tutoring schedule
  (for example, mid-week) is likely to beat daily pings.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study of DAILY streaks in children, of "streak anxiety" in children, or of streak freezes/repairs in
  children was found. Streak-anxiety evidence remains parent guides (earlier notes).
- The Peru study gave right/wrong feedback after each exercise. How streaks behave when, as in Quest, there is no
  correctness feedback is untested.
- Habit-formation timelines (for example the often-cited "66 days, range 18-254" from Lally et al. 2010) were seen only
  in blog summaries and are not used here.
- Research on adolescents and Snapchat "Snapstreaks" (peer-pressure streaks) was not retrieved.

## 5. Badges: do they act as goals, do meaningful badges beat trivial ones, do effects persist?

### Takeaway
Badges reliably raise the QUANTITY of the targeted activity, in a university RCT and a year-long marketplace comparison,
without lowering quality in the one study that checked. People work toward a badge as it gets close (goal-gradient).
Several small badges for the same action outperformed one big "super-badge" in a randomized MOOC study, and making
badges and progress salient raised engagement. The only middle-school math study found that effects depend on badge
type and prior knowledge. Evidence on learning, on persistence beyond a year and on children is thin.

### Cited Findings
- Denny, "The Effect of Virtual Achievements on Student Engagement" (CHI 2013), 1,031 university students using PeerWise
  (an MCQ authoring and answering tool). Badges had "a highly significant positive effect on the quantity of students'
  contributions, without a corresponding reduction in their quality", and on how long students engaged with the tool.
  (Read through a secondary report; randomization details not confirmed here.) —
  [Solve Education gamification report](https://solveeducation.org/wp-content/uploads/Gamification-Report.pdf).
  Denny's own framing, as cited elsewhere, is that badges "could trigger desirable behaviors if used by specific
  learners for specific purposes". — [ICCE paper citing Denny 2013](https://library.apsce.net/index.php/ICCE/article/download/3362/3238/4326)
- Hamari, "Do badges increase user activity? A field experiment on the effects of gamification" (Computers in Human
  Behavior 71, 469-478, 2017; online 2015): a peer-to-peer trading service (ShareTribe), adults. Design: one year
  pre-implementation (N = 1,410) vs one year post-implementation (N = 1,579), a between-period comparison, not
  randomized. Result: significant increases in trade listings, transactions, comments and total activity. —
  [University of Turku record](https://research.utu.fi/converis/portal/detail/Publication/27336812?lang=en_US);
  [Tampere gamification group summary](https://webpages.tuni.fi/gamification/2018/09/20/badges-increase-user-activity-a-field-experiment-on-the-effects-of-gamification/).
  A preliminary analysis (2013, about 3,234 users) found that merely adding badges did not raise activity; activity rose
  among users who monitored their own and others' badges. —
  [gamification-research.org 2013](https://gamification-research.org/2013/05/1-5-year-experiment-on-gamification-surprising-results/)
- Anderson, Huttenlocher, Kleinberg & Leskovec, "Steering User Behavior with Badges" (WWW 2013), Stack Overflow, adults,
  log data: badges steer the mix of activities as their model predicts, which treats badges as milestones for effort.
  The paper also studies where to place badges to induce particular behavior. —
  [WWW 2013 proceedings](https://archives.iw3c2.org/www2013/proceedings/p95.pdf);
  [Kleinberg talk slides](https://simons.berkeley.edu/sites/default/files/docs/4061/simons-nov15.pdf)
- Anderson et al., "Engaging with Massive Online Courses" (WWW 2014), a randomized experiment in a Coursera course
  forum: making badges more salient (badge counts beside authors' names; a pop-up after each badge saying how many more
  actions the next one needs) increased forum engagement. A suite of several less valuable badges for the same action
  worked better than one "super-badge" (latter point via a secondary summary). — [arXiv 1403.3100](https://arxiv.org/abs/1403.3100)
- Abramovich, Schunn & Higashi, "Are badges useful in education? It depends upon the type of badge and expertise of
  learner" (Educational Technology Research and Development 61, 217-232, 2013): middle-school students in an intelligent
  tutor for applied math; merit-style vs videogame-achievement-style badges. Badge-earning patterns differed by prior
  knowledge, and different badge types affected different learners' motivation. Designers "must consider the ability and
  motivations of learners" when choosing badges. (Abstract-level; full results not retrieved.) —
  [CMU RI record](https://ri.cmu.edu/?p=127319); [BibSonomy](https://www.bibsonomy.org/bibtex/b686114666e109a219e65eb449c873ff)
- Khan Academy badges (a local Khan Academy instance, 291 engineering freshmen, Universidad Carlos III de Madrid):
  students showed more intentional badge-chasing for REPETITIVE (easier) badges; no learning-outcome test. —
  [Ruipérez-Valiente et al. preprint](https://webs.um.es/jruiperez/papers/journals/2017_IJEE_Badges_preprint.pdf);
  [LAK 2016 intentionality paper](https://webs.um.es/jruiperez/papers/conferences/2016_LAK_Intentionality_postprint.pdf)
- In an RCT (adults, online simulation), badges, leaderboards and performance graphs raised competence-need
  satisfaction and perceived task meaningfulness (Sailer, Hense, Mayr & Mandl, Computers in Human Behavior 69, 2017). —
  [Augsburg OPUS](https://opus.bibliothek.uni-augsburg.de/opus4/frontdoor/index/index/docId/109059)

### Inferences
- **Badges as goals.** The goal-gradient and "how many more to the next badge" findings support showing progress toward
  the next badge on "My progress" ("7 of 10 confirmed"), which the Coursera RCT tested directly.
- **A ladder of small badges.** The 10/50/100/500 ladder fits Anderson et al.'s finding that several small badges beat
  one big one. A child's first rung should be reachable in the first session or two.
- **Weight badges toward mastery.** Abramovich's middle-school result and the Khan Academy observation (easy, repetitive
  badges get farmed) both favour mastery and competence badges ("level cleared", "first approval") over volume badges
  that reward grinding. Count CONFIRMED correct answers, as planned, rather than raw answers.
- **Badges will move quantity more than quality** (Cerasoli: incentives predict quantity). Pair every volume badge with
  the rapid-guess gate (section 3), so the badge cannot be earned by tapping.
- **Not every child cares.** Effects concentrated among users who looked at badges (Hamari, preliminary). Make the
  badge shelf visible but optional, and do not build core progress (levels) on it.

### Gaps
- No RCT of badges with children aged 8-14 measuring LEARNING was found. Abramovich et al.'s detailed findings (which
  badge type helped low vs high prior knowledge) were not retrieved.
- No evidence was found on whether badge effects persist beyond one year or fade after the first few are earned.
- A later critique of the Stack Overflow steering result (Hoernle et al., "Phantom Steering", arguing most users are
  apathetic toward badges) was seen only in a search summary and not verified.

## 6. Feedback timing: immediate vs delayed, and practice with no correctness feedback ("Saved")

### Takeaway
Bare right/wrong feedback adds almost nothing to learning by itself (ES .05). Giving the correct answer (.32) or an
explanation (.49) is what helps, and immediacy matters mostly for simple, procedural practice. In middle-school math
homework, immediate correctness feedback beat next-day feedback (ES ≈ .37-.40, small studies). With no feedback at all,
multiple-choice practice can teach the wrong options ("lure" intrusions); feedback, immediate or delayed, removes most of
that harm. For children with more prior knowledge, right/wrong feedback during exploration can hurt. For Question Quest,
"Saved" is defensible as a short interim state, but the delayed feedback must carry the correct answer and an
explanation, and misses should be revisited.

### Cited Findings
- Van der Kleij, Feskens & Eggen (Review of Educational Research 85(4), 475-511, 2015): 40 studies, 70 effect sizes,
  computer-based item feedback.
  - Elaborated feedback (an explanation) ES = 0.49; knowledge of correct response (the right answer) 0.32; knowledge
    of results (right/wrong only) 0.05.
  - Larger effects in mathematics. Effects were smaller with DELAYED feedback and in primary and high school.
  - Immediate feedback trended better for lower-order and delayed for higher-order outcomes (no significant
    interaction).
  — [ACU repository](https://acuresearchbank.acu.edu.au/item/86y84/effects-of-feedback-in-a-computer-based-learning-environment-on-students-learning-outcomes-a-meta-analysis)
- A later network meta-analysis (Leibniz-IPN) found right/wrong-only feedback less effective than the other feedback
  types for both lower- and higher-order learning. —
  [IPN publication](https://www.leibniz-ipn.de/en/research/publications/effects-of-computer-based-feedback-on-lower-and-higher-order-learning-outcomes)
- Kehrer, Kelly & Heffernan (FLAIRS 2013): crossover RCT, 65 seventh graders (61 analysed) at one Massachusetts middle
  school, ASSISTments homework. Immediate correctness feedback scored 67% vs 55% for next-day feedback, ES = 0.37
  [0.05, 0.77]. A separate counterbalanced eighth-grade study found ES = 0.40 for computer-supported homework vs
  next-day feedback in class. Small samples. —
  [Kelly & Heffernan slides/summary (UMD)](https://education.umd.edu/sites/default/files/uploads/1%20Kim%20Kelly%20and%20Neil%20Heffernan.pdf)
- Fyfe & Rittle-Johnson, "Feedback Both Helps and Hinders Learning: The Causal Role of Prior Knowledge" (Journal of
  Educational Psychology, 2016; online 2015): second and third graders (about 108) solving math-equivalence problems
  during exploration; feedback was the tutor saying right or wrong. Feedback improved procedural knowledge for
  lower-knowledge children, and NO feedback was better for higher-knowledge children (an expertise reversal). The
  authors suggest feedback drew higher-knowledge children's attention to performance and self-image (a hypothesis). —
  [Vanderbilt IR](https://ir.vanderbilt.edu/items/133cb4e3-42ab-4296-a618-23c30ed5040e);
  [Atlanta Journal-Constitution coverage](https://www.ajc.com/blog/get-schooled/new-study-feedback-can-undermine-learning-for-some-students/CZKstpTL0OCkYuCh3TSTYL/)
- Roediger & Marsh, "The positive and negative consequences of multiple-choice testing" (Journal of Experimental
  Psychology: Learning, Memory, and Cognition 31(5), 1155-1159, 2005), adults: MC testing produced a large testing
  effect, but the more lures participants read, the more of them reappeared as wrong answers on a later test, even under
  a no-guessing warning; MC tests "may inadvertently lead to the creation of false knowledge". —
  [WashU profile](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/the-positive-and-negative-consequences-of-multiple-choice-testing/);
  [Duke Scholars](https://scholars.duke.edu/publication/677292).
  Butler & Roediger (2008) compared immediate, delayed and no feedback and found feedback "enhances the positive effects
  and reduces the negative effects" of MC testing. Fazio, Agarwal, Marsh & Roediger (2010) found that more lures per
  question increased the harm. (Via an aggregator.) — [science.gov topic page](https://www.science.gov/topicpages/m/multiple+choice+test)
- Delayed feedback can help transfer even though students prefer immediate feedback (Mullet, Butler, Verdin, von Borries
  & Marsh, 2014, engineering students). —
  [WashU profile](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/delaying-feedback-promotes-transfer-of-knowledge-despite-student-/)
- Praise as a reward did not raise children's free-choice motivation (d = .11, not significant), and controlling praise
  undermines. — [Deci et al. 1999](https://depts.washington.edu/techdocs/papers/deciExtrinsicRewardsAndIntrinsicMotivation99.pdf)

### Inferences
- **What "Saved" costs.** Withholding right/wrong at answer time forgoes little learning directly (KR ≈ .05). The real
  costs are elsewhere: (a) uncorrected multiple-choice errors can be learned as "knowledge", (b) lower-knowledge children
  lose the most (Fyfe), and (c) the motivational loop of "I got it!" is gone, so the delayed correctness point never
  works as an immediate reward (section 2).
- **Make the review moment the feedback moment.** When the tutor approves, show the question again with the right answer
  and the explanation (KCR/EF, ES .32-.49), not just "+1". For a miss, show the correct answer and offer a similar
  question later, which also undoes lure learning.
- **Keep the delay short.** Feedback days later is still feedback, and delay may even aid transfer (Mullet et al.), but
  homework-style procedural practice for middle-schoolers did better with same-day feedback. Make tutor review within
  24-48 hours a target, and show "N answers waiting for your tutor" so the student knows feedback is coming.
- **A partial alternative.** Where the code's marking is reliable (most bank questions are machine-checked by
  `checkAnswer`), consider an immediate PROVISIONAL "looks right" for high-confidence items, marked as awaiting the
  tutor. That would recover the immediate-feedback benefit for low-prior-knowledge students. This is a product decision
  against the "key not yet confirmed" constraint, so it is flagged, not recommended outright.
- **"Saved" suits stronger students.** Fyfe's result suggests that for higher-knowledge children the lack of feedback may
  even help during exploratory problem solving.

### Gaps
- No study was found of children's MOTIVATION or persistence when practice gives no correctness feedback for days, nor
  of feedback that arrives only after human confirmation.
- Two larger online math-feedback RCTs surfaced in search (243 students in grades 10-11; 1,629 students in algebra) could
  not be matched reliably to their sources and are omitted.
- Whether Kehrer et al.'s ES is from the 61 or 65 students, and the full paper's details, were not verified.

## 7. Novelty decay, and what sustains engagement after it wears off

### Takeaway
Gamification's lift is largest in the first week and fades. In the one longitudinal study tracked week by week
(university, 14 weeks), the effect began falling after about 4 weeks, bottomed out over 2-6 weeks, and partly recovered
between weeks 6 and 10 as students familiarized themselves. A semester-long competitive design lowered motivation
(earlier notes). What is associated with lasting engagement fits self-determination theory: progress and competence
feedback (badges, performance graphs), personal progress over competition, cooperation, and streaks that deepen an
existing habit. Autonomy was the need that game elements did not move.

### Cited Findings
- Rodrigues et al., "Gamification suffers from the novelty effect but benefits from the familiarization effect: Findings
  from a longitudinal study" (International Journal of Educational Technology in Higher Education 19, 2022, DOI
  10.1186/s41239-021-00314-6): 14-week quasi-experiment, Brazilian STEM students in introductory programming (N = 756
  per the repository summary), gamified vs non-gamified versions of one system, behavior measured at seven points. The
  effect began to decrease after four weeks, the decline lasted two to six weeks, and an uptrend followed between six and
  ten weeks, a U-shape. University students; not randomized. — [DOI](https://www.doi.org/10.1186/s41239-021-00314-6);
  [Durham repository](https://durham-repository.worktribe.com/OutputFile/1203232)
- Duration in the meta-analyses:
  - Li et al. 2023: g = 1.874 under one week, falling to about .5-.6 for one week to a semester (the >1 semester cell
    is k = 5 and anomalous). — [Frontiers 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1253549/full)
  - Bai et al. 2020: effect sizes varied significantly with intervention duration. — [UZH reference](https://www.gbl.uzh.ch/quartz/references/Bai-et-al.-(2020))
  - Sailer & Homner: duration not a significant moderator of behavioral outcomes (as cited). — [Durham repository](https://durham-repository.worktribe.com/OutputFile/1203232)
- Outside education: a 2022 meta-analysis of gamified physical-activity interventions found a weaker but persisting very
  small to small effect at follow-ups averaging 14 weeks after the intervention ended (adults). —
  [JMIR 2022](https://www.jmir.org/2022/1/E26779/). A review of 12 gamified cybersecurity studies found most were
  short-term and measured within the novelty window. — [Informing Science](https://www.informingscience.org/Publications/5601)
- SDT mechanisms (Sailer et al. 2017, RCT, adults, online simulation): badges, leaderboards and performance graphs raised
  COMPETENCE satisfaction and task meaningfulness; avatars, a meaningful story and teammates raised RELATEDNESS; perceived
  decision freedom (AUTONOMY) "could not be affected as intended". —
  [Augsburg OPUS](https://opus.bibliothek.uni-augsburg.de/opus4/frontdoor/index/index/docId/109059)
- Intrinsic motivation predicts quality of performance and incentives predict quantity (Cerasoli et al. 2014, mostly
  adults). — [oa.mg](https://oa.mg/work/10.1037/a0035661)
- In math specifically, Ratinho et al. 2026 recommend "personal progress and cooperative tasks" over "excessive
  competition". — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-025-10108-1)
- Streak effects GREW over six weeks while reminder effects did not (habit formation), but persistence after the messages
  stopped was not measured. — [Aulagnon et al. 2026](https://www.ipr.northwestern.edu/documents/working-papers/2026/wp-26-05.pdf)
- Durable effects in school incentive trials: Fryer's Dallas reading gains persisted after payments stopped. —
  [Fryer 2011](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/sites/default/files/publications/919_Financialincentives_and_student_Fryer_2011.pdf).
  Levitt et al.'s immediate rewards had little effect on later, unrewarded tests. —
  [Levitt et al. 2016](https://rady.ucsd.edu/_files/faculty-research/sadoff/Levitt_et_al_Behavioralist_Goes_to_School_Nov_2016_AEJ_Policy.pdf)
- Feature-level decay: over 99% of Khan Academy learners did not use streaks regularly. —
  [Khan Academy](https://support.khanacademy.org/hc/fr/community/posts/360075847492/comments/360013900252)

### Inferences
- **Plan for the 4-10-week dip.** Badges and streak bonuses will look strongest at launch. Hold back some content (later
  badge rungs, Phase 45's shelf) to arrive around weeks 4-8, and evaluate on a 10-12-week window.
- **Lean on competence.** "My progress" should show mastery: levels cleared, topics where answers are confirmed right,
  and a trend line (performance graphs moved competence satisfaction). A point total alone does not do this.
- **Supply the autonomy that game elements did not.** Let the child choose a topic or "challenge vs practice" mode, and
  opt in or out of leaderboards and confetti.
- **Supply relatedness through the tutor and family.** Tutor review is a built-in social loop. A short tutor note
  ("nice work on fractions") and a parent-visible weekly summary are SDT-consistent and cooperative, not competitive.
- **Engineer the transition from extrinsic to intrinsic.** Rewards that inform ("you've mastered NC.5.NF.1") rather than
  control ("do 5 more for a bonus") are the ones the theory says survive novelty.

### Gaps
- No week-by-week novelty-decay study with children aged 8-14 in math practice was found. Rodrigues et al. is university
  and quasi-experimental.
- No study tested which SDT-aligned features (choice, tutor praise, progress graphs) sustain children's engagement after
  the novelty fades.

## 8. Leaderboards for children and lower performers (short)

### Takeaway
Covered in the earlier notes, section 5: leaderboards motivate those near the top and demotivate those near the bottom; a
semester-long competitive design lowered motivation and grades (Hanus & Fox 2015, university); competition produced
gender-dependent effects in a children's math game (Child Development 2023). New here: the 2026 math meta-analysis
advises limiting competition; students cite anxiety and jealousy as gamification's downside; and in the lab, the most
undermining reward is one that tells you that you got less than the best (d = −.88).

### Cited Findings
- Bottom-ranked students feel it is "not worth trying"; high rank raises and low rank lowers motivation. —
  [GamiFIN 2017](https://ceur-ws.org/Vol-1857/gamifin17_p7.pdf); Hanus & Fox 2015 —
  [Dichev & Dicheva 2017 review table](https://educationaltechnologyjournal.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41239-017-0042-5/tables/13);
  children's competition vs collaboration — [Child Development 94(5)](https://academic.oup.com/chidev/article/94/5/1239/8255182)
- Ratinho et al. 2026 (math): "limit excessive competition and prioritize personal progress and cooperative tasks". —
  [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-025-10108-1)
- Students' qualitative dislikes of gamification include anxiety and jealousy (Bai et al. 2020, 32 studies). —
  [UZH reference](https://www.gbl.uzh.ch/quartz/references/Bai-et-al.-(2020))
- Leaderboards raised competence-need satisfaction in an adult RCT (Sailer et al. 2017), which shows they are not uniformly
  harmful. — [Augsburg OPUS](https://opus.bibliothek.uni-augsburg.de/opus4/frontdoor/index/index/docId/109059)
- Performance-contingent rewards where the recipient gets less than the maximum: d = −.88 (the lab analogue of a low rank).
  — [Deci et al. 1999](https://depts.washington.edu/techdocs/papers/deciExtrinsicRewardsAndIntrinsicMotivation99.pdf)

### Inferences
- **The planned opt-in, first-name leaderboard should:**
  - rank within a small near-peer group (same level or same tutor's students), never the whole organization;
  - show only the child's own neighbourhood (the few names just above and below), never "27th of 30";
  - reset weekly;
  - rank by effortful, confirmed-correct answers, not raw volume, so it cannot be won by rapid guessing.
- **Offer a cooperative alternative first.** A shared class or tutor-group goal ("our group confirmed 200 this month")
  is the safer default for children and fits Ratinho's advice.
- **Expect the board to lag.** Confirmed-correct counts wait on tutor review, so the board will run behind. Show it as a
  weekly summary, not a live race.

### Gaps
- No controlled study comparing global, near-peer and opt-in leaderboards with 8-14-year-olds was found (as in the earlier
  notes), and none on first-name-only boards specifically.
