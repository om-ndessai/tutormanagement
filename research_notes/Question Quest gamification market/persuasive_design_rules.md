## 1. US federal: the amended COPPA Rule, FTC dark-patterns work and enforcement, KOSA and COPPA 2.0

### Takeaway
The amended COPPA Rule (in force 23 June 2025; full compliance since 22 April 2026) does **not** ban streaks, push notifications or other engagement prompts. The FTC dropped the 2024 proposal that would have stopped operators from using the "support for internal operations" exception to send engagement prompts without parental consent. It also declined to codify an edtech/school exception. What remains is: a disclosure duty when an operator relies on that exception; the old §312.7 rule against conditioning a child's participation in a game or prize on more data than necessary; and Section 5 unfairness and deception, which the FTC has used against children's in-game purchase flows (Epic 2022), loot-box odds and multi-tier currency (Genshin Impact, January 2025) and fabricated engagement messages (Sendit, September 2025, still in litigation). KOSA and COPPA 2.0 are **not law**. COPPA 2.0 passed the Senate in March 2026. KOSA cleared Senate Commerce in August 2026, but a unanimous-consent floor attempt was blocked before the election recess. The House instead passed a different bill (the KIDS Act) without KOSA's design "duty of care".

### Cited Findings
**What the final COPPA Rule did and did not do about engagement**
- The FTC did not adopt the proposed amendment that would have expressly barred using the support-for-internal-operations exception to justify engagement techniques such as push notifications. Law-firm accounts give the reasons as: overbreadth, possible limits on beneficial uses, and First Amendment concerns raised by commenters, which the FTC found "persuasive" [search summary] — [Jones Day, May 2025](https://jonesday.com/en/insights/2025/05/ftc-finalizes-amendments-to-coppa--rule); [Mondaq, FTC finalizes amendments](https://www.mondaq.com/privacy-protection/1579750/ftc-finalizes-amendments-to-coppa-rule)
- Instead, an operator relying on the exception must post an online notice. The notice must say which internal operations it collects persistent identifiers for, and how it ensures they are used only for those operations. High-level, "general, categorical terms" suffice [search summary] — [Mondaq](https://www.mondaq.com/privacy-protection/1579750/ftc-finalizes-amendments-to-coppa-rule); [Alston & Bird, Jan 2025](https://www.alston.com/en/insights/publications/2025/01/ftc-finalizes-amendments-coppa-rule)
- Commentators note that practices dropped from the final rule, "such as limitations on push notifications", can still be pursued under Section 5 of the FTC Act [search summary] — [Davis+Gilbert](https://www.dglaw.com/the-ftc-finalizes-amendments-to-the-coppa-rule/). The FTC said in January 2025 that it remained "concerned about the use of push notifications and other engagement techniques" (already in earlier notes) — [FTC press release, Jan 2025](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/01/ftc-finalizes-changes-childrens-privacy-rule-limiting-companies-ability-monetize-kids-data)
- **Edtech and schools, not codified.** The Statement of Basis and Purpose explains that the FTC held off "To avoid making amendments to the COPPA Rule that may conflict with potential amendments to DOE's FERPA regulations", and that "The Commission will continue to enforce COPPA in the ed tech context consistent with its existing guidance" (Part I.A) [primary] — [Federal Register, 90 FR, 22 Apr 2025 (GovInfo copy)](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2025-04-22/html/2025-05904.htm)
- **§312.7 (unchanged since 2013):** an operator must "Not condition a child's participation in a game, the offering of a prize, or another activity on the child disclosing more personal information than is reasonably necessary to participate in such activity" [primary; rule text reproduced in the FTC's Edmodo filing] — [FTC, Edmodo joint motion and exhibits (PDF)](https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/ftc_gov/pdf/2023129edmodojointmotionorder.pdf)
- The rest of the final rule's text (the Statement of Basis's discussion of engagement and of AI training as "not integral") was too long to read in full here. The non-integral treatment of AI training comes from secondary sources in the earlier notes.

**FTC dark-patterns report (2022)**
- *Bringing Dark Patterns to Light*, 15 Sep 2022. It groups dark patterns into four types: (1) misleading consumers and disguising ads; (2) making it hard to cancel subscriptions or charges; (3) burying key terms and junk fees; (4) tricking consumers into sharing data. It found dark patterns in, among other places, "children's apps" [primary] — [FTC press release](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2022/09/ftc-report-shows-rise-sophisticated-dark-patterns-designed-trick-trap-consumers). The press page says nothing specific about streaks, points or virtual currency.

**Enforcement on children's engagement, rewards and virtual currency**
- **Epic Games (Fortnite), dark-patterns order finalized March 2023 ($245M in refunds).** The FTC alleged that until 2018 Epic let children buy V-Bucks "by simply pressing buttons without requiring any parental or cardholder action or consent". The order bans dark patterns that obscure charges and requires express informed consent before billing [search summary of FTC page] — [FTC, Mar 2023](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2023/03/ftc-finalizes-order-requiring-fortnite-maker-epic-games-pay-245-million-tricking-users-making). About $72M had been paid to 629,344 consumers by 9 Dec 2024 [search summary] — [Nat'l Law Review](https://natlawreview.com/article/over-fortnite-epic-games-to-pay-record-us520-million-to-settle-ftc-claims-children-s)
- **Edmodo (May 2023; order filed with the court 22 May 2023).** This was the FTC's first action alleging it is unfair to make schools and teachers carry COPPA compliance for the vendor. The order bars using schools as intermediaries for parental consent unless the data is used only for an Educational Purpose. It forbids using children's data for advertising or building profiles and forbids conditioning participation on more data than necessary. It requires deletion within one year after a school contract ends, and deletion of models or algorithms built on unlawfully collected children's data. The $6M penalty was suspended for inability to pay [search summary of FTC pages] — [FTC press release](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2023/05/ftc-says-ed-tech-provider-edmodo-unlawfully-used-childrens-personal-information-advertising); [FTC business blog](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/blog/2023/05/oh-no-you-dont-edmodo-ftc-sues-ed-tech-company-violating-school-kids-privacy)
- **Edmodo order definitions [primary, from the stipulated order text]:** "'Educational Purpose' means any use related to a Child's education including, but not limited to, instruction in the classroom or at home, administrative activities, and collaboration between students, School personnel, or Parents. Educational Purpose does not include commercial purposes unrelated to the provision of the online service requested by the School such as advertising or building user profiles." "School Authorization" is valid only "on the condition that Personal Information is Collected only for an Educational Purpose" — [FTC, Edmodo proposed stipulated order (PDF)](https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/ftc_gov/pdf/2023129edmodojointmotionorder.pdf)
- **Cognosphere/HoYoverse (Genshin Impact), 17 Jan 2025, $20M.** The order: no loot-box sales to under-16s without a parent's affirmative express consent; no selling loot boxes for virtual currency unless they can also be bought directly with real money; disclose loot-box odds and the exchange rates of multi-tier virtual currency; do not misrepresent odds or prices. The complaint described real dollars exchanged into virtual currency, then re-exchanged several times, at "unusual denominations" [search summary of FTC release] — [FTC press release](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/01/genshin-impact-game-developer-will-be-banned-selling-lootboxes-teens-under-16-without-parental); [FTC business blog](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/blog/2025/01/level-tips-businesses-ftcs-settlement-genshin-impact-developer-hoyoverse). Then-Commissioner (now Chair) Ferguson concurred on the COPPA and odds counts but **dissented** on the counts about the real-money cost of prizes through the currency system. That signals the current FTC's reluctance to treat virtual-currency architecture alone as unlawful [search summary] — [Ferguson concurrence (PDF)](https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/ftc_gov/pdf/ferguson-cognosphere-concurrence.pdf). Whether the court entered the order was not confirmed (C.D. Cal. No. 25-cv-00447).
- **Sendit / Iconic Hearts (DOJ complaint on FTC referral, September 2025).** The complaint alleges COPPA violations: more than 116,000 users said they were under 13 in 2022. It alleges that "friend" messages were written by the company to push a paid "Diamond Membership", and that buyers got false or generic sender information. It is in litigation; a 2026 FTC speech cites the denial of the motion to dismiss [search summary] — [FTC press release, Sep 2025](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/09/ftc-alleges-sendit-app-its-ceo-unlawfully-collected-personal-data-children-deceived-users-about); [FTC BCP Director remarks, 12 Mar 2026 (PDF)](https://search.ftc.gov/system/files/ftc_gov/pdf/2026-3-12-Mufarrige-Remarks-for-Venable-Ad-Law-Symposium.pdf)
- **FTC agenda signals.** The FTC held a workshop on 4 June 2025, "The Attention Economy: How Big Tech Firms Exploit Children and Hurt Families", on "addictive design features" and parental authority. Chairman Ferguson's keynote argued parents should decide whether children may hold accounts on social media, online gaming and streaming services [search summary] — [FTC event page](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/events/2025/06/attention-economy-tech-firms-exploit-children); [Ferguson keynote (PDF)](https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/ftc_gov/pdf/andrew-n-ferguson-keynote-attention-economy-06-04-25.pdf). ### Inferences
- Federal law today does not forbid streaks, points, badges or reminders for children. The federal risk is (a) COPPA notice and consent: what the notice says each use of the child's data is for, including push tokens, which are persistent identifiers; and (b) Section 5: a nudge that is false (fake urgency, invented social proof, an untrue "your friends are ahead of you") or a purchase flow a child can complete alone. Sendit shows that engagement copy which misleads children is a live enforcement theory.
- Because tutoring organizations are not schools, the school-consent route does not apply; the earlier notes reached the same conclusion. Whatever parental consent the platform obtains should name Question Quest explicitly: answers, points, any leaderboard display, and reminders. Then nothing relies on the internal-operations exception, and the dropped push-notification proposal stops mattering.
- If KOSA's duty of care becomes law, it would reach "design features" (the Senate text's subject); the House KIDS Act would not. Neither is close to enactment before 2027.

## 2. US states: design codes, addictive-feed laws and app-store accountability acts

### Takeaway
Only one state law found so far bans badges, points and streaks by name: **Vermont's proposed rules** under its Age-Appropriate Design Code (statute effective 1 Jan 2027; comments closed 2 Oct 2026; final rules due by 1 Jan 2027). They would bar "variable or intermittent rewards (e.g., likes, badges, points)", "social pressure features" such as "streaks" and "popularity indicators", and engagement notifications. **Nebraska's** code (operative 1 Jan 2026; penalties from 1 July 2026) lists "rewards or incentives for frequency of visits or time spent" and "notifications or push alerts" as covered design features. It bans notifications to known minors from 10pm to 6am and from 8am to 4pm on school-year weekdays. But it reaches only very large data businesses: all three thresholds are joined by "and". New York's SAFE for Kids Act (rules final 28 July 2026; effective 25 Jan 2027) and California's SB 976 reach only "addictive feed" platforms. Maryland's code is in force and unenjoined, pending a NetChoice suit. California's AADC is partly enforceable since March 2026, but its dark-patterns ban is still enjoined. The app-store acts: Texas is enforceable pending appeal since 4 June 2026; Utah's key duties start 6 May 2027; Louisiana was delayed to 1 July 2027. California's AB 1043 (OS-level age signals, developer duties) starts 1 Jan 2027. No North Carolina design code or app-store act was found.

### Cited Findings
**Nebraska, Age-Appropriate Online Design Code Act (LB 504, 2025) [primary, slip law text]** — [LB504 slip law (PDF)](https://www.nebraskalegislature.gov/FloorDocs/109/PDF/Slip/LB504.pdf)
- Sec. 2(3): "Covered design feature means any feature or component of a covered online service that will encourage or increase the frequency, time spent, or activity of a user", including "(a) Infinite scroll; (b) Rewards or incentives for frequency of visits or time spent on the covered online service; (c) Notifications or push alerts; (d) In-game purchases; or (e) Appearance-altering filters".
- Sec. 2(5)(a): a covered online service must (iii) have annual gross revenue over $25M, **and** (iv) annually buy, receive, sell or share the personal data of 50,000 or more consumers, **and** (v) derive "at least fifty percent of its annual revenue from the sale or sharing of consumers' personal data". Sec. 2(5)(c) excludes a service that knows fewer than 2% of its users are minors.
- Sec. 4: covered minors must get tools to opt out of "all unnecessary covered design features" and to limit time spent, with defaults "at the option or level that provides the highest protection".
- Sec. 5(6): "The use of notifications and push alerts to a covered minor is prohibited between the hours of 10 p.m. and 6 a.m. and between the hours of 8 a.m. and 4 p.m. on week days during the school year in the covered minor's local time zone."
- Sec. 6: parental tools are on by default for a known child, including restricting use "during school hours and at night". Sec. 8(2): no dark patterns, defined as including any practice the FTC had deemed a dark pattern as of 1 Jan 2024. Sec. 9: no penalty actions before 1 July 2026; up to $50,000 per violation after that. Sec. 10: operative 1 Jan 2026.
- A secondary source mentions an amendment, "LB 838 (2026)"; it was not verified — [privacylawmap.com](https://privacylawmap.com/states/nebraska)

**Vermont, Age-Appropriate Design Code Act (Act 63, S.69, signed 12 June 2025; effective 1 Jan 2027)** [secondary] — [Mayer Brown, Sept 2026](https://www.mayerbrown.com/en/insights/publications/2026/09/vermonts-proposed-aadc-rules-would-force-a-redesign-of-how-online-services-present-content-to-minors-reaching-far-beyond-social-media-companies)
- Coverage: a business that does business in Vermont, earns most of its annual revenue from online services, offers features "reasonably likely to be accessed by a minor", collects personal data, and controls how it is used. The "reasonably likely" test is generally met when minors aged 2-17 are 2% or more of the audience, or when the service is child-directed under COPPA. The summary mentions no education exemption.
- The statute imposes a duty of care: the design and data use must not cause reasonably foreseeable emotional distress, "compulsive use" (repetitive use that materially disrupts major life activities such as sleep or learning) or discrimination. It bans push notifications to covered minors **from midnight to 6am**, with suppression as the default.
- **Proposed AG rules:** they would bar any notification feature "designed or reasonably likely to encourage, increase, prolong, or reinitiate engagement" based on personal, behavioural or inferred data. Transactional, safety and account notices are allowed. They would also bar "variable or intermittent rewards (e.g., likes, badges, points)", "social pressure features" including "streaks" and "popularity indicators", autoplay, infinite scroll (a discrete "Load More" action is required instead), and interfaces that make continued engagement "substantially easier than disengagement". Per the summary, parents cannot consent around these rules. Comments closed 2 Oct 2026; final rules are due on or before 1 Jan 2027.
- No final rules and no NetChoice suit against Vermont were found as of early October 2026 [search summary] — [Captain Compliance](https://captaincompliance.com/news/vermont-moves-forward-with-age-appropriate-design-rules-for-online-services/); [Mondaq](https://www.mondaq.com/vermonts-proposed-aadc-rules-could-force-a-redesign-of-how-online-services-present-content-to-minors-reaching-beyond-social-media-companies/1849942)

**California**
- **AADC.** After the Ninth Circuit's 12 Mar 2026 decision, the data protection impact assessment duties, the dark-patterns prohibition and the "materially detrimental" use prohibition are still enjoined. - **SB 976 (Protecting Our Kids from Social Media Addiction Act).** On 9 Sep 2025 the Ninth Circuit upheld denial of a preliminary injunction for the addictive-feed parental-consent and default-private provisions. It held that the like-count restriction fails strict scrutiny. Rehearing en banc was denied in November 2025 [secondary] — [Hunton](https://www.hunton.com/privacy-and-cybersecurity-law-blog/ninth-circuit-upholds-addictive-social-media-feed-ban-and-default-privacy-settings-for-minors-in-californias-protecting-our-kids-from-social-media-addiction-act); [Crowell](https://www.crowell.com/en/insights/client-alerts/netchoice-llc-v-bonta-what-the-ninth-circuits-ruling-could-mean-for-online-speech-regulation); [order denying rehearing, 6 Nov 2025 (PDF)](https://docs.reclaimthenet.org/NetChoice-v-Bonta-SB976-2025-11-06.pdf). - **AB 1043 (Digital Age Assurance Act).** Signed 13 Oct 2025; operative **1 Jan 2027** for new accounts, with operating systems having until 1 July 2027 for existing accounts. Operating systems collect an age at device setup and send age-band signals. Developers must request and apply those signals when an app is downloaded and launched, treat them as the primary indicator of age, and not reuse them. A developer is deemed to have "actual knowledge" of the age range once it receives a signal. The AG may seek up to $2,500 per affected child (negligent) or $7,500 (intentional) [secondary] — [Kelley Drye FAQ](https://www.kelleydrye.com/viewpoints/blogs/ad-law-access/faq-digital-age-assurance-act-california-youth-safety-on-the-internet); [Hunton](https://www.hunton.com/privacy-and-cybersecurity-law-blog/california-introduces-new-age-verification-requirements-for-software-applications)

**New York**
- **SAFE for Kids Act.** The AG's final rules were released 28 July 2026 and published in the State Register 29 July 2026 (13 NYCRR Part 700). The Act takes effect **25 Jan 2027**. ### Inferences
- **Starting in North Carolina, no state design code appears to apply today.** Nebraska's conjunctive thresholds (more than $25M in revenue and 50,000+ consumers' data and at least 50% of revenue from selling data) exclude a small SaaS company. SAFE for Kids and SB 976 need an "addictive feed" of user-generated media, which a question bank is not. Vermont is the one to watch: it has no revenue threshold in the summary, and a SaaS business "earns most of its annual revenue from online services". Its proposed rules name badges, points and streaks directly. If any organization enrols Vermont children from 1 Jan 2027 (2% audience test), Phase 44 as planned (streak bonuses, badges, a re-engagement nudge) could conflict with the final rules. Maryland and California AADC coverage was not checked.
- **The reading most likely to survive is narrow.** Vermont's ban is on *variable or intermittent* rewards and *social-pressure* features. Fixed, predictable points for a stated action (one point per attempt, one per correct answer, as `QUEST_POINTS` does) are closer to a grade than a slot machine. Streaks are named outright. Treat streaks as the highest-risk Phase 44 mechanic, and badges as lower risk when they are fixed, earned for learning milestones and announced in advance.
- **Notification quiet hours are converging:** midnight-6am (Vermont statute; NY "nighttime"), 10pm-6am plus 8am-4pm on school-year weekdays (Nebraska). Using the organization's `time_zone` to suppress student-facing reminders from 10pm to 6am, and in school hours on weekdays, meets every window found here.
- **App-store laws:** a student who downloads the mobile app in Texas triggers SB 2420 developer duties now; Utah follows in May 2027 and Louisiana in July 2027. California's AB 1043 applies to every developer whose app is downloaded in California from 1 Jan 2027. Adding streaks, rewards or a leaderboard may count as a "significant change" requiring renewed parental approval through the stores, though this was not verified (see Gaps).

### Gaps
- No North Carolina children's design code, addictive-feed law or app-store act was found, but the 2025-2026 NC legislative sessions were not searched directly.
- Maryland Kids Code coverage thresholds and wording on "compulsive use" or notifications were not retrieved.
- The text of Vermont's statute and proposed rules was not read directly (Mayer Brown summary only), nor any education exemption. Whether final rules were adopted in October 2026 is unknown.
- How Texas SB 2420 and Louisiana define a "significant change" requiring renewed parental consent was not retrieved, so whether gamification triggers it is unknown.
- SB 976's notification provision (hours and status after the Ninth Circuit) is unverified.

## 3. Student-privacy laws: SOPIPA-style rules, North Carolina, using students' answers, FERPA and leaderboards

### Takeaway
North Carolina's student online privacy statute (G.S. 115C-401.2, 2016; amended 2025) copies SOPIPA. It binds only an operator whose service is "designed and marketed for K-12 school purposes" and is known to be "used primarily" for them, meaning purposes "directed by or that customarily take place at the direction of a K-12 school". A platform sold to private tutoring organizations is probably outside it. Where it does apply, it treats material "created by or provided to an operator by a student" as covered information. It allows using and sharing student information "not associated with an identified student" to improve educational products. So the shared question bank is safest if an approved question carries no link to any student's identity or answer. FERPA binds only schools and agencies that receive Department of Education funds (34 CFR 99.1), so it does not reach private tutoring organizations. Where it does apply, posting students' scores by name to classmates is treated by universities as a disclosure of education records. Directory information lists "honors and awards received" but not grades.

### Cited Findings
**North Carolina G.S. 115C-401.2 [primary]** — [NC General Assembly, § 115C-401.2](https://www.ncleg.gov/EnactedLegislation/Statutes/HTML/BySection/Chapter_115C/GS_115C-401.2.html)
- (a)(6) Operator: an operator of a website, service or app "with actual knowledge that the site, service, or application is used primarily for K-12 school purposes" "and was designed and marketed for K-12 school purposes". A K-12 school or local board is not an operator.
- (a)(4) K-12 school purposes: "Purposes that are directed by or that customarily take place at the direction of a K-12 school" "or aid in the administration of school activities, including, but not limited to, instruction in the classroom or at home".
- (a)(1) Covered information includes material "Created by or provided to an operator by a student, or the student's parent or legal guardian" in the course of use for K-12 school purposes, and personally identifying information gathered through the service, such as grades and test results.
- (b) The operator shall not knowingly: engage in targeted advertising; amass a profile "except in furtherance of K-12 school purposes"; sell or rent student information; or disclose covered information except (a) "In furtherance of the K-12 school purpose of the site", (b) legal compliance, (c) judicial process, (d) safety and security, (e) to a third party for a purpose "requested by the student" or parent, or (f) to a bound subcontractor.
- (e) Permitted: (1) using covered information "that is not associated with an identified student" to "improve educational products"; (2) using it to demonstrate effectiveness; (3) sharing non-identified information "for the development and improvement of educational sites, services, or applications"; (4)-(5) educational recommendations and responding to a student's request for feedback. (f)(2) The section does not limit "adaptive learning".
- (c) Operators must keep reasonable security and delete within 45 days on the school's request. (g) Enforcement is by the AG (injunctive and equitable relief only); "Nothing in this section shall be construed to create a private right of action."
- California's SOPIPA has the same structure (earlier notes); its statute page returned 403 here.

**Using children's work under COPPA and the Edmodo definitions**
- Edmodo's "Educational Purpose" excludes "commercial purposes unrelated to the provision of the online service requested by the School such as advertising or building user profiles" [primary] — [FTC, Edmodo stipulated order (PDF)](https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/ftc_gov/pdf/2023129edmodojointmotionorder.pdf)
- The COPPA FAQ (school authorization, N.1-N.2) says any use for the operator's "own commercial purposes in addition to the provision of services to the school" needs parental consent (earlier notes) — [FTC COPPA FAQ](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/complying-coppa-frequently-asked-questions)
- Edmodo's order also required deleting models or algorithms built in whole or part on children's data collected without consent [search summary of FTC release] — [FTC press release](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2023/05/ftc-says-ed-tech-provider-edmodo-unlawfully-used-childrens-personal-information-advertising)

**FERPA**
- 34 CFR 99.1(a): FERPA "applies to an educational agency or institution to which funds have been made available" under "any program administered by the Secretary". 99.1(b): it does not apply "solely because" students receive non-monetary benefits, if "no funds under that program are made available to the agency or institution" [primary] — [34 CFR 99.1 (Cornell LII)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/34/99.1)
- 34 CFR 99.3: "Education records" are records "Directly related to a student" and "Maintained by an educational agency or institution or by a party acting for the agency or institution". The exclusions include grades on peer-graded papers before a teacher collects and records them. "Directory information" (information "not generally considered harmful or an invasion of privacy if disclosed") includes name, grade level, "participation in officially recognized activities and sports" and "degrees, honors, and awards received". It does not list grades or scores [primary] — [34 CFR 99.3 (Cornell LII)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/34/99.3)
- University FERPA guidance: "public dissemination of information about students' grades, be it electronically, by distribution of grade sheets, or even by informal announcement in class or elsewhere is a disclosure of private student records" [secondary] — [CSUN](https://www.csun.edu/studentaffairs/public-dissemination-student-grades). Posting grades "in a way that exposes individual grades to other class members is not acceptable" [secondary] — [Washington State Univ. FERPA FAQ](https://ferpa.em.wsu.edu/faqs/staff-faq). Obscured names and partial IDs are still treated as identifiable [secondary] — [Case Western policy (2025)](https://case.edu/provost/policies/public-posting-grades-policy). (No Department of Education page on leaderboards was found.)
- A vendor gets education records as a "school official" only under the school's direct control (earlier notes) — [FPF](https://fpf.org/blog/who-exactly-is-a-school-official-anyway/)

### Inferences
- **For private tutoring organizations, FERPA and NC 115C-401.2 most likely do not apply.** Such an organization does not receive Department of Education funds. Its lessons are not "directed by" a K-12 school, and the platform is designed and marketed for tutoring organizations. That changes if Aimtrue sells to a school or district, or markets itself "for K-12 schools". Then both laws attach, and the earlier notes' COPPA school-authorization limits (educational, non-commercial use only) apply as well. COPPA itself applies either way for under-13s.
- **Using students' answers to approve questions for every organization's bank.** Under NC (e)(1) and (e)(3) and the SOPIPA pattern, improving the operator's own educational product with de-identified information is expressly allowed; doing it with identified information is not listed. Under the Edmodo definition, checking the questions that the same service uses is arguably "related to the provision of the online service requested". But the benefit flows to other customers, so this is a grey zone and not clearly educational. Prudent design:
  - Keep `quest_attempts` (who answered what) inside the student's organization.
  - Let only the tutor's review, not the child's answer or identity, travel with an approved question. Tests to apply: no student id on the `questions` row, no answer text copied into the bank, and no "approved via student X".
  - Say it plainly in the parent-facing notice and consent: "your child's answers help their tutor check new practice questions; checked questions join a bank all organizations use; your child's name and answers are not shared".
  - Give guardians an opt-out of Quest. The organization switch already defaults off.
  - Cover `quest_attempts` and `points_ledger` in the COPPA written retention schedule, since the amended Rule prohibits indefinite retention.
- **The append-only ledger and retention.** COPPA's deletion and retention duties (the parent's right to delete, no indefinite retention) apply to an append-only `points_ledger`. A trigger that refuses UPDATE is fine, but deleting a child's rows on a guardian's request or account deletion must remain possible.
- **Leaderboards and FERPA (school customers only).** Points are performance data, closer to grades than to "honors and awards". A school customer could not treat a named points table as directory information without its own designation and parental notice. Even then, university practice treats score lists visible to classmates as disclosures. Private tutoring customers are outside FERPA, but the same norm is what parents and schools will expect.

### Gaps
- No case, AG opinion or FTC statement found that addresses using children's answers to improve or validate content sold to other customers, sometimes framed as "crowdsourcing children's work". The analysis above is inference.
- North Carolina has other student-data rules not checked here (for example, State Board of Education data-sharing policies, or whether NC DPI publishes vendor guidance on gamification).
- The California SOPIPA text was not readable (403), so the claim that it matches NC word for word is unverified.
- No Department of Education (Student Privacy Policy Office) guidance on classroom leaderboards or points was found.

## 4. UK and EU, in brief, for an eventual expansion

### Takeaway
UK: the Children's Code does not mention streaks by name. Standard 5 (detrimental use) names "reward loops, continuous scrolling, notifications and auto-play" as "sticky" features. It says to avoid using personal data to "incentivise children to stay engaged" and to give pause buttons that keep progress. The ICO's 2023 games guidance adds break prompts "without feeling pressurised… or becoming fearful of missing out" and early risk assessment of randomised rewards. Standard 13 covers nudges against privacy, not engagement. Ofcom's Protection of Children Codes (in force July 2025) are about harmful content and contact, not streaks. Separately, the UK government announced on 15 June 2026 a ban on social media services for under-16s. Regulations are due to go before Parliament by the end of 2026, under a power in the Children's Wellbeing and Schools Act. The consultation named "streaks" and "infinite scrolling". EU: the DSA Article 28 guidelines (final 14 July 2025; non-binding; micro and small enterprises exempt) want streaks, autoplay and night-time push notifications off by default. They also want no infinite scroll or urgency cues, and no "tokens or loot boxes" that obscure real-world value. The CPC consumer network's principles on in-game virtual currencies (21 March 2025) require real-money price transparency. The Digital Fairness Act proposal, covering addictive design and virtual currencies, is expected from the Commission in Q4 2026 and had not been tabled as of the latest sources.

### Cited Findings
**UK ICO Children's Code**
- Standard 5: "Do not use children's personal data in ways that have been shown to be detrimental to their wellbeing". "Strategies used to extend user engagement, sometimes referred to as 'sticky' features can include mechanisms such as reward loops, continuous scrolling, notifications and auto-play features". Designers should "avoid using personal data in a way that incentivises children to stay engaged", "present options to continue playing or otherwise engaging with your service neutrally", "avoid features which use personal data to automatically extend use instead of requiring children to make an active choice", and "introduce mechanisms such as pause buttons which allow children to take a break at any time without losing their progress" [primary] — [ICO, Standard 5](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/childrens-information/childrens-code-guidance-and-resources/age-appropriate-design-a-code-of-practice-for-online-services/5-detrimental-use-of-data/)
- Standard 13 (nudge techniques) concerns nudges "to provide unnecessary personal data" or "turn off privacy protections". It encourages "nudging them towards supportive resources or providing tools such as pause and save buttons", and for ages 0-9, "Nudge towards wellbeing enhancing behaviours (such as taking breaks)" [primary] — [ICO, Standard 13](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/childrens-information/childrens-code-guidance-and-resources/age-appropriate-design-a-code-of-practice-for-online-services/13-nudge-techniques/)
- ICO games guidance (February 2023): use checkpoints and age-appropriate prompts so players can disengage "without feeling pressurised to continue playing or becoming fearful of missing out". Randomised rewards such as loot boxes "should be risk assessed against the Children's Code at an early stage". Profiling for marketing should be off by default [search summary of ICO page and law-firm summaries] — [ICO news, Feb 2023](https://ico.org.uk/about-the-ico/media-centre/news-and-blogs/2023/02/new-guidance-to-industry-issued-for-game-developers-on-protecting-children); [Bristows](https://inquisitiveminds.bristows.com/post/102i85n/ico-boosts-childrens-code-with-walkthrough-for-game-developers); [RPC](https://www.rpclegal.com/snapshots/data-protection/spring-2023/ico-publishes-guidance-on-compliance-of-game-design-with-the-childrens-code/)
- The ICO launched a review of children's privacy in mobile gaming in December 2025 [secondary] — [Hunton, Jan 2026](https://hunton.com/privacy-and-information-security-law/uk-government-launches-consultation-on-under-16s-social-media-ban)

**Ofcom, Protection of Children Codes (Online Safety Act)**
- Final codes were published 24 April 2025; services had until 24 July 2025 to complete children's risk assessments and adopt measures. The measures centre on age assurance, content filtering, removal and reporting, and default settings against contact by strangers [search summary] — [5Rights on Ofcom code](https://5rightsfoundation.com/uks-regulator-ofcom-launches-the-countrys-first-childrens-online-safety-code/). In a Westminster debate on 30 Oct 2025, critics said the codes do little about design features such as scroll mechanisms, "push notifications that constantly pull them back" and loot boxes [search summary] — [Westminster Brief](https://westminsterbrief.co.uk/archive/debate/30-october-2025/protection-children-codes-practice-ae63). Internet Matters' one-year review (July 2026) says the OSA does little about addictive design [search summary] — [Internet Matters](https://www.internetmatters.org/hub/research/one-year-of-protection-of-children-codes-whats-changed/)

**UK government, 2026**
- A consultation launched 19/20 Jan 2026 named "restricting addictive design features such as 'streaks' and 'infinite scrolling'", alongside an under-16 social media ban and a higher digital age of consent [secondary] — [Hunton, Jan 2026](https://hunton.com/privacy-and-information-security-law/uk-government-launches-consultation-on-under-16s-social-media-ban); [RPC, spring 2026](https://www.rpclegal.com/snapshots/technology-digital/spring-2026/a-safer-digital-childhood-uk-government-consults-on-childrens-mobile-and-social-media-use/)
- The government response ("Growing Up in the Online World", signed by the DSIT Secretary of State) announced on 15 June 2026 a ban on social media companies serving under-16s, with the first regulations before Parliament by the end of 2026 and in effect early 2027. For 16-17-year-olds it proposes defaults: overnight access limits from midnight to 6am, muted overnight push notifications, and autoplay and personalised feeds off. The Children's Wellbeing and Schools Act (Royal Assent) gives the power to act. The outcome on streaks for non-social services is unsettled [search summary] — [gov.uk response (PDF)](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6a57d7ce31fb6daf31413797/NCP_Government_response_A.pdf); [Jurist, June 2026](https://www.jurist.org/news/2026/06/uk-plans-to-restrict-under‑16-access-to-high-risk-social-media-features/); [Lewis Silkin](https://www.lewissilkin.com/passle/102mrv7)

**EU, DSA Article 28 guidelines (final 14 July 2025)**
- "Features that promote excessive use – such as autoplay, streaks, or push notifications during sleep hours" should be off by default. Interfaces should avoid "persuasive design features such as infinite scroll or urgency cues". Platforms must offer "effective time management tools, including reminders and nudges". "Monetisation features that obscure real-world value, such as tokens or loot boxes, should be avoided"; purchases must be priced in national currency and "must not be required to access core features" of a free service; guardian spending limits or approvals should be considered. AI tools "must not be activated by default or promoted to minors", and platforms must "clearly indicate when users are interacting with AI". "Micro and small enterprises are exempt" unless designated a very large online platform or search engine [secondary, with the guidelines' wording as quoted by the firm] — [Hogan Lovells](https://hlc.com/en/publications/the-longawaited-eu-guidelines-on-article-281-dsa-what-online-platforms-must-know); [Taylor Wessing](https://www.taylorwessing.com/en/insights-and-events/insights/2025/07/rd-european-commission-guidelines-on-protection-of-minors-under-the-digital-services-act); [European Audiovisual Observatory (Merlin)](https://merlin.obs.coe.int/article/10383)

**EU consumer law, virtual currencies and the Digital Fairness Act**
- **CPC Network "Key Principles on In-game Virtual Currencies" (21 March 2025; non-binding).** Currencies bought with real money are "digital representations of value". The principles require clear and transparent pricing and pre-contract information, no designs that hide the cost of in-game content, no forcing consumers to buy currency, respect for withdrawal rights, and attention to children's vulnerability. A parallel enforcement action targeted Star Stable Entertainment; industry groups objected [secondary] — [Baker McKenzie](https://connectontech.bakermckenzie.com/european-consumer-protection-network-issues-new-key-principles-on-in-game-virtual-currencies-impact-for-gaming-and-gambling-entities-in-belgium-the-eu-and-beyond/); [Reed Smith Q&A](https://www.reedsmith.com/en/perspectives/2025/03/qas-on-the-eu-consumer-protection-authorities-joint-guidance-paper)
- **Digital Fairness Act.** The consultation ran 17 July to 24 Oct 2025. The Commission's 2026 work programme puts the proposal in **Q4 2026**, and the commissioner has said it will come "this year". As of mid-October 2026 no proposal had been tabled. Options reported include default-off engagement features, opting out of algorithmic recommendations, and bans on especially harmful features aimed at children. BEUC (December 2025) urges banning premium virtual currencies and paid loot boxes [secondary and search summary] — [European Parliament Legislative Train](https://www.europarl.europa.eu/legislative-train/theme-protecting-our-democracy-upholding-our-values/file-digital-fairness-act); [The European Post](https://theeuropeanpost.eu/research-analysis/digital-fairness-act-targets-dark-patterns-before-2027/); [BEUC position paper (PDF)](https://www.beuc.eu/sites/default/files/publications/BEUC-X-2025-110_Towards_the_Digital_Fairness_Act.pdf?wpacode=67475); [Sidley, Jul 2025](https://www.sidley.com/en/insights/newsupdates/2025/07/eu-consults-on-digital-fairness-act-big-changes-ahead-for-consumer-facing-platforms)

### Inferences
- For a UK or EU launch, the safe template is: streaks and reminders off by default, chosen by the child or guardian; no night-time pushes; neutral "continue" options; a pause that never loses progress; AI-written questions labelled as AI-written. The DSA's small-enterprise exemption would likely spare Aimtrue the Article 28 guidelines as such. The ICO Children's Code has no size exemption, and the DFA, a consumer law, probably will not either.
- The UK's 2026 restrictions target social media services. A tutoring platform with an organization-only leaderboard and no user-to-user content is probably out of scope, but this depends on regulations not yet published.

### Gaps
- The Commission's guidelines text (OJ C/2025/5519) did not load from EUR-Lex here, so section numbers and exact wording come from law-firm quotations. The guidelines' treatment of rankings, leaderboards or educational gamification was not found.
- The ICO games guidance's own wording on streaks was not read.
- The final text of the UK regulations and their definition of a covered service are not yet public.

## 5. App-store rules: Apple's Kids Category, Google Play Families, and the age-signal APIs

### Takeaway
Apple's rules: promotional push notifications need explicit in-app opt-in and an opt-out (4.5.4). Push notifications may not be monetised (4.10). Apps may incentivise in-app actions such as "completing a level" but may not force ratings or reviews (3.2.2(x)). Purchased credits may not expire (3.1.1). Kids Category apps may have no links out, purchases "or other distractions" except behind a parental gate, and no third-party analytics or ads (1.3). Google Play's Families policy bans "shocking or emotionally manipulative tactics to encourage ads viewing or in-app purchases". It requires a clear distinction between virtual coins and real money and "adult action" before children exchange personal information. It has no rule on notifications or leaderboards as such. The age APIs: Apple's Declared Age Range and Google's Play Age Signals (beta) send age bands and parental-approval status. They are live in Texas since the 4 June 2026 stay, will be needed for California AB 1043 from 1 Jan 2027, and then for Utah and Louisiana in 2027. An app with adult roles (admin, tutor, parent) beside a student mode is a poor fit for Apple's Kids Category. On Google Play, Families policy applies whenever the declared target audience includes children.

### Cited Findings
- **Apple 4.5.4:** "Push Notifications must not be required for the app to function… should not be used for promotions or direct marketing purposes unless customers have explicitly opted in to receive them via consent language displayed in your app's UI, and you provide a method in your app for a user to opt out" [primary] — [Apple App Store Review Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)
- **Apple 4.10:** "You may not monetize built-in capabilities provided by the hardware or operating system, such as Push Notifications…" [primary] — [Apple Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)
- **Apple 3.2.2(x):** "Apps must not force users to rate the app, review the app, download other apps, or other store-related actions in order to access functionality… Apps may otherwise incentivize users to take specific actions within apps (such as by completing a level or watching an ad)" [primary] — [Apple Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)
- **Apple 3.1.1:** "Any credits or in-game currencies purchased via in-app purchase may not expire" [primary] — [Apple Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)
- **Apple 1.3 (Kids Category):** "These apps must not include links out of the app, purchasing opportunities, or other distractions to kids unless reserved for a designated area behind a parental gate"; no personally identifiable or device information to third parties; "should not include third-party analytics or third-party advertising" [primary] — [Apple Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/). The earlier notes add that the rules keep applying after an app leaves the Kids Category, and that "For Kids" metadata is reserved for it.
- **Apple education-related rules:** 1.5 (a contact route, "particularly important for apps that may be used in the classroom"); 3.1.3(c) (organization-sold access to students may bypass in-app purchase); 4.8 exemption for apps that require "an existing education or enterprise account" [primary] — [Apple Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)
- **Apple age-assurance tooling:** Declared Age Range returns an age bracket (for Texas: under 13, 13-15, 16-17, 18+), whether parental controls are on, and eligibility for age-gated features. It is based on what the user or a parent declared. Developers must also use the Significant Change API (PermissionKit) to request renewed parental consent after major updates, plus StoreKit age-rating properties and App Store Server Notifications. Apple: "You are solely responsible for ensuring compliance" [secondary] — [9to5Mac, Oct 2025](https://9to5mac.com/2025/10/08/apple-announces-new-requirements-for-apps-available-in-texas/); [9to5Mac, Nov 2025](https://9to5mac.com/2025/11/04/app-store-texas-sb2420-new-apis/). Apple paused the Texas rollout after the December 2025 injunction [secondary] — [TechCrunch, 24 Dec 2025](https://techcrunch.com/2025/12/24/apple-pauses-app-store-changes-in-texas-after-court-blocks-age-assurance-law/). It reportedly reactivated the Texas rules after the June 2026 stay [search summary] — [PPC Land](https://ppc.land/apple-activates-texas-sb-2420-age-rules-for-app-store-after-injunction-lifted/)
- **Google Play Families policy [primary]:** it prohibits the "Use of shocking or emotionally manipulative tactics to encourage ads viewing or in-app purchases" and "Not providing a distinction between the use of virtual game coins versus real-life money to make in-app purchases". Rewarded or opt-in ads must be closeable. Child-directed apps use only Families Self-Certified Ads SDKs with no interest-based ads, may not transmit the AAID, and may not request location. Social features need an in-app online-safety reminder and "adult action before enabling features that allow children to exchange personal information". "All apps that comply with Google Play Families policies can opt in to be rated for the Teacher Approved program." No notification-specific rule — [Google Play Families policy](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/9893335)
- **Google Play Age Signals API (beta):** gives age-verification or supervision status and age ranges. Parents enable sharing and may revoke it. Developers must take in age ranges and parental approval status for significant changes, and notify Play of significant changes (through Play Console, without a new release). Play Console reports when a parent revokes approval [primary page via search summary] — [Google Play Console Help, Age Signals](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/16569691). Google says it will cover the remaining markets, including other US states with app-store laws, by the end of 2026 [secondary] — [TechRepublic](https://techrepublic.com/article/news-google-play-age-signals-api-global-rollout)

### Inferences
- **Kids Category: no.** Aimtrue's single app serves admins, tutors and parents, with sign-in, external links (calendar files, PDFs) and organization features. Apple 1.3's "no links out… or other distractions" and no third-party analytics would bind the whole app permanently. The Kids Category is a choice, not a duty, and COPPA applies regardless. Answer the age-rating questions honestly (2.3.6) and keep "for kids" out of the metadata.
- **Google Play: declare honestly.** If the target audience includes under-13s, Families policy applies to the app: no AAID, Families-certified SDKs only, adult action for social features. An organization-only leaderboard showing nicknames and points is not "freeform content", but a later chat or "cheer" feature would be.
- **Push reminders:** treat student streak reminders as promotional for Apple 4.5.4 purposes. That means in-app opt-in language plus an in-app off switch, and for under-13s ideally the guardian's opt-in.
- **Age signals:** build one age-gate service that reads Declared Age Range and Play Age Signals and records parental approval per student. Texas needs it now, California from 1 Jan 2027, and Utah and Louisiana in 2027. Plan to report Phase 44/45 releases as possible "significant changes".

### Gaps
- Apple's text defining Kids Category age bands (5 and under, 6-8, 9-11) was not in the excerpt read, and the guidelines' "last updated" date was not visible (the earlier notes give 16 Oct 2026).
- The statutory meaning of "significant change" in Texas, Utah and Louisiana, and whether adding gamification triggers it, was not verified.
- Whether Apple or Google treat an organization-only points leaderboard as a "social feature" was not found.

## 6. Industry codes and school expectations

### Takeaway
The best-known US edtech code, the FPF/SIIA **Student Privacy Pledge, was retired on 25 April 2025**. No current procurement checklist that scores gamification, streaks or notifications was found. School and district vetting still runs through data-privacy agreements (SDPC), privacy ratings and COPPA/FERPA questionnaires, which centre on data use rather than engagement design (earlier notes). 5Rights' *Disrupted Childhood* (2018; updated April 2023) is the main advocacy source naming streaks, notifications, likes and reward loops as persuasive design. Its framing that design, not children or parents, should carry the burden runs through the ICO code and the Vermont and Nebraska laws.

### Cited Findings
- FPF "retired the Student Privacy Pledge previously hosted at studentprivacypledge.org" as of 25 April 2025, after ten years, citing "the changing technological and policy landscape"; the signatory list stayed public until 31 July 2025 [primary] — [FPF, Student Privacy Pledge page](https://fpf.org/student-privacy-pledge/). Before retirement, FPF stripped Illuminate Education of signatory status after its breach and referred it to the FTC and state AGs [secondary] — [The 74](https://www.the74million.org/article/illuminate-ed-pulled-from-student-privacy-pledge-after-massive-data-breach/)
- 5Rights, *Disrupted Childhood: The Cost of Persuasive Design* (2018; v2 April 2023): persuasive design uses behavioural science to "influence user behaviour"; human instincts such as response to "rewards and punishments" are leveraged "to keep children online". It lists streaks with auto-play, notifications and likes as creating an "ecosystem of distraction, competition and invasion". Its 24 recommendations include adding "compulsive use" to policy lists of harms and placing responsibility on design rather than on children and parents [primary PDF via search summary] — [5Rights, Disrupted Childhood 2023 (PDF)](https://5rightsfoundation.com/uploads/Disrupted-Childhood-2023-v2.pdf); [5Rights persuasive design page](https://5rightsfoundation.com/our-work/design-of-service/persuasive-design.html)
- Research: a December 2025 study of popular children's mobile apps found deceptive designs such as "Attention Capture", "Gamification (Forced Action)", "Shaming" and "Urgency" that prolong play by creating pressure to stay or return [secondary] — [arXiv 2512.17819, "Playful but Persuasive"](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.17819). An academic explainer describes streaks as negative reinforcement (fear of losing something), notes the link between Snapchat streaks and problematic smartphone use, and notes that lost streaks can often be restored for money [secondary] — [University of Wollongong, 2024](https://www.uow.edu.au/media/2024/the-design-tricks-keeping-your-kids-hooked-on-games-and-apps.php)
- No K-12 procurement rubric that scores gamification or notification design was found. What turned up were higher-education gamification rubrics (Quality Matters) and research evaluation routines (EVER) [search result] — [Quality Matters](https://qualitymatters.org/qa-resources/resource-center/conference-presentations/gamification-matters-applying-qm-rubric); [EVER, PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10482916/)

### Inferences
- With the Pledge gone, schools lean on state laws (SOPIPA-type), the national data-privacy agreement and privacy ratings. Engagement design is not yet a standard procurement line item. Publishing a short "how Question Quest motivates without pressure" statement (no loss-framed streaks, no random rewards, no night or school-hours pushes, opt-in leaderboard) would answer the questions sophisticated organizations and parents are starting to ask, and is cheap.

### Gaps
- Common Sense Privacy Program evaluation criteria were not retrieved. Whether they now score engagement design (notifications, streaks, leaderboards) is unknown.
- The successor guidance FPF said it would provide after the Pledge, and whether the SDPC national data-privacy agreement added design terms, were not checked.

## 7. The specific patterns regulators and codes flag, with a citation for each

### Takeaway
Six of the seven patterns have a regulator or legislature behind them. Night-time or school-hours notifications, infinite flows, variable or random rewards, streaks and virtual currency are each named in at least one law, proposed rule or official guidance. Public rankings of children are reached indirectly: through "popularity indicators", like counts, the COPPA rule that making personal information public counts as collection, and FERPA practice on posting grades. **Paid streak repair was not found named by any regulator.** It is flagged by researchers, and sits under the general rules against purchases required for core features and emotionally manipulative purchase prompts.

- **Loss-framed or default-on streaks:**
  - Vermont proposed rules ban "social pressure features" including "streaks" — [Mayer Brown, Sept 2026](https://www.mayerbrown.com/en/insights/publications/2026/09/vermonts-proposed-aadc-rules-would-force-a-redesign-of-how-online-services-present-content-to-minors-reaching-far-beyond-social-media-companies)

  - ICO games guidance: disengage without "becoming fearful of missing out" [search summary] — [Bristows](https://inquisitiveminds.bristows.com/post/102i85n/ico-boosts-childrens-code-with-walkthrough-for-game-developers)

### Inferences
**A compliance checklist for Phases 44 and 45, drawn from the findings above.** It is designed to clear the strictest text found (Vermont's proposed rules, Nebraska, the DSA guidelines and the ICO code). It is not legal advice.

*Streaks and bonuses (Phase 44)*
1. Frame streaks as gains, not losses. "3 days of practice" or "one question keeps it going" is acceptable; "you'll lose your streak" and countdown timers are not, because they are urgency cues (EU) and fear of missing out (ICO).
2. Never sell streak repair or let points buy one. Give an automatic, free grace: weekends, organization holidays and school breaks don't break a streak. A paid repair is a purchase required for a core feature (EU), an emotionally manipulative purchase prompt (Google) and an in-game purchase (Nebraska).
3. Cap streak bonuses, or make them flat, and keep them predictable. Nebraska covers "rewards… for frequency of visits", and Vermont would ban variable rewards. A fixed, announced bonus is much easier to defend than an escalating or surprise one.
4. Give students (and guardians) a switch to hide streaks. For under-13s, consider defaulting streaks off unless a guardian or organization turns them on (EU default-off; Nebraska opt-out with protective defaults).
5. Keep the "Now/Next" question flow deliberate: one question, then an explicit "Next question" tap, never auto-advance into the next one. Offer an obvious "Stop for today" that keeps progress (ICO Standard 5; Vermont autoplay and infinite-scroll ban). Quest's "no going back" is fine; endless auto-advance is the risk.
6. Make the home-screen nudge truthful and specific: no invented peers ("Sam just passed you"), which is the Sendit theory. Show it only in-app and only during the student's own session. Treat it as an engagement notification under Vermont's draft if Vermont students are ever enrolled.

*Badges (Phase 44)*
7. Award badges for announced learning milestones (a level finished, 50 questions checked), never at random and never as mystery or loot drops (Vermont, ICO, FTC Genshin).

*Push reminders (later)*
8. Student pushes only by opt-in: guardian opt-in under 13, plus Apple 4.5.4 in-app consent language and an in-app off switch.
9. Quiet hours on the organization's clock: no student pushes 10pm-6am, and none 8am-4pm on school-year weekdays (Nebraska, the widest window). Keep transactional or lesson reminders separate from engagement reminders (Vermont's exception).
10. One push a day at most, and none that re-engages a student who has gone quiet. "Reinitiate engagement" is the Vermont draft's own wording.

*Rewards shelf (Phase 45)*
11. Points are earned, never bought, never converted to cash or gift cards, and never expiring for purchased value (there is none). Nothing random: no spins or mystery rewards. Show each reward's cost in points plainly. Keep a guardian view of what was redeemed (Nebraska parental tools; CPC transparency).
12. If an organization ships a physical reward, get the address from the guardian, not the child (COPPA contests FAQ, earlier notes).

*Leaderboard (Phase 45)*
13. Organization-only, opt-in by the student and approved by the guardian, nicknames or first names only, and nothing that works as contact information (COPPA). Default off (Nebraska Sec. 4(1)(b); EU and ICO private-by-default). Prefer bands or "personal best" views over a full rank list, and never show the bottom of the table. No cross-organization board.
14. If an organization is a school, or sells through one, treat named points as education records: no display to classmates without the school's FERPA basis.

*Question Quest data*
15. In the guardian notice and consent, say that answers help tutors check AI-written questions that then join a shared bank. Keep answers and identities out of the shared bank. Add `quest_attempts` and `points_ledger` to the written retention schedule, with deletion on request (COPPA; NC 115C-401.2(e) pattern).
16. Label questions as AI-written and not yet reviewed (EU guidelines' AI-transparency expectation; the platform's own "checked, not approved" status).

*App stores and age signals*
17. Stay out of Apple's Kids Category. Declare the Google Play target audience honestly (Families policy applies if it includes under-13s). Integrate Declared Age Range and Play Age Signals for Texas now and California on 1 Jan 2027. Treat each Phase 44/45 release as a possible "significant change" for those laws.

### Gaps
- Paid streak repair: no regulator or statute naming it was found.
- Whether an in-app home-screen nudge (not a push) counts as a "notification" under Nebraska Sec. 5(6) or Vermont's draft is unresolved in the sources.
- Whether fixed, predictable points and badges fall within Vermont's "variable or intermittent rewards" depends on final rule text not yet published.
- No source addresses leaderboards limited to one organization specifically (as opposed to public rankings).
