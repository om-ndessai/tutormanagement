# Math challenge, competition and game-based learning products for children: market, business models, buyers and regulation (as of 21 October 2026)

Scope note: US focus, with EU/UK notes. Every figure is dated. Reliability flags: **[primary]** = company/regulator/filing/official page; **[secondary]** = law firm, trade press, reputable journalism; **[low]** = vendor blog, pricing aggregator, paywalled market-research teaser, auto-generated directory. Market-research "market size" numbers are teaser figures from paywalled reports whose definitions are not visible; they disagree by 2-3x and should be treated as directional only.

## 1. Market size and growth (game-based learning, K-12 math supplemental/enrichment, competition prep, tutoring-centre software)

### Takeaway
No credible single figure exists for "math competition/challenge products for children". The adjacent markets are large but poorly measured: global "game-based learning" teaser estimates range from ~US$9B to ~US$25B for 2024-25 (definitions include corporate training), and US tutoring is roughly a US$13-20B category on weak, secondhand attributions. The most reliable hard numbers are company-level: Mathnasium's FDD (1,047 units, average gross receipts ~$385k per centre in 2025), Kahoot's ~US$1.7B take-private (2024, ~$146M trailing revenue), and Duolingo's 12.5M paid subscribers (Q1 2026).

### Inferences
- The serviceable market for this platform is better sized bottom-up than from market reports: ~1,050 Mathnasium units averaging ~$385k gross, ~1,500 Kumon units, ~85 RSM sites, 23 AoPS Academies, plus thousands of independent centres. A centre averaging $385k gross that already pays 10% royalty plus a technology fee is used to software being a few percent of revenue; an engagement/tournament add-on priced at a fraction of one student's monthly tuition ($250-$400) is an easy ROI argument if it improves retention by even one student.
- "Game-based learning" headline numbers (US$20-25B) are not usable in a pitch without heavy caveats; company-level evidence (Kahoot's $1.7B valuation on ~$146M revenue; Duolingo's 12.5M payers) is more defensible.

## 2. Business models and prices (who pays, what they pay)

### Takeaway
Four payers dominate: (1) teachers/schools via freemium classroom game tools (~$3-$60/month per teacher), (2) parents via consumer subscriptions (~$60-$240/year per child), (3) schools/parents via per-student contest entry fees ($2.50-$80 per student per event), and (4) tutoring centres via per-tutor or per-lesson management software (~$15-$190/month base). Sponsorship (defence/engineering firms, quant trading firms, AoPS, Brilliant) funds the non-profit contest organisers. Contest-hosting software is a thin, mostly per-event market.

### Inferences
- Price anchors a tutoring-organisation platform will be compared against: management software at roughly $0.40-$2 per student per month (TutorBird/Teachworks examples above at 80 students = ~$35-$145/month), teacher game tools at ~$5-$60 per teacher per month, consumer math subscriptions at ~$5-$20 per child per month, and contest entry at $18-$50 per student per event.
- A per-active-student add-on (e.g., $1-$3/student/month) or per-tournament fee (e.g., $2-$10 per student per event, cheaper than MATHCOUNTS/Kangaroo because no physical proctoring) fits between these anchors. Franchise systems (Mathnasium) are hard to sell into individually because technology is chosen and billed by the franchisor; independent centres and small chains are the realistic first buyers.
- Live/real-time head-to-head play is what teachers pay Kahoot/Gimkit/Blooket for; homework/asynchronous assignment is the feature most often gated behind paid plans.
- Sponsorship is concentrated among defence/engineering firms and quant-trading firms recruiting future STEM talent; a platform hosting inter-centre tournaments could in principle carry similar sponsorships, but that is a non-profit-organiser model.

## 4. Regulation for a product where children compete online

### Takeaway
The amended COPPA Rule has been fully enforceable since **22 April 2026**: separate verifiable parental consent for non-integral third-party disclosure, a written security programme, a published retention schedule, and penalties up to $53,088 per violation. Schools (not tutoring centres) may consent for parents only for an educational, non-commercial purpose — and the FTC declined in 2025 to codify that exception. Live play is the highest-risk feature: the Epic/Fortnite order (2022) made default-off voice/text chat for children the enforcement benchmark; Apple bans random/anonymous chat; Google requires adult action before children exchange personal information. App-store age-verification laws (Texas in force pending appeal; Utah from May 2027) and the partially revived California AADC add obligations; UK and EU expect private-by-default profiles and no engagement-maximising streaks.

### Cited Findings
**COPPA as amended (US federal):**
- Final amendments published in the Federal Register 22 Apr 2025, effective 23 Jun 2025, full compliance required by **22 Apr 2026** (safe-harbour members had earlier dates) — [Davis Polk, 16 Apr 2026](https://www.davispolk.com/insights/client-update/ftc-prioritizes-coppa-enforcement-new-compliance-obligations-take-effect); [Toy Association, 30 Mar 2026](https://toyassociation.org/PressRoom2/News/2026-News/updated-coppa-rule-requirements-take-effect-april-22.aspx)
- New obligations: separate ("distinct") verifiable parental consent before disclosing children's data to third parties for purposes not integral to the service (targeted advertising and AI model training are not integral); a written information security programme (designated staff, annual risk assessment, testing, annual review); a written data-retention policy naming data, purpose and deletion timeframe, published in the online notice ("indefinite retention is expressly prohibited"); expanded notices naming third-party recipients/categories; written assurances from vendors; personal information now includes biometric identifiers (voiceprints, faceprints); operators relying on "support for internal operations" must disclose which operations and safeguards; max civil penalty **$53,088 per violation** — [Mondaq: "COPPA's amended rule is now in full effect"](https://webiis10.mondaq.com/unitedstates/privacy-protection/1790866/coppas-amended-rule-is-now-in-full-effect-what-operators-need-to-know)
- FTC (Jan 2025): parents must opt in to third-party advertising; personal information now covers biometric and government-issued identifiers; Safe Harbor programmes must publish membership lists; the Commission **declined** to finalise limits on push notifications to children (while remaining "concerned about the use of push notifications and other engagement techniques") and **declined** to finalise changes for ed-tech companies operating in schools; vote 5-0 [primary] — [FTC press release](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/01/ftc-finalizes-changes-childrens-privacy-rule-limiting-companies-ability-monetize-kids-data)
- FTC policy statement (25 Feb 2026): enforcement discretion for collecting children's data solely for age verification without prior consent, if used only for that, secured, deleted after, disclosed, and the operator complies with the Rule "in every other respect"; further age-verification rulemaking planned — [Mondaq](https://webiis10.mondaq.com/unitedstates/privacy-protection/1790866/coppas-amended-rule-is-now-in-full-effect-what-operators-need-to-know)
- Screen names: personal information includes "a screen or user name that functions as online contact information"; collection includes "enabling a child to make personal information publicly available in identifiable form" [primary] — [FTC COPPA FAQ (A.3, D.12)](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/complying-coppa-frequently-asked-questions)
- Support for internal operations explicitly lets operators "maintain user driven preferences, such as game scores, or character choices in virtual worlds"; it excludes behavioural advertising (FAQ J.5/J.8) — [FTC COPPA FAQ](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/complying-coppa-frequently-asked-questions)
- Public posting: operators must prescreen and delete children's photos/videos/audio of themselves from submissions, or first obtain parental consent (FAQ F.1-F.2) — [FTC COPPA FAQ](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/complying-coppa-frequently-asked-questions)
- **School authorization (FAQ N.1-N.2):** a school may act as the parent's agent only "in the educational context", for information collected "for the use and benefit of the school, and for no other commercial purpose"; the operator must give the school the direct notice it would give a parent, let it review/delete the child's data and stop further collection; any use for the operator's "own commercial purposes in addition to the provision of services to the school" needs parental consent; the FTC recommends the school or district, not an individual teacher, make the decision — [FTC COPPA FAQ](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/complying-coppa-frequently-asked-questions)
- **Contests (FAQ J.1):** an operator may collect only a child's online contact information to enter a contest, contact the child once at the end to say win/lose, then delete it; contacting more than once needs the multiple-contact exception (parent notice + opt-out); collecting a winner's home address requires direct notice and verifiable parental consent — or ask for a parent's online contact information and ask the parent for the address — [FTC COPPA FAQ](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/complying-coppa-frequently-asked-questions)

**FERPA (sold via schools):**
- A vendor may receive education records without consent as a "school official" only if it performs a service the school would otherwise use employees for, is under the school's direct control regarding use and maintenance of the records, and is bound by use/re-disclosure limits (34 CFR 99.31(a)(1)) — [Future of Privacy Forum](https://fpf.org/blog/who-exactly-is-a-school-official-anyway/); schools remain legally responsible for disclosures to ed-tech companies — [New America](https://www.newamerica.org/insights/privacy-considerations-higher-education-online-learning/applicable-laws/)

**App stores:**
- Apple Guidelines (last updated 16 Oct 2026). 1.3 Kids Category: no links out, purchasing or "other distractions" except behind a parental gate; may not send personally identifiable or device information to third parties; "should not include third-party analytics or third-party advertising" (narrow exceptions: no IDFA/identifiable info; contextual ads only with human-reviewed creatives); once in Kids Category the app must keep meeting the rules even if deselected — [Apple App Store Review Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)
- Apple 5.1.4: apps may ask for birthdate and parental contact only to comply with COPPA/GDPR etc.; apps that can collect "the ability to chat" or persistent identifiers from a minor need a privacy policy and must comply with children's privacy law; the Kids Category parental gate "is generally not the same as securing parental consent"; "For Kids"/"For Children" in metadata is reserved for the Kids Category — [Apple Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)
- Apple 1.2 (user-generated content): must filter objectionable content, offer reporting with timely response, blocking of abusive users and published contact info; "random or anonymous chat" and bullying apps "do not belong on the App Store" — [Apple Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)
- Apple 5.3: "Sweepstakes and contests must be sponsored by the developer of the app" (5.3.1); official rules must be presented in the app and state Apple is not a sponsor (5.3.2); no in-app purchase of credit/currency for real-money gaming (5.3.3) — [Apple Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)
- Google Play Families policy: declare target age groups; apps showing ads to children or unknown-age users may use only Families Self-Certified Ads SDKs with no interest-based ads; child-directed apps must not transmit AAID, SIM/Build serial, BSSID, MAC, SSID, IMEI, IMSI, must not request location; social features for children require an in-app online-safety reminder, adult controls to enable/disable them, and "adult action" (e.g., PIN, password, email verification) before children can exchange personal information; apps whose main purpose is chatting with strangers "must not target children"; Families-compliant apps can opt into "Teacher Approved" — [Google Play Families policy](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/9893335)

**Prizes, sweepstakes and contests for minors:**
- Beyond COPPA's one-time-contact rule (above), common practice is to require parent/guardian consent for entrants under the age of majority, with the parent indemnifying the sponsor — [Amplify sweepstakes terms (example)](https://amplify.com/sweepstakes-terms); many brands simply require entrants to be 18 or 21 to avoid COPPA burdens — [Thompson Coburn sweepstakes law blog (2012)](https://thompsoncoburn.com/insights/blogs/sweepstakes-law/post/2012-02-20/sorry-tweens-no-sweepstakes-or-contests-until-you-re-13); "no purchase necessary" / alternative method of entry is standard because paid entry + chance + prize = lottery — [Venable (2022)](https://www.venable.com/-/media/files/events/2022/03/sweepstakes-contests-and-other-types-of-giveaways.pdf)
- Cognosphere's $20M settlement included deception about the cost of winning prizes (loot-box style) — [Davis & Gilbert](https://www.dglaw.com/tag/coppa/page/3/)

**UK and EU:**
- UK ICO Children's Code applies to online games likely used by under-18s even if not aimed at them; children's profiles private by default, geolocation off by default, high-privacy defaults, profiling for targeted ads off by default, no nudges to weaken privacy; under-13s cannot consent themselves (UK GDPR), parental consent required — [RPC on ICO games guidance](https://www.rpclegal.com/snapshots/data-protection/spring-2023/ico-publishes-guidance-on-compliance-of-game-design-with-the-childrens-code/); [Browne Jacobson](https://www.brownejacobson.com/insights/protecting-children-and-their-data-in-the-online-environment); [ICO priorities 2024](https://ico.org.uk/about-the-ico/media-centre/news-and-blogs/2024/04/ico-sets-out-priorities-to-protect-childrens-privacy-online)
- EU DSA Article 28 guidelines (final 14 Jul 2025; non-binding but the Commission's yardstick): "accessible to minors" judged by demographics, marketing and design, not disclaimers; micro/small enterprises generally excluded unless designated VLOPs; age verification for high-risk content, age estimation where verification is disproportionate; EU age-verification prototype app released — [Taylor Wessing, Jul 2025](https://www.taylorwessing.com/en/insights-and-events/insights/2025/07/rd-european-commission-guidelines-on-protection-of-minors-under-the-digital-services-act). Draft (May 2025) called for private-by-default accounts, contacts limited to approved users, and engagement features such as streaks off by default, and against exploiting minors via virtual currencies/loot boxes; final text softened some default wording — [Covington, May 2025](https://www.covingtonblogs.com/2025/05/22/european-commission-publishes-draft-guidelines-on-the-protection-of-minors-under-the-dsa/); [Digital Poland, Jul 2025](https://digitalpoland.org/en/blog/2025/07/european-commission-publishes-dsa-article-28-guidelines-on-protection-of-minors)

### Inferences
- **Tutoring centres cannot use the school-authorization path.** The FTC FAQ speaks of "the school" acting as the parent's agent in the educational context; a private tutoring centre is a commercial customer, so verifiable parental consent from a parent is the safe default for under-13 accounts. A platform whose data model already requires every student to have a guardian who is a member of the organisation has a natural place to collect and record that consent (and the separate consent the amended rule requires for any non-integral third-party disclosure, e.g. an AI provider used to write questions or evaluate answers).
- **Leaderboards:** game scores are expressly within "support for internal operations", so showing a score is low-risk; the risk is the display name. A screen name becomes personal information when it "functions as online contact information", so leaderboards should show system-assigned or moderated nicknames that cannot be used to contact the child, never real names or emails, and should default to organisation-only visibility (ICO/EU private-by-default).
- **Live head-to-head play:** do it without free chat. The Epic order, Apple 1.2 and Google's "adult action" rule together make "voice/text off by default, no stranger matching for children, parent-controlled enablement" the de facto standard; preset emotes/reactions and matching only within the child's own organisation avoid most of it.
- **Prizes:** keep tournament rewards non-monetary (badges, certificates) or, if a prize must be shipped, route the address request through the parent (COPPA FAQ J.1), sponsor it as the developer with in-app official rules disclaiming Apple (5.3.1-5.3.2), and never charge entry plus chance (lottery). A paid entry to a skill tournament is legally distinct from a sweepstakes, but state treatment was not researched (see Gaps).
- **Streaks/push:** the FTC declined to regulate push notifications in 2025 but flagged concern; the EU draft guidelines and ICO treat engagement-maximising features for children as a risk. Keep streak/notification mechanics opt-in and parent-visible.
- **App stores:** an app used by students under 13 but sold to organisations can avoid the Kids Category (which forbids third-party analytics and purchase flows without a parental gate) only if its metadata does not imply children are the main audience (Apple 2.3.8 via 5.1.4); either way COPPA applies. Texas SB 2420 in force means app stores may pass age/consent signals that the app must honour.

## 5. Accessibility and fairness expectations from schools

### Takeaway
For US public-school buyers the benchmark is WCAG 2.1 AA: the DOJ's ADA Title II rule covers state and local governments' web content and mobile apps, but in April 2026 the DOJ pushed compliance one year to **26 April 2027** (large entities, 50k+ population) and **26 April 2028** (smaller entities and special districts), leaving the standard unchanged. Timed contests are a fairness flashpoint: extended time is a common testing accommodation, yet at least some math contests forbid extra time in competition rounds.

### Cited Findings
- DOJ interim final rule (effective 20 Apr 2026; comments to 22 Jun 2026) extended ADA Title II web/mobile accessibility deadlines from 24 Apr 2026 to 26 Apr 2027 for entities serving 50,000+ and from 26 Apr 2027 to 26 Apr 2028 for smaller entities and special districts; standard remains WCAG 2.1 Level AA; reasons cited include resource constraints and limits of generative-AI remediation; ongoing Title II obligations continue regardless — [Plante Moran, Apr 2026](https://plantemoran.com/explore-our-thinking/insight/2026/04/doj-extends-ada-title-ii-web-accessibility-compliance-deadlines); [Level Access](https://www.levelaccess.com/blog/the-ada-title-ii-extension-what-it-doesnt-change/); [Consumer Financial Services Law Monitor, Apr 2026](https://www.consumerfinancialserviceslawmonitor.com/2026/04/doj-extends-title-ii-ada-web-accessibility-rule-compliance-deadlines-for-state-and-local-governments/). One source gives the original large-entity date as 22 Apr 2026 rather than 24 Apr (minor conflict).
- Extended time is defined as additional time to complete an assessment or section of one — [NCEO Accommodations Toolkit](https://publications.ici.umn.edu/nceo/accommodations-toolkit/extended-time-research)
- Missouri Council of Teachers of Mathematics middle-school contest: accommodation requests must reach coordinators in writing at least three weeks ahead; impermissible accommodations include "granting a student extra time during any of the competition rounds" — [MOCTM FAQ](https://moctm.org/images/pdf/FAQ_-_Middle_Contest.pdf)
- MATHCOUNTS funds access for underserved schools (131 competition schools via a 3M grant; 852 Title I clubs) and Math Kangaroo halves fees for Title I schools — signals that equity of access is an expectation in this sector — [MATHCOUNTS annual26](https://mathcounts.org/annual26); [Math Kangaroo invitation letter](https://mathkangaroo.org/mks/wp-content/uploads/2025/06/MK-2026_Invitation-to-Host-the-29th-Math-Kangaroo-Competition-letter-to-the-school-from-a-parent.pdf)

### Inferences
- A timed-tournament product should support a per-student time multiplier (set by an adult, recorded, not visible to peers) and a mode where accommodated students are ranked separately or not timed — schools will ask for IEP/504 accommodations even where elite contests refuse them.
- Live head-to-head speed games disadvantage students with processing, motor or visual disabilities; offering an untimed or turn-based variant, keyboard/screen-reader-operable answer input, and no colour-only signalling is the practical path to WCAG 2.1 AA for this feature.
- The DOJ delay lowers the immediate legal pressure on districts, but WCAG 2.1 AA remains the procurement checklist item and the 2027 deadline lands within a typical roadmap horizon.

### Gaps
- MAA (AMC) and MATHCOUNTS official accommodation policies for extended time were not found.
- No source found on fairness/anti-cheating expectations for online children's contests (proctoring, device lockdown, identity checks for minors).
- Section 504 / IDEA obligations for private tutoring centres (as opposed to schools) were not researched.
