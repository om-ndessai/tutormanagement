# Profile of Math Kangaroo, Junior (grades 9-10), level id MK910

Built from the ten papers 2016-2025 (300 questions). Everything below is described in general words; no
question, number or story is copied. Counts are by hand and rounded; where a question was a judgement call
between two kinds I put it where its main idea sits.

## 1. Format

- 30 questions per paper in every year 2016-2025 (so 300 in all): questions 1-10 are worth 3 points, 11-20
  worth 4, 21-30 worth 5. Five options (A)-(E) each; 75 minutes; 30 starting points; a wrong answer costs
  a quarter of the question's points.
- Origin of the texts: the Austrian paper in 2016-2019 and 2022-2025, the Brazilian Junior paper (Level J) in
  2020 and 2021 (translated; slightly different style, a little more geometry and counting on grids).
- Most options are plain numbers or short expressions (a few are ratios, fractions with surds, or "only a / only b"
  statements). Roughly one paper in two has an option of the form "It cannot be determined" or "another number"
  on one or two questions.
- Every paper has a lot of pictures: about 120 of the 300 questions (40%) lose something essential without
  the figure. The Austrian text also carries a few small extraction slips (a repeated option, a lost
  formula), so the data are not perfectly clean.
- Questions are not strictly in order of difficulty inside a third, but the thirds are clearly graded.
- Calculators are not allowed, so every numerical answer is reachable by hand, usually with a trick (a pattern,
  a symmetry, a factorisation) rather than long arithmetic.

Observations:

- Geometry in all forms (plane, solid, spatial, graph reading) is about a third of the paper (101 of 300), the
  largest block, and most of it is shown in a figure. Number theory, logic, algebra and counting each
  carry about a ninth to a seventh.
- Picture-only kinds (spatial/visual, graph reading, ring/star figures) are about 7% in total and are
  best folded into "geometry in words" or "logic".
- The 3-point third is mostly arithmetic, parity, a simple count, a mean, a clean area; the 5-point third is
  dominated by geometry with a hidden step, digit/number-theory constructions and tricky counting.
- Over the ten years the mix is fairly stable; plane geometry is never below 5 of 30, and digit and number-theory
  problems are a little more frequent in the last four papers.

## 3. Maths the level uses

What a strong 9th to 10th grader knows, and no more.

Numbers and arithmetic
- Whole numbers up to 4-5 digits are the usual size; the year number (about 2000) appears often as a target
  sum or as a count, and powers of ten or two are used for "how many digits" and pattern questions.
- Fractions, decimals and percentages, including percentage change and "percent of a percent"; ratios
  and proportion; arithmetic mean (including finding a missing value or how a mean changes); estimating
  a value by rounding.
- Integers including negatives; parity rules (odd + odd, product parity); divisibility tests for 2, 3,
  4, 5, 6, 8, 9, 11; primes up to about 50; prime factorisation and gcd/lcm; remainders and cycles (day of the
  week after N days); a number's digits and their sum or product; consecutive integers and their sums.
- Powers and square roots with integer exponents; sums of squares; difference of squares; a simple use of
  surds (a root of 2 or 3 in an answer option) but no calculation with roots beyond that.

Algebra
- Linear equations and small systems from words; simple quadratic identities, factorising to solve an integer
  equation (a product equals a number); inequalities used for "smallest/largest possible"; sequences defined
  by a rule or a pattern, arithmetic progressions, a repeating cycle; sums of the first n numbers; an
  equation of a straight line (gradient and intercept) used with the coordinate axes.
- Rates: distance = speed x time, work and mixture ratios, circular motion with two speeds; average speed.

Geometry
- Areas of triangles, rectangles, parallelograms, trapezia, circles and sectors; area ratios through
  common base or height; similar triangles and scaling (area scales as the square); perimeter.
- Pythagoras (including 3-4-5 and similar triples and a diagonal of a square or rectangle), special triangles
  (30-60-90, 45-45-90), equilateral triangles and regular hexagons built of them.
- Angle facts: triangle sum, exterior angle, polygon angle sum, angles in parallel lines, inscribed angle and
  the angle in a semicircle, tangent perpendicular to the radius, the isosceles triangle from two radii; the
  circle's circumference and area with pi left in the answer.
- Triangle inequality, triangle heights and area, midpoints and medians, a point on a coordinate grid, the
  distance between two coordinate points.
- Solids: cubes and cuboids (volume, surface, painted faces after cutting up a big cube), counting vertices,
  edges, faces; nets of a cube or octahedron; a die with opposite faces summing to 7.

Counting and probability
- Product rule, permutations and arrangements of a few objects, combinations of small numbers (choose 2 of 6),
  counting by cases, complementary counting, pigeonhole, binary and powers of two; a probability as favourable
  over possible with equally likely outcomes (dice, drawing a card, a random removal order).

Logic
- Truth-tellers and liars (including who tells the truth on alternate days), elimination tables, ordering by
  clues, seating around a table, graphs by handshakes/degrees, invariants (parity, colouring), extremal
  arguments ("at least ... must").

Notation
- Standard: (A)...(E), fractions as a/b or stacked, decimals with a point (the Brazil year uses a comma), %, degrees,
  1/2 and mixed numbers, a x b or a dot for product. Letters A, B, C for points and a, b, c for numbers; "|"
  or comma for coordinates; surds and pi in options. No trigonometry, no logarithms, no calculus, no complex
  numbers, no matrices, no binomial theorem, no formal sets beyond small lists.

## 4. What makes a 3-, 4- and 5-point question

3 points (about 1 to 2 minutes, one idea, one or two steps)
- A direct application: a mean with a missing value; a percentage or ratio of a quantity; converting units or
  counting hours into weeks; the parity of an expression; a remainder after dividing once; how many whole
  numbers or pages in a range; reading the day of the week after many days; a clean rectangle or triangle area.
- A first counting idea: "enter by one door and leave by another", the number of different sums three dice
  can show, how many numbers from a list fit one condition.
- A first logic idea: a family with brothers and sisters, one clue, the pigeonhole with two colours and
  "at least three of one".
- Traps are mild: unit slip, off-by-one in an interval, the word "more" versus "times".

4 points (about 3 minutes, two or three linked steps, a small trick)
- Setting up an equation from a verbal story (ages, money, who has how many), a percentage-change chain, or an
  equation with an unknown in two places.
- Counting with a condition: count arrangements without adjacent equal items, walks up stairs with one step
  forbidden, how many numbers n have a property across a boundary (digits changing at 999 or 9999), choices
  of days with no two adjacent.
- Divisibility with a twist: a number divisible by two given numbers so that the missing digits are forced; the
  largest number of a special digit shape that is also a multiple of something; consecutive numbers with a stated
  sum of powers.
- Geometry in two steps: ratio of areas through midpoints; angles through isosceles triangles; a rectangle cut
  into similar rectangles; a square on the sides of a right triangle.
- Logic in two steps: a truth-teller schedule over days, a set of sporting results that must fit a bracket,
  compare unknown amounts from sums of pairs.

5 points (about 4 to 6 minutes, a hidden idea, case analysis or a construction)
- Number theory constructions: find all numbers with a property in a family (a digit-deletion rule, a
  divisibility condition between two expressions in one unknown, how a digit product or digit sum behaves when 1
  is added), the largest possible remainder, several primes whose product is tied to their sum, Diophantine
  equations in two unknowns.
- Extremal and construction problems: the largest sum in a tournament with the total fixed, the least number of
  moves to reach a pattern, the most elements that can be placed under a rule, the smallest set to remove so
  that a product becomes a square.
- Counting that needs organising: arrangements around a circle under a remainder rule, counting routes that spell
  something in a small grid by symmetry, colour assignments with pairs forced to match, strings built by a stated
  rule.
- Geometry with a hidden step: area through congruent pieces and the Pythagorean theorem, tangent circles with
  radius relations, a chord and a tangent, a folded sheet and the overlap, the sum of angles over a star.
- Logic with several layers: inhabitants classified by what kind of statement they can make, a day-alternating
  speaker and a list of statements of which all but one were made, the pigeonhole in a graph.
- The answer options in this third are often close together (to make a wrong method plausible), and one option is
  often "cannot be determined".

## 6. Writer cautions

General
- The bank is text only and every question is original. Do not borrow a recognisable contest story or number.
  Invent a new setting and new data, keep the idea at the level in section 4.
- Make the question solvable by hand in a few minutes with a clever step, not by a long calculation. If a
  brute-force check is shorter than the intended idea, change the numbers.
- Give each question exactly one correct option. The four wrong options should come from plausible mistakes
  (forgot to double, off by one, used the wrong ratio, added instead of multiplied). Avoid "cannot be
  determined" unless it is truly the answer, and then make it correct for a stated reason.

Ambiguities and traps in words
- Say whether numbers in a range are inclusive ("from 15 to 47, both included").
- "Between" is ambiguous with endpoints; "more than" and "times as many" differ: state "50% more" or "half as
  much again" in a way that has only one reading.
- "Different" versus "distinct": say "pairwise different". For positive integers say whether zero is allowed. Say
  "whole number" or "integer" correctly (negative allowed or not).
- Counting problems: say whether order matters, whether rotations or reflections of an arrangement are the
  same (for a circle or a necklace say "seats are distinguishable" or give a fixed starting person), whether
  an item may be reused, and whether the people or objects are distinguishable.
- Digits: a number cannot start with 0; say "four-digit" when you mean 1000 to 9999. Say which digit
  is "the last" and avoid letters that could mean either a digit or a product (write ab as "the number with tens
  digit a and units digit b").
- Percentages: say of what. A change of a percent followed by another is of the new value, not the original;
  say it.
- Rates: say whether speeds are constant, and give units once and consistently (km/h and minutes mixed is the
  commonest slip). In current problems say the speed given is relative to the water or the bank.
- Truth-teller and liar problems: define who always tells the truth, who always lies, and what a statement
  about "the other one" refers to; check that the answer is unique by testing every case.
- Without a figure, geometry must name everything: say which side is which, which points are midpoints, whether a
  point is inside or on a side, that the figure is not drawn to scale, and that "square" or "rectangle" is exactly
  that. Give enough data so that the figure is determined (a triangle by three sides, or two sides and the
  angle between); do not rely on how a drawing "looks".
- Never ask about a shape whose picture is the whole difficulty (a net that must be folded in the head, a
  cross-section, a rolling path). Reword it as a count (how many faces, edges, how many painted) or drop it.
- Solid problems: say whether the cube is painted before or after cutting, and on which faces; say a die has
  opposite faces summing to 7 when it matters.
- Coordinates: give points as ordered pairs, say which axis is which, and keep to the first quadrant or say so.
- Exact values: if a root or pi appears in an answer, give all five options in the same form (not one decimal and
  one surd); keep decimals to at most two places.
- Grid puzzles: describe the table row by row ("row 1: 4, ?, 7") and say what must agree (row sums, column sums,
  diagonals); check by hand that the solution is unique.
- Keep numbers in the contest's usual range: results under about 5000, the data of the year of the contest are
  fine but should not be copied from a given year; avoid two-step surd simplification and anything that needs
  trigonometry, logarithms or the binomial theorem.
- Check each question twice: once with the stated method and once with a different method (a quick enumeration
  or a different formula), and make sure the key is in the options exactly once.
