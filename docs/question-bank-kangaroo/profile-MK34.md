# Profile: Math Kangaroo, level Ecolier (grades 3-4), level id MK34

Built from the ten papers 2016-2025 (240 questions, each sorted by hand). Nothing below quotes or
closely paraphrases a question; examples are general ideas only.

## 1. Format

- 24 questions per paper in every year 2016-2025 (240 in all), in three bands of 8: questions 1-8 are
  worth 3 points, 9-16 worth 4, 17-24 worth 5. Five options (A)-(E), exactly one right.
- 60 minutes; 24 starting points; a wrong answer costs a quarter of the question's points, a blank costs
  nothing. Sources are the Austrian papers, except 2020 and 2021, which are the Brazilian papers
  (Level E, and the 2020 one is the "second application"); they follow the same pattern and style.
- Options are numbers for most calculation questions, but for many spatial questions the options are five
  small pictures. A few options are words (a name, a day, "to the left of ..."), or a pair ("A and B").
- Wording is short: one to four sentences, a named child or animal (often a kangaroo), everyday settings
  (shops, zoo, kitchen, school, toys). Several questions carry a title-line story and then a bare question.
- Unusual: some options in the numeric questions are spaced to catch a slip (an answer one off, the sum
  instead of the difference, the double), and in a few questions the option "none / it can be either" is a
  real answer. The same trick appears repeatedly: one option is what you get if you stop one step early.

## 2. Mix

All 240 questions were sorted by hand, one kind each (the main skill that decides the answer).

| Kind | Questions | Share | What it covers |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Spatial and visual (V) | 75 | 31% | Mirror images, turning, folding paper and cutting or punching holes, which piece fits or is missing, layers seen from above or the side, cubes in a stack, dice, nets, shadows, overlaying sheets, tiling a shape with given pieces. |
| Number puzzles and arithmetic (N) | 55 | 23% | Digit sums, biggest or smallest result from given digits, missing digits in an addition, number tables, circles or boxes joined by sums, calculations with a hidden number, packages and "fewest needed", leftovers, simple divisibility and remainders, sequences stepping by a constant. |
| Logic and deduction (L) | 42 | 18% | Constraint puzzles (each row and column holds each symbol once), who-said-what with one liar, ordering by weight or rank, matching people to objects from clues, "which must be true", minesweeper-style neighbour counts, scheduling two cookers. |
| Equations, balances, ages, shares (E) | 29 | 12% | Balance scales with shapes or fruit, items with equal weight, ages that differ by fixed amounts, two kinds of thing with a total, sharing out, fractions of a whole (halves, quarters), proportions such as "4 kg fresh makes 1 kg dried". |
| Counting and arrangements (C) | 16 | 7% | How many orders, how many ways to pick or colour, how many different figures can be built, how many pairs, minimum colours for a map-like drawing. |
| Length, perimeter, area, paths (G) | 13 | 5% | A border of squares around a picture, perimeter of an assembled shape, area built from squares of different sizes, a path along a corridor or fence, tile dimensions fitted into a picture. |
| Time, calendar, cycles (T) | 10 | 4% | Days of the week from "yesterday/tomorrow", weeks and days, bus or train schedules, a pattern that repeats along a long row, a journey that bounces between two ends. |

Pictures. About 180 of the 240 (roughly three quarters) cannot be posed without the picture, or the
picture carries the data (a grid, a diagram of pieces, a scale, a map). The picture-bound share differs by
band: nearly half of the 3-point questions (40 of 80) are the spatial kind, against about one in six of the
5-point ones (14 of 80), where logic and number work take over (logic is about 30% of the 5-pointers).
Papers drift towards more pictures: 2025 has 12 spatial questions, 2021 only 6.

Ideas that survive in words (the picture is only decoration or can be replaced by a description):
- Balance and weight problems: "X and Y balance Z" as a list of equations.
- Grid and Latin-square puzzles: "a 3 by 3 table holds 1, 2, 3 once in each row and column; some cells are
  given" (give the cells in a sentence or a small text table).
- Number rings, walls and triangles: describe which cells add to which, give the known numbers.
- Digits in an addition (a three-digit number plus a two-digit one with digits eaten away), written as a
  line of text with underscores.
- Counting in a described figure: layers of a cube stack given as "bottom layer 3 by 3, second layer 2 by 2".
- Dice: opposite faces add to 7, a die rolled over edges, which face is on top or hidden.
- Paths and hops on a grid: moves given as a list of directions and lengths; the answer is the square or
  the total distance.
- Strip or sheet folded in half and a hole punched: numbered cells in a row, which numbers meet.
- Paper cut-and-unfold, in the simple version: a rectangle folded once, a shape cut off the fold, how many
  pieces or holes or how many cut-outs after unfolding.
- Symmetry: how many lines of symmetry a described shape has; the number of triangles in a described figure
  made of equal parts; the perimeter after joining equal tiles.
- Orderings: a queue, rankings, a podium, comparison of heights or weights from clues.

Picture-only kinds that cannot be saved (mirror image of a drawn figure, which option is a rotated copy,
which tile fits a missing gap, shadows and overlays) should be folded into "geometry in words" at the idea
level (symmetry, turns, folds, counting faces) rather than reproduced.

## 3. Maths the level uses

Numbers and operations
- Whole numbers mostly below 100; sums of up to three small numbers; totals up to a few hundred in the
  "story" problems (a thousand or two only as a year, a page count or a prize). Four-digit numbers appear
  as years and in a digit-puzzle; three-digit numbers in missing-digit sums.
- Addition and subtraction fluently; multiplication and division by one-digit numbers, times tables to
  10 x 10; multiplying by 10, 100 only occasionally; no long division or decimals. Remainders appear
  in "cannot be shared equally between 7 children" style conditions.
- Divisibility by 2, 3, 4, 5, 10 and the idea of a common multiple (a pattern that repeats every 3 and every
  2 positions, items packed in fives, tens, twenty-fives).
- Fractions only as halves, thirds, quarters and "three quarters of" a small set; double and half; no
  fraction arithmetic. Ratios appear as "twice as many", "3 times as heavy", "4 to 1".
- Place value: digits of a number, digit sum, largest or smallest number from given digits, writing numbers
  such as years, pages and dates; counting a digit's occurrences in a run of page numbers.
- Powers, negative numbers, percentages and square roots do not appear. Basic notation only: + - x / =,
  brackets in a calculation chain, a symbol or box standing for an unknown digit or number.
- Units: kg and g, cm and m, minutes, days and weeks, coins or euros. Conversions are small (weeks to days,
  hours to minutes, kg to g in whole thousands).

Algebraic ideas without algebra
- Balance and "same weight" reasoning, a missing number, guess-and-check, two conditions on two unknowns,
  working backwards, a total shared between two kinds of thing, ages as "older by".

Geometry
- Names of plane figures (triangle, square, rectangle, circle, hexagon), cube, cuboid, pyramid built of cubes.
- Perimeter and area by counting unit squares; area as a count of cells; perimeter as a count of edges or
  as lengths added along a border. Right angles and "in the same direction"; left/right/up/down, clockwise
  and anticlockwise.
- Symmetry and reflection, rotation by quarter or half turns, folding, nets of a cube, opposite faces of a
  die, views of cube structures from above.

Logic and combinatorics
- Truth-tellers and one liar, elimination, orderings, simple ordered pairs, systematic listing of all cases
  (up to about 12-24), no factorials or formulas. Neighbour-counting (a square or hexagon grid where each
  cell tells how many neighbours have an object).

## 4. What makes a 3-, 4- and 5-point question

3 points (questions 1-8): one idea, one or two steps, answerable in under a minute.
- Spatial: pick the picture that matches after a flip, a turn or a fold; spot the piece that fits; which
  figure cannot be built from given parts. Half of the band is of this kind.
- Number: read a value off a small diagram and add; find a missing number in a short chain of sums; a
  digit sum or a year; a day-of-week step.
- Simple word problem: ages or heights compared; "half of them are visible, so ..."; fruit and prices where
  two pieces cost a known total.
- Logic: order three or four things from two clues.
Idea example: "a figure is turned over, which picture shows it?" or "three ages given by differences, add them".

4 points (questions 9-16): two or three steps, or a small search.
- Spatial: fold and punch then unfold with a count; the number of pieces that fit a shape; cubes with a
  given number of painted faces.
- Number: a table or ring where every cell is the sum of the labels on its row and column; packages of
  several sizes where a minimum has to be found; a total after a transaction with a change between two
  states; a sequence with a rule that has to be extended.
- Equations: shapes on a balance with two weighings; a total shared out with a given difference; weights
  where one item is the unknown.
- Counting and logic: all orders of a small set; a column-and-row table filled with each symbol once; a
  who-ate-what puzzle with a constraint.
Idea example: "find the smallest number of packages that make an exact total" or "two weighings show how heavy
one object is".

5 points (questions 17-24): several steps, a hidden condition, or a case split that must be exhausted.
- Logic: a statement puzzle with one liar; a grid with neighbour-counts; a table of clues each giving
  information about a different row; a process repeated until nothing can happen, where the remainder has
  to be found (a game of taking tokens from three places at once).
- Number: three or four unknown digits in a column addition; ordering numbers on a chain where neighbours
  must differ; a number wall or tower whose bottom row is unknown; the largest total from selected cells.
- Counting: how many different arrangements satisfy two or three restrictions; colouring a figure so touching
  parts differ; paths that must not reuse a doorway.
- Cycles: a pattern with two periods (for instance two repeating colour schemes along a line) and a question
  about which positions get both a given colour of each; a day-of-week question that needs a week-cycle.
- Spatial: a stack of cubes in layers where hidden cubes are counted; a figure built from three given shapes
  where one option is impossible.
Idea example: "five friends make statements and exactly one is lying; who did it?"; "a pattern repeats every
3 and every 2; how many places have both features among 60?".

Trap options. The most common wrong options are the intermediate result, the answer to a neighbouring
question, and off-by-one in "between" and "from ... to ..." counts.

## 5. Eight topics for the bank

Text only. Kinds of problem that work in words, together covering the level's mix: number puzzles (23%),
spatial work folded into geometry (31%), logic (18%), equations (12%), counting (7%), measure (5%), cycles (4%).

| MK34.02 | Everyday arithmetic stories | Number |
| MK34.03 | Balances, weights and ages | Algebra |
| MK34.04 | Patterns, cycles and calendars | Algebra |
| MK34.05 | Logic and deduction | Reasoning |
| MK34.06 | Counting ways and orders | Combinatorics |

Descriptions (one sentence each, with their skills):

- **MK34.01 Digits and number puzzles (Number).** Digits and place value in numbers up to four digits, digit sums of a year or a page number, the largest or smallest number from given digits, a missing digit in an addition written in a line, numbers filling a ring or a short chain so that sums match, and sorting numbers by a rule.
- **MK34.02 Everyday arithmetic stories (Number).** Shopping, packages of several sizes and the fewest needed, leftovers and sharing with remainders, halves, thirds and quarters of a small group, change after buying, totals gathered over several days, and "how many more are needed" questions.
- **MK34.03 Balances, weights and ages (Algebra).** Items that weigh the same, what balances on a scale described in words, two kinds of object with a known total, sharing a total so one share is twice or three times another, ages that differ by fixed amounts, and finding the one unknown by guess-and-check or working backwards.
- **MK34.04 Patterns, cycles and calendars (Algebra).** Sequences that step by a constant or follow a repeating rule, a pattern that repeats every few places and which place falls where, days of the week from "yesterday" and "the day after tomorrow", weeks and days, timetables and clocks, and a bouncing back-and-forth journey between two ends.
- **MK34.05 Logic and deduction (Reasoning).** Who-is-who from clues, one child lying among several statements, ordering people or things by height, weight or rank, a small table where each row and column uses each symbol once, matching people to objects, and finding what must be true when some cases are excluded.
- **MK34.06 Counting ways and orders (Combinatorics).** How many orders for a few people to line up, how many ordered pairs from a short list, ways to build a tower from some of the discs, ways to colour with restrictions, different figures from a small set of parts, and listing all cases systematically.
- **MK34.07 Shapes, folds and moves (Geometry).** Naming and counting plane shapes in a described figure, symmetry and mirror lines of a shape, turning by quarter and half turns, folding a strip or a sheet once or twice and punching a hole (where do the holes land), and a path on a grid given as a list of direction moves.
- **MK34.08 Solids, lengths and areas (Geometry).** Cubes in stacks and layers described in words, faces that are painted or hidden, dice with opposite faces adding to seven, perimeter and area by counting unit squares, a border of squares around a rectangle, and lengths of a fence or path made of equal pieces.

## 6. Writer cautions

General
- No pictures: every figure must be fully described. If a diagram would be needed, rewrite the situation so
  the data is in the sentences, or give a small text table. Say how many things there are and where.
- Original problems only: use new numbers, new contexts and new set-ups. A contest idea (balance, hop count,
  fold-and-punch, one liar) is common maths; a particular story is not.
- One correct option and four options each tempting for a plausible mistake (the one-step-early result,
  the double, one off). Never two options that are the same number in different form.
- Do not give a hint through option order or a unique odd-looking option; shuffle option positions.
- Keep to the level: whole numbers below 100 for most arithmetic, no formulas, no algebra letters used
  as unknowns on a child's reading line (a word like "the secret number" is fine).

Ambiguities and traps
- "Between" and "from ... to ..." counts (fence posts versus gaps, houses 15 to 47 inclusive): say whether
  ends are included, or state "including".
- "At least", "at most", "exactly" and "more than half": state each once, and avoid combining them in a way
  that needs reading twice.
- Left and right: state from whose point of view (the reader's, the child's, facing the table).
- Directions on a grid: define up, down, left, right once, and what a "move" is (one cell); avoid diagonals
  unless named.
- Folding and cutting: say where the fold is, whether the sheet is folded in half each time, and where on
  the folded sheet the hole is; avoid asking for a picture of the result.
- Cubes and dice: say what is visible and what is hidden; "opposite faces add to 7" must be stated.
- Ages and years: say whether it is before or after the birthday; time spans use whole numbers of years.
- Clock and calendar: say which day it is today; say whether "next Friday" is meant; avoid month lengths
  and leap-year traps unless that is the point.
- Liars: say exactly how many liars there are, and that everyone else tells the truth; check the puzzle has
  exactly one consistent solution by trying every suspect.
- Ordering and ranking: say whether ties are possible.
- Counting questions: say whether order matters, whether things may repeat, and whether items of the same
  kind are different (are the two red discs the same?).
- Equations with weights or prices: every item of one kind has the same weight; different kinds are not
  assumed different unless stated; give a whole-number answer so there is no rounding.
- Percentages, fractions beyond halves/thirds/quarters and decimals are outside the level.
- Check by solving blind: a puzzle with a second solution, or an option that is also correct by another
  reading, is the usual failure; test every option.
- Avoid names that need a picture ("the figure on the right"), and avoid referring to the position of a
  thing on a page.
- Keep sentences short; one fact per sentence. Grade 3 readers should be able to read the story without a
  dictionary: avoid long words and abstract setting.
