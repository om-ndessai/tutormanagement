# Profile of Math Kangaroo level Benjamin (grades 5-6), id MK56

Source: the ten Benjamin papers 2016-2025 (252 questions, every one sorted by hand). Nothing below quotes or
closely paraphrases a question; examples are described at the level of the idea only.

## 1. Format

- Eight papers (2016-2019 and 2022-2025) are the Austrian paper in English: **24 questions**, in thirds of
  8 questions worth **3, 4 and 5 points**, five options (A)-(E), 60 minutes, a wrong answer costs a quarter of
  the question's points. The 2018 and 2019 papers call the groups "examples" but are the same shape.
- Two papers (2020, 2021) are the **Brazilian** edition of the same Benjamin level: **30 questions**,
  10 each at 3, 4 and 5 points. They show decimal commas ("1,30") and read slightly more translated; the
  mathematics is the same level.
- Sorted in all: 84 questions at 3 points, 84 at 4 and 84 at 5.
- Several questions say "the minimum", "at least", "the largest possible"; others say "which statement is
  always true". Options are numbers (most), letters or names, or small pictures.
- Anything unusual: the questions are short (1-4 sentences). About a third carry a story (kangaroos, animals,
  children, a restaurant, a witch), but the story is only a frame for one idea. A few options are not
  numbers but "cannot be determined" or "it is not possible to be certain" (about 1 in 25).

## 2. Mix

Each of the 252 questions was put in exactly one kind. "Picture" means the question cannot be understood or
solved without the figure (a figure that only decorates a fully stated problem is not counted).

How the kinds sit across the points (3 / 4 / 5): visual-spatial 33 / 16 / 17, so it is by far the most common
3-point kind (39% of the 3-pointers); geometry of area and perimeter 5 / 16 / 9 (a 4-point favourite);
patterns and arrangements 9 / 9 / 16; logic 3 / 5 / 9 (grows with difficulty); word problems and number
puzzles are spread evenly.

**Picture-only kinds, and what survives in words.** 179 of 252 questions (71%) lean on a figure, but many
ideas can be told in words without loss:

- Cube and solid problems: "a block made of unit cubes; how many have exactly 2 painted faces", the largest or
  smallest number of cubes consistent with three described views (as a table of heights), opposite faces of a
  dice, which sum is possible when a die is rolled several times. Survive.
- Folding: a sheet folded in half twice and cut, how many pieces; a strip folded into equal parts and cut at
  marks; a rope marked in 12ths and 16ths and cut at every mark. Survive.
- Mirror and rotation: what time a clock shows in a mirror, digits that read as digits when turned,
  reflections of a word, a hexagon or a card turned by a given number of steps (describe the position of each
  mark by number). Survive with care.
- Rectangle and square areas: a rectangle cut by lines at stated fractions, a square joined at midpoints,
  rectangles side by side with stated areas, perimeter of a staircase or cross shape given as dimensions.
  Survive when every length is stated.
- Magic-sum puzzles: numbers on a ring of circles or on the vertices and sides of a shape, equal sums along
  every line. Survive if the shape is described by listing which positions are on each line.
- Paths and networks: bridges between islands, a colouring of regions or lines that meet, a walk that visits
  every place once. Survive as lists of connections ("A touches B, C").
- Do not survive: choosing among five small drawings, jigsaw-piece fit, nets with printed markings,
  which silhouette cannot be made, tile patterns that must be completed, pure symmetry of drawn figures,
  matching pictures of 3D shapes. These are the roughly 60 questions best left out.

## 3. Maths the level uses

What a strong student in grades 5-6 knows, and no more.

- **Numbers.** Whole numbers to about 10,000 in arithmetic; results up to 7 digits appear only as part of a
  pattern (products of repunits, a long string of digits). A string of 10 digits is cut and the pieces added.
  Years such as 2020-2025 are used as numbers. Addition, subtraction and multiplication by hand; division with
  small divisors; remainders. No negative numbers.
- **Divisibility.** By 2, 3, 5, 9 (digit sum), and combined (18). Multiples, factors, a common multiple of two
  small numbers (12 and 16), even/odd.
- **Fractions.** Halves, thirds, quarters, fifths, eighths, as parts of a whole, of a figure or of a distance;
  1/4 turn, 1/6 turn. Adding unlike fractions is very rare. "Twice as many", "half as much" and simple ratios
  (3 parts of one to 9 of another) are everywhere. No percent.
- **Decimals and money.** Euro amounts with cents (one decimal or two), prices in order, change-free payment
  with three of five coins. Measures: cm, m, km, g, kg, litres, minutes, hours.
- **Algebra without notation.** Two or three unknowns found by guess-and-check or by reasoning; sums and
  differences of ages; "total of 60 split so that..."; arithmetic sequences (a distance growing by the same
  amount each day); averages (the middle value is the mean of its neighbours); equal-sum conditions. Letters
  stand for digits in a calculation. No equations to solve are written in the question, and no x appears.
- **Geometry facts.** Area of rectangle and square, perimeter, surface of a cube, volume of a box from
  length x width x height, parts of a figure by halving. The area of a square joined at midpoints is half the
  big one. Counting unit squares and cubes. Lines of symmetry, rotation by quarter turns, reflection. Nets of
  a cube. No circle area, no angles in degrees except right angles and a quarter turn, no triangle area
  formula beyond halves of rectangles, no Pythagoras.
- **Time and calendar.** Hours and minutes, digital clocks, 7-day week, months, a day of the week by counting.
- **Combinatorics and logic.** Listing cases systematically, up to about 24 arrangements; choosing 3 of 7;
  arranging digits; pigeonhole in "how many to be sure"; truth-tellers and liars (one or two statements);
  a statement that is "always/never/sometimes" true.
- **Notation.** Plain: digits, +, -, x, =, fractions a/b, powers of ten never. Chips with letters or small
  symbols for digits. "At least", "at most", "exactly", "smallest/largest possible" are the key phrases.

## 4. What makes a 3-, 4- and 5-point question

**3 points: one idea, one or two steps, about a minute.**
- Read a single fact and do a short calculation (a two-stage cutting of a whole, a total of pairs of shoes
  against feet, the legs of animals set against another group of animals).
- Find the one case that works in an easy check (which of five prices can be paid with exactly three coins).
- Count a small set carefully (how many positions of a block, how many posts for a stated spacing).
- A small logic read (who is first in a short ranking; which room holds an animal when exactly one sign is
  true).
- Visual: pick the net, the rotated view or the object that cannot be built.

**4 points: two ideas, or one idea that needs organising; two to three minutes.**
- A sum with a constraint: cut a digit string twice and make the sum smallest; make the largest number by
  deleting digits; the largest score of the smallest scorer when all the scores are different and the others'
  total is given.
- Fraction of a shape through a short chain (a rectangle halved, then a quarter shaded).
- Unknowns that need two equations in disguise: two seating plans of a restaurant, one with chairs short and
  the other with chairs spare; a number of animals after two changes of kind.
- A systematic count: two-digit numbers made with a property; arrangements of digits larger than a given
  number; ways to colour a row of regions so neighbours differ.
- Area and perimeter where one must find a missing length first.
- Visual: three-dimensional reasoning with several faces to track.

**5 points: a hidden structure, a proof-style argument, or a search of cases; three to five minutes.**
- "Smallest possible / largest possible" with a bound to establish and an example to show (the least number of
  moves, the largest number of non-hat wearers at a circular table).
- Truth/lie reasoning with consequences from several statements, or a two-day cycle (a person who lies on
  certain days).
- Numbers placed in a figure so that products or sums match in every triangle or line, found by reasoning about
  which number can sit where.
- Counting with overlap: groups that overlap and must be counted once; bouquets that total a fixed price from
  three prices; ways to split a collection.
- A game with a winning strategy (taking 1 to 5 cards, loser takes the last), found by working back from the end.
- A repeated process of many steps that has a pattern (a tower of 50 re-stacked two at a time).
- A fraction-of-distance race where one must compare speeds (positions of two runners when one finishes).
- Visual: cube buildings from three views, nets matched with colour rules.

## 6. Writer cautions

- **Always state the whole situation.** A problem that said "see the picture" must be rewritten with every
  length, position and count listed; if the list is long the idea is not worth keeping. Do not depend on a
  diagram for directions (left, right, above, behind); say "facing north", number the positions, or give a
  table.
- **One answer.** Check that exactly one of five options is right and that "cannot be determined" is
  used only when it is the truth and the question makes that fair. For "largest possible" and "smallest
  possible", the answer must be both attained by an example and impossible to beat; prove both.
- **Distinct numbers.** If people score "different numbers" or are "all of different ages", say so. Say
  whether zero is allowed and whether a number may start with 0 ("three-digit" means not starting with 0).
- **Cuts, posts and sections.** A rope of 12 sections has 11 cuts; ten posts make nine gaps; count the
  endpoints and say whether the ends of the track need a post. Cuts that coincide (12ths and 16ths share
  marks) are a reason to be exact: say "every mark is cut".
- **Truth and lies.** Say whether a person who lies says only false statements, and whether a statement made by
  a truth-teller refers to the people next to them. If a puzzle allows several consistent worlds, the question
  must ask for what holds in all of them.
- **Ages and time.** Say "today" or "the same day" so that ages differ by whole years; avoid birthdays that
  split a year. Time puzzles: state 12 or 24 hours and which day; leap years are out of scope.
- **Fractions of a figure.** Say what the whole is and that the parts are equal. Do not let "half" be
  ambiguous between half of the length and half of the area.
- **Money.** Use euros or dollars consistently; two-decimal prices must be written 4.10 not 4.1. Keep coins
  to real denominations and say when no change is given.
- **Numbers.** Keep calculations feasible by hand; the number of digits in an option should not give the
  answer away; avoid a key that is at an end or in the middle every time; make wrong options come from real
  slips (off by one, forgetting a step, adding where one multiplies), not random values.
- **Symbols for digits.** Say "different letters stand for different digits" and which leading digits cannot
  be zero. Do not use a character that may not render; letters A, B, C are safest.
- **Games.** State who moves first, the allowed moves, and whether taking the last piece wins or loses.
- **Geometry words.** Say "square", "rectangle", "cube", "unit cube" exactly; for a solid made of cubes, state
  that it is solid with no hidden holes unless intended; for painting, say whether the bottom is painted.
- **Reading load.** Keep each question to four sentences or fewer and avoid rare words: the readers are
  10-12 years old and some are learning English. Stories are a frame only; do not add distractor numbers that
  are not needed unless the point of the problem is to ignore them.
- **Originality.** Change the setting, the numbers and the shape of the question. The ideas above are
  the common ones of the contest; any single problem must be a new instance, not a retelling.
