# Profile of level Kadett (grades 7-8), level id MK78

Source: ten papers, 2016-2025 (`txt/<year>_Kadett.txt`), 300 questions, every one sorted by hand into one kind
(table in section 2). Nothing here quotes or closely paraphrases a question; the kinds are described in general words.

## 1. Format

- 30 questions per paper in every year 2016-2025: questions 1-10 are worth 3 points, 11-20 worth 4, 21-30 worth 5.
  Five options (A)-(E), one correct. Austrian papers give 75 minutes, 30 starting points, and take a quarter of the
  points off a wrong answer.
- 2016-2019 and 2022-2025 are the Austrian papers (German origin, English translation). **2020 is a Brazilian paper
  ("Level C", second application)** and **2021 is the Brazilian "Cadet" paper**; they are the same international
  question pool in somewhat rougher English, with a few Portuguese leftovers (units such as "minutos", decimal
  commas). Their difficulty and kinds fit the others.
- The text extraction drops figures, and in the 2016-2019 papers it also drops many fractions, square roots and
  symbols from the printed options, so a few questions could only be sorted from their wording.
- Options are numbers, short quantities (cm, cm2, euros, minutes, degrees), fractions or percentages, a letter or
  name, or (when figures are involved) a picture. About one question in twelve has options that are names, rooms,
  letters or "this is not possible / cannot be determined".
- Many questions are reused from the international pool, so the same idea (for example "sum of areas does not depend
  on where the point is", "everybody says yes", "three people make a chain of sums") comes back across years in a new
  story.

By year (all kinds) every paper has the same balance within a few questions: geometry of measure runs from 3 to 9 per
paper (more in 2017, 2018 and 2025), solids and symmetry from 1 to 6 (low in 2017 and 2025), and the story, logic and
counting kinds fill the rest.

**Picture dependence.** 139 of the 300 questions (about 46 %) cannot be read without the figure (a drawing of
a composite shape, a grid, a solid, a net, a table or a diagram of circles). Per paper it is 10 to 17 (the Brazilian
2020 paper is lowest). Nearly all of the geometry-of-measure kind and almost all of the solids kind are
picture-based; so are the grid, number-wall and some counting-route questions. Of those 139:

- **Can be posed fully in words (roughly 60-70 of them):** a figure described by its construction (a rectangle made
  of equal rectangles in a stated row-and-column pattern, a square with a stated point as a midpoint, a triangle with
  stated angles, a strip folded in half, two circles in a rectangle touching stated sides); cubes and cuboids with a
  stated count, painting or removal; dice and their opposite faces; grids and number walls given as "a table with these
  cells filled; each pair of neighbours sums to..."; routes on a grid given as "right or down only, from corner to
  corner"; symmetry of letters, digits or words typed in plain text; folding a strip or square at stated places.
- **Cannot survive in words (roughly 70):** matching a flipped, rotated or folded picture to one of five pictures,
  choosing the one view or net that does not belong, drawings with irregular shaded regions, "which road sign",
  decoded matchstick or seven-segment digit shapes, angle diagrams with many lines, anything where the answer options
  are themselves pictures. Writers should simply skip these and use the nearest wordable idea (counts of faces,
  vertices, edges, symmetries, areas by lengths).

## 3. Maths the level uses

What a strong student of grades 7-8 is expected to know. Writers should stay inside this.

**Numbers.** Whole numbers to a few thousand freely; occasionally a year-size number (2000s) as a pretext, solved
by structure, not by large arithmetic. Integers including negatives (sums and differences, a quotient with a
negative divisor, smallest value of an expression). Decimals with one or two places (lengths, mean of jumps,
prices, fractions as decimals). Place value, digit sums, last digit of a sum or product, simple remainders.
Divisibility by 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 9, 10, 11 (rarely 11); factor pairs, prime numbers below 100, multiples, powers of 2
and squares up to about 20^2. Order of operations with brackets. No exponents beyond squares and an occasional
cube; at most a very simple root.

**Fractions, percent, ratio.** Fractions with denominators up to about 12; sums and products of fractions;
fraction of a fraction; percentages of a quantity, percentage increase and decrease, and the reverse base problem
("40 % more", then "by how much must it fall to double the value"); ratios such as 5:3 and mixtures; equivalent forms
(0.6, 60 %, 3/5). Averages (arithmetic mean) and how one new value moves it.

**Algebra.** Unknowns introduced informally, linear equations in one unknown, two simple conditions on two unknowns
(often solvable by trial), sums and differences of unknowns, consecutive integers, "x more than y". Proportional
reasoning (rate x time = distance, work rates). No quadratics, no systems written out, no function notation. Formal
variables appear only in the question's own letters for points or lengths.

**Geometry.** Perimeter and area of rectangles, squares, triangles, parallelograms, trapezia, rhombi, composite figures;
areas by cutting and recombining, halves and fractions of a region; angle sum of a triangle (180 degrees) and
a quadrilateral, exterior angle, isosceles and equilateral triangle angles, angles on a straight line and in
a right angle, vertical angles; circle, semicircle and quarter circle (radius, touching circles, tangency to a side),
rarely a circle area in terms of pi but usually only comparisons or lengths; the Pythagorean theorem and
similar triangles appear only in the hardest items, and only with friendly numbers or hidden behind area-halving
tricks; midpoints; ratios of areas of triangles with the same height. Solid geometry: cube and cuboid, unit cubes,
faces/edges/vertices, surface area, volume of a cuboid, dice (opposite faces sum to 7), nets, tetrahedron, painted
cubes, views from the front, side and top.

**Combinatorics and logic.** Counting by listing or by a product rule; arrangements of 3-5 distinct objects;
paths on small grids; pairs of people (n(n-1)/2); pigeonhole-style worst case; coloring rules; knights and knaves;
simple inequalities from rankings; parity arguments; periodic sequences. No factorials beyond 5!, no binomial
coefficients by name, no probability.

**Notation.** Angles with the degree sign and capital letters (angle ABC); segments by two letters; units cm, cm2,
m, km, h, min; decimals with a point (the Brazilian papers use a comma); percent written `%`; fractions with a
slash or stacked; options in (A)-(E) with a number, so a question needs one numerical (or short) answer.

## 4. What makes a 3-, 4- and 5-point question

**3 points (questions 1-10): one step, or one clean idea; most strong students finish all ten in 10-15 minutes.**
Typical shapes: an evaluation of a small expression involving a bracket quotient or a negative number; the time after
adding hours across midnight; reading a result off a diagram of a few rectangles; a count of integers in a range or of
the ways to arrange two or three objects; "the sum of three different positive whole numbers is small, so which
numbers" (forced); a percentage of a given group; the missing number in a short calculation; a symmetry or fold
question; the fraction of a square grid shaded; an equal-share money question; a single 2-step logical ranking.
Idea-level examples: undo one operation; add two ages of three people to find the third; compute a perimeter
difference from two nested rectangles; count the cubes left after some are removed from a known big cube; choose
the figure with the most axes of symmetry.

**4 points (questions 11-20): two or three steps, or one idea that is not obvious at first.** Typical shapes: a
percentage or fraction chain (fraction of the spectators who are boys, two-stage change); a mean that moves after one
new value; a word equation whose unknown is hidden in two statements (full jar versus partly filled jar); a
non-trivial count (weekly plans with no consecutive days, numbers in a range with only odd digits, wire cut by two
sets of marks); angle chasing through two triangles; the area of a composite figure of squares; a sum rule on a grid
of nine cells; a deduction from five or six true-or-false statements; a speed or clock comparison; a surface-area
question about glued solids; a systematic search with a few cases (three-digit numbers with a property).
Idea-level examples: pick the "difference of two consistent statements" to find a unit; split the shape into
congruent parts; use a "total and difference" pair; work backwards from an end state.

**5 points (questions 21-30): a real insight, several cases, or a long chain that needs organisation.** Typical
shapes: a percentage reversal (reduce by what to double); average speed over unequal parts; two vehicles or runners
meeting repeatedly, including the second meeting; a liars-and-truth-tellers puzzle with a count; worst-case counting
with four or five categories; a cryptarithm or digit puzzle with a unique solution after case analysis; a handshake or
tournament count where the unknown is deduced from a list of totals; a periodic sequence over a very large position
(a position of the form "2000-something"); halving/doubling repeated steps; an area problem where a complicated
figure splits into equal triangles; a maximum or minimum under constraints (as many odd numbers as possible, longest
guaranteed run); counting arrangements with a restriction; a puzzle whose key is an invariant (parity, remainder).
Idea-level examples: find the cycle length and reduce a huge index; notice that an average is preserved by
a swap; see that unknown quantities cancel; test the extreme case and prove nothing larger works.

Rough pattern: the 3-point tier is mostly "read and compute" and symmetry/fold items, the 5-point tier carries most
of the logic, counting and speed work, and geometry of measure is spread through all three tiers.

## 6. Writer cautions

- **Never rely on a picture.** If a diagram is needed, describe the construction exactly: "a rectangle is made of six
  equal rectangles in two rows of three" is fine; "the shaded part shown" is not. State which sides are parallel, which
  points are midpoints, which lengths are given, and which is asked.
- **Say the units.** cm vs cm2 vs cm3; hours vs minutes; euros vs cents. Options must carry the same unit.
- **Make "between" and "from ... to ..." explicit** ("including both ends", "strictly between"). Counting questions
  fail most often on whether the ends count and on fence-post errors (n pieces need n - 1 cuts).
- **"Percent more / less / of" is the commonest ambiguity.** Say which quantity is the base: "35 % of all participants",
  "40 % more than before", "decreased by what percent of the original". Never write "30 % less than" when two
  readings (of the old, of the new) are both plausible.
- **Average and mean**: say "arithmetic mean" or "average of the first n values"; say whether the new value is
  counted in the new list.
- **"Different"**: say whether numbers, people or arrangements must be different. Say whether two arrangements that
  are rotations or reflections of each other are the same. For circular arrangements say whether the seats are
  labelled.
- **Digits and numbers**: a three-digit number cannot start with 0; say whether digits may repeat; say whether
  "number" means positive whole number, integer or any number. Zero is the usual trap (is it positive, even, a
  natural number?); say what you mean or avoid it.
- **Consecutive**: say "consecutive whole numbers" or "consecutive days", and whether the sequence is cyclic.
- **Order and positions**: say "to the left of" or "next to" with a clear direction, and "in front of / behind" in
  a line. "Between" for a line of people does not mean adjacent.
- **Logic puzzles**: make clear who always tells the truth, who always lies, and whether the lying person may say
  something true by accident (a liar's statement is simply false). A statement counted as a lie should be false in
  the whole, so spell out "every one of his statements is false" or "exactly one is true". Check the puzzle has
  exactly one answer by trying every case.
- **Speed and time**: say constant speed; say whether stops are counted; give the start of both travellers and
  whether the meeting is "first" or "second"; clocks that run fast or slow should say per hour or per day and what
  the owner believes.
- **Geometry**: a text has no figure to scale, so give every length that the
  answer depends on. For "the area does not depend on where the point is", give a clear configuration and make
  sure the claim really holds. Name vertices in order (ABCD goes round). Say "inside" and "on the side" for points.
- **Cubes and solids**: say whether hidden faces count, whether the bottom is on the table (and so not visible),
  whether glued faces are painted, and whether "faces of the big cube" include the base. For dice, say a standard
  die has opposite faces summing to 7 if you use that.
- **Grids and tables**: give the position of every given number ("row 1, column 3"), and say whether "adjacent"
  includes diagonals (at this level it means sharing a side).
- **Answers must be unique and checkable**: the four distractors should be plausible wrong results (a common error
  such as forgetting to subtract, using the wrong base, or an off-by-one), distinct from each other, and in the same
  form as the key. Keep the key and distractors in a sensible, mostly ascending order.
- **Keep to the maths of the level** (section 3): no quadratics, no trigonometry, no probability, no heavy arithmetic.
  A question may look large (a year, 2000 people) but must be solved by structure, not by long calculation.
- **Avoid near-copies of well-known contest ideas**: use new stories, new numbers and a fresh combination of two
  ideas; do not reuse the exact setting of a past paper question.
