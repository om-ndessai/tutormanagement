# Profile: Math Kangaroo, level Student (grades 11-12), MK1112

Based on the ten papers 2016-2025 (300 questions, every one sorted by hand). All description below is in
general words; no question is quoted or closely paraphrased.

## 1. Format

- 30 questions per paper in every year 2016-2025 (not 24): questions 1-10 are worth 3 points, 11-20
  worth 4 points, 21-30 worth 5 points. Five options (A)-(E) each; 75 minutes; 30 starting points, a
  wrong answer costs a quarter of the question's points (so the maximum is 150 and guessing is mildly punished).
- Nominal grades: 11 and up (Austrian "Schulstufe 11-13"; the 2017 paper says grades 11-13). The 2020 and
  2021 papers are the Brazilian edition of the same level (same shape, 30 questions); the other eight are the
  Austrian papers. The Brazilian ones lean a little more on number theory (2020) and on functions (2021).
- Options are usually five numbers, five expressions, or five short statements. Five or six questions a year have
  a last option like "another value", "cannot be determined" or "no such number"; these are often the right
  answer when the question looks like it should have a unique value.
- Mathematical notation in the papers is typeset (powers, roots, factorials, fractions, absolute value, interval
  brackets, sums); in the extracted text many symbols are missing, so numbers and signs in a few questions
  could only be guessed from context.
- About 40 percent of the questions depend on a figure (see section 2).

  number theory, algebra, logic and probability rarely do.
- Ideas that survive in words (fully posable without a figure): a shaded area that is "a square with a quarter
  circle cut out" can be stated; two rectangles or squares sharing a corner with given sides; a right triangle
  with its angle bisectors, incircle or altitude; a triangle with named points on its sides and given ratios; a
  rectangle folded so two vertices meet; a reflected ray or a shortest route "via a point of a line"; points and
  midpoints with coordinates stated; a circle with chords or tangents at given arc lengths; a cuboid
  painted and cut into unit cubes; a sequence of dot or tile patterns described by its rule; a table or a row of
  cells with a rule on sums; a chain of statements on a circular table (liars). Graph-reading questions
  (pick the picture of a parabola, line or function) can be posed as "which statement is true about the graph of
  y = ..." with the facts in words. Net and block-fitting questions, "which piece fits", calendar-ink and
  tile-pattern questions, and "which of these five pictures" questions cannot.

## 3. Maths the level uses

What a strong student in grades 11-12 knows. The paper assumes no calculus and no trigonometry beyond what a
good Year 10 geometry student could manage; it uses school-level algebra heavily.

- **Numbers.** Integers up to about 10 digits, with products and sums that need structure rather than a
  calculator (a number such as one less than a perfect square; the current year, 2023, 2024, 2025, is often
  the number used). Powers of 2, 3, 5, 7 with exponents up to ~100 (and 2017-2025 as exponents in
  "highest power dividing" or "largest common divisor" questions). Factorials up to 15!. Perfect squares and cubes.
  Fractions, decimals, percent, ratios. Exact surds (square roots of 2, 3, 5, 8, ...) in lengths and areas; pi in
  areas of circles and in arc lengths. Floor and rounding appear only occasionally.
- **Number theory.** Divisibility rules (2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 9, 11, 18), digit sums, last digits and remainders by
  cycles, prime factorisation, number and sum of divisors by the exponent formula, product of divisors, gcd and lcm,
  Euclid-style "factor out the common power", parity and remainders mod 3, 4, 5, 7, 9, 10, "n(n+1)/2" sums,
  consecutive-product divisibility (n(n+1)(n+2) is a multiple of 6), difference of squares.
- **Algebra.** Linear and quadratic equations; Vieta's formulas (sum and product of roots) and the discriminant;
  absolute value equations with two or three layers; exponential equations of the type "base^exponent = 1";
  systems of two or three equations; word problems turning into one equation; polynomial division by x - a,
  integer roots, a point common to a family of graphs; inequalities and comparing expressions (arithmetic-geometric
  means, positive numbers below 1); logarithm-free exponent manipulation (rewrite everything with the same
  base); recursive sequences (a term from the previous one, or from index halving); functional equations of the
  type f(x+y) = f(x)f(y), or f symmetric about a line; arithmetic and geometric series; telescoping sums.
- **Functions and graphs.** Linear and quadratic functions: slope, intercepts, vertex, sign of a, b, c from a graph, number
  of intersection points; transformations (reflect, shift, x to 1/x); maximum of a quadratic on an interval;
  the xy-plane with coordinates, distance, midpoint, area of a triangle from coordinates.
- **Plane geometry.** Angle sums, isosceles and equilateral triangles, Pythagoras (with ratio tricks like 3-4-5, 5-12-13),
  similar triangles and ratios of areas, area of triangle (half base times height, and by coordinates), circle area
  and circumference, arc length and sector area, tangent-chord and inscribed-angle facts, tangent lengths from a point,
  incircle (area = half perimeter times radius), regular hexagon (six equilateral triangles) and octagon,
  angle bisectors, midpoint line, a rectangle's diagonal, reflection tricks for shortest routes, lattice points on a circle.
- **Solid geometry.** Volume and surface of cuboid, cube, cylinder, cone and sphere (rarely), pyramid and tetrahedron
  (volume = third of base times height; cutting corners off a tetrahedron), polyhedron counting with Euler-type
  face-edge counts, nets, parallel sections, the shortest path on a surface by unfolding.
- **Counting and probability.** Product rule, permutations and combinations (binomial coefficients up to ~15 choose
  something), inclusion and exclusion, complements ("at least one"), expected equal-probability outcomes with dice,
  coins, balls; conditional probability by a tree of 2 to 3 steps; pigeonhole ("how many draws to be certain");
  Markov-style games (race to a lead of 2 or 3 points) solved by a simple recurrence.
- **Notation.** Ordinary school notation: factorial n!, absolute value |x|, intervals [a, b] and ]a, b[, sets
  with union, the sum sign sparingly, f(x), a_n for sequences, "mod" almost never (the questions say "remainder"
  or "divisible"). Angles are in degrees; no radians.
- **Not used:** calculus, trigonometric identities (beyond perhaps an exact 30-60-90 or 45-45-90 triangle), complex
  numbers, matrices, vectors, logarithms (very rare), induction written as a proof.

## 4. What makes a 3-, 4- and 5-point question

**3 points (questions 1-10): one idea, one or two steps, a single clean fact.** Typical forms:
- Read a rule and apply it once: scale a model to its real height; undo a percent change; a single trick on the last digit of a long product.
- A direct count with a boundary (how many whole numbers lie between two nearly equal products, where the middle one has to be spotted by "square minus one").
- A fraction that simplifies by factoring a common power out of numerator and denominator.
- A simple deduction: a hat-wearing chain of implications; a row of seats; an ordering from three comparisons.
- A shaded area or an angle by one decomposition (a square with a circle in it; sector area as a fraction of the circle).
- An extremal count in a small situation (the most sixes that can sum to a given total; a minimum of flips for a given number of coins; the number of planes needed to bound a region).
- A first-order function fact: which quadrant a line does not enter; the sign of an expression for a given graph.
- One-step probability: which box makes a blue ball most likely; a die with weighted faces.

**4 points (questions 11-20): two ideas combined, or a standard trick that must be seen.** Typical forms:
- A number-theory fact plus a counting step: how many divisors of a factorial are odd; three-digit numbers whose reversal differs by a fixed amount; how the gcd of two big sums collapses by factoring a power.
- A quadratic or system whose roots or sum of roots are asked without solving; two consecutive numbers whose digit sums both satisfy a divisibility condition, asking for the smallest length.
- A proportional-reasoning problem (equal volumes with different radii, candles burning at different rates, an average given three ways).
- A two-stage probability or a tournament/seating probability; at least one digit of a set; domino or colouring bound (a minimum number of colours, a maximum number of dominoes).
- A geometric configuration needing two facts (similar triangles plus Pythagoras; areas of overlapping squares; tangents plus an inscribed angle); a minimum path by reflecting.
- A truth-teller puzzle on a round table; a "which statements can be true" puzzle with a small case split.
- A functional or sequence puzzle where the first few terms reveal a pattern and one has to see why it continues.

**5 points (questions 21-30): a hidden structure, a classical lemma, a long chain, or a sharp extremal argument.** Typical forms:
- Highest power of a prime dividing a combined expression of powers; the number of divisors of one number that do not divide another; the greatest common divisor of all values of a polynomial expression in n (look at n = 1, 2 and prove the rest).
- A functional equation or recursion solved by guessing a form and checking, with a sum of many terms afterwards.
- A real-solutions count for an equation with a variable exponent or nested absolute values; a parameter for which an equation has exactly two solutions; a minimum over a parameter of the maximum of a quadratic on an interval.
- A geometry problem where a clever auxiliary line or an area ratio does everything (a triangle cut by two lines with three areas known; a regular hexagon with areas of triangles given; arcs of circles around a regular polygon; a rectangle with circles meeting it in segments of known lengths).
- Invariants and extremal arguments: parity or colouring shows an impossibility; a smallest number of moves needs both a construction and a bound; the largest number of truth-tellers around a table; the smallest number of draws to be sure of a conclusion; a table in which every block of a fixed size sums to zero, and whole-table sum asked.
- A game or probability with a recursive structure (a race to a lead), or a combinatorial count with a bijection or complement.
- A puzzle about a sequence of digits or a factorial with missing digits, solved by divisibility by 9 and 11.

Difficulty is mostly about seeing the trick, not about heavy computation: nothing at 5 points needs more than a few
lines of arithmetic once the idea is found. Answer options at 5 points often include "another value" or "cannot be determined".

## 6. Writer cautions

- **Never rely on a picture.** Say exactly which figure it is: "a square of side s with a quarter circle centred at one vertex and passing through the two adjacent vertices". If you cannot say it in two sentences, change the problem. Do not write "as shown".
- **Name every point and say where it lies.** "A point X on side CD" is not enough if its position matters: give a rule ("X is the point on CD equidistant from A and C").
- **Say what is asked in the same unit as given.** Areas in square units, lengths in units, angles in degrees; do not mix degrees with radians. Say "exact value" or give the options as exact expressions if surds or pi are involved.
- **Whole numbers vs. real numbers.** State "positive whole numbers", "integers" or "real numbers" explicitly in equation and counting problems; contest answers hinge on it (an extra root, a zero, a negative).
- **"Between" and "up to":** say whether the ends are included. "How many whole numbers are strictly between ..." Avoid ordinary-language "from 1 to 10" without saying inclusive.
- **Digits and leading zeros.** "Three-digit number" excludes a leading zero; say so when it matters. Say "the digits of a number are all different" or "digits may repeat".
- **Counting problems:** state whether order matters, whether objects are identical, and whether rotations or reflections count as the same. Avoid "different ways" without a definition.
- **Liars and knights:** state that every person is either one or the other, who knows what, and what each says exactly. Avoid statements that can be read ambiguously ("both neighbours are liars" vs "at least one"). For round tables, say whether neighbours are on both sides.
- **Probability:** say the draw is at random and each outcome is equally likely; say with or without replacement; say fair die or biased. Give the answer as a fraction in the options, in lowest terms.
- **Unique answers.** Check that exactly one option is correct and that no distractor is correct under another reading. Do not use "none of the above" as the key unless the question was designed for it; "cannot be determined" is correct only after you have proved at least two cases give different answers.
- **Extremal questions (smallest, largest):** the stem must say the extreme is over a stated set, and the answer must be both attained and bounded. Check both parts before including the question.
- **Games:** state who moves first, whether players play perfectly, and what "wins" means; the answer should be "the first player" type with a one-line strategy, not a long case analysis.
- **Do not copy contest wording or storyline.** The contest likes animals (kangaroos, beavers), school settings and the current year as a number; use fresh stories, and vary the numbers so no question is a near copy. The year numbers 2016-2025 appear in many contest questions; avoid them as the key numbers.
- **Calculator-free.** Every number should be handled by structure (factorise, pair terms, complete the square). Avoid answers that need a decimal computation; sums and products should simplify by a visible trick.
- **Stay at level.** No calculus, trigonometric identities, logarithms or complex numbers. Keep stems under about 60 words; at 5 points the idea may be hard, but the statement should be short.
- **Options.** Five distinct options, sorted increasing for numbers; distractors should be the natural wrong answers (off by one, forgotten case, wrong sign, half or double) and not obviously silly.
